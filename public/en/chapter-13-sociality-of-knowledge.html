Chapter 13: A Formal System for the Sociality of Knowledge

Chapter Introduction

I. The Place of This Chapter in the Book

This chapter is the second chapter of Part IV, "The Formalization of the New View of Cognition," of Volume II, and it undertakes the task of providing a rigorous formalization foundation for the sociality of knowledge. If Chapter 12 formalized the participatory and embodied features of cognition, this chapter focuses on the social dimension of cognition—knowledge is not discovered by individuals in isolation, but is jointly constructed by epistemic communities through negotiation and critical dialogue.

In the protocol stack architecture, this chapter mainly involves:

  • The L₁ layer (life-cognition foundation): the social-ontological features of knowledge
  • The L₂ layer (normative-ethical foundation): the normative structure of epistemic communities
  • The formalization layer: providing a rigorous mathematical reconstruction of the philosophical arguments of Volume I, Chapter 3

The core task of this chapter is to construct the formal system SK (Social Knowledge), which formally expresses the core thesis NK-3 of the New View of Cognition:

NK-3 (the Sociality of Knowledge): Knowledge is not the private property of individual minds, but the intersubjective consensus reached by epistemic communities through negotiation, criticism, and revision. The justification of knowledge depends on social practice, and the authority of knowledge comes from the recognition of the community.

II. The Core Innovations of the Revised Edition

Relative to the preliminary edition (which did not include this volume), the core innovations of this chapter include:

Innovation One: A Complete Continuity from Philosophy to Formalization

The discussion of the sociality of knowledge in Volume I, Chapter 3, §3.3 is mainly conceptual and argumentative, citing the literature of social epistemology (Goldman, Kitcher, Longino), but it lacks rigorous formalization. By constructing the formal system SK, this chapter transforms these philosophical insights into operable mathematical structures.

Innovation Two: A Topological Characterization of Epistemic Communities

This chapter innovatively uses topology and graph theory to characterize the structure of epistemic communities. We model an epistemic community as an epistemic network, in which nodes are epistemic subjects and edges are epistemic relations (trust, authority, criticism, etc.). This formalization reveals the deep mathematical structure of the sociality of knowledge.

Innovation Three: Dynamic Modeling of the Negotiation Process

Knowledge is not static, but dynamically evolves through a continuous negotiation process. This chapter uses Dynamic Epistemic Logic (DEL) to model the negotiation process, including:

  • Information sharing (public announcement)
  • Private communication
  • Belief revision
  • Consensus formation

Innovation Four: The Social Dimension of Justification

Traditional epistemology focuses on individual justification, whereas this chapter formalizes social justification: a belief is justified in an epistemic community if and only if it passes the critical review procedure of the community. We prove several non-trivial properties of social justification.

Innovation Five: Direct Interface with AI Governance

The formalization of this chapter is not only a theoretical tool but also a practical tool. We show how SK can be applied to:

  • Auditing the knowledge sources of AI systems
  • Consensus protocols for multi-agent systems
  • Modeling knowledge production in scientific communities
  • Formal analysis of democratic deliberation

§13.1 Construction of the Formal System SK

§13.1.1 Motivation and Goals

Philosophical Motivation

Traditional epistemology (especially the Cartesian tradition) regards knowledge as the private property of individual minds. A belief is justified if and only if the individual possesses sufficient evidence or reasons. This individualist epistemology faces many difficulties:

1. The problem of specialization: Modern knowledge is highly specialized, and no individual can independently verify all knowledge. We rely on the testimony of experts.

2. The problem of the social division of labor: The production of scientific knowledge depends on a complex social division of labor (experimentalists, theorists, reviewers, funders, etc.).

3. The problem of epistemic injustice: The knowledge claims of certain groups are systematically ignored or disparaged (Miranda Fricker's theory of epistemic injustice).

4. The problem of collective knowledge: Some knowledge can only be possessed by a collective rather than by individuals (e.g., "the scientific community knows that the Higgs boson exists").

Social epistemology attempts to overcome these difficulties by maintaining that knowledge is essentially social. But most of the work in social epistemology remains at the level of conceptual analysis and case studies, lacking rigorous formalization.

Formalization Goals

The goal of this chapter is to construct the formal system SK so that it can:

1. Precisely characterize epistemic communities: Transform "epistemic community" from a vague concept into a rigorous mathematical object.

2. Model the negotiation process: Formalize the process by which knowledge evolves through negotiation, criticism, and revision.

3. Define social justification: Give a rigorous definition of "a belief is justified in a community."

4. Prove non-trivial theorems: Reveal the deep structure of the sociality of knowledge.

5. Support practical applications: Provide operable tools for AI governance, science policy, and democratic deliberation.

III. New Insights from the Formalization

The process of formalization itself has produced three important new insights:

Insight 1: The topological structure of the sociality of knowledge

Knowledge is not only social, but also has a specific topological structure. The geometric properties of epistemic networks (such as small-worldness and scale-freeness) profoundly affect the production and dissemination of knowledge. This insight was not sufficiently recognized in Volume I.

Insight 2: The convergence conditions of negotiation

Negotiation does not always converge to consensus. Convergence requires specific conditions (rationality, honesty, a common prior, public evidence). When these conditions are not satisfied, negotiation may lead to polarization rather than consensus. This explains many epistemic conflicts in reality.

Insight 3: The quantifiability of epistemic injustice

Epistemic injustice is not only a qualitative concept, but can be quantified (the epistemic injustice index EI). This enables us to systematically detect and correct injustice rather than relying only on case-by-case analysis.
