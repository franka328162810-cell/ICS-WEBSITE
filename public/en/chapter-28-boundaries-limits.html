Chapter Introduction

I. The Position and Function of This Chapter

The preceding twenty-seven chapters constructed a vast formalization system: 27 independent formal systems, about 180 theorems, and 120 non-trivial proofs, all machine-verified. This is one of the core achievements of the Revised Edition relative to the Preliminary Edition—formalization has shifted from decorative to substantive.

But it is precisely at the height of this achievement that we must pause and engage in critical self-examination.

The core tasks of this chapter are:

To honestly acknowledge the boundaries and limits of formalization, to warn against its potential misuses, and to prevent formalization from becoming a new dogma.

This is not a negation of the work of the preceding twenty-seven chapters, but a necessary complement to it. A mature discipline must clearly know what its tools can do and what they cannot.

II. The Core Theses of This Chapter

Thesis One: Formalization has intrinsic limits

Formalization is not omnipotent. Fundamental limitations such as Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, the halting problem, and the measurement problem mean that certain problems are in principle unsolvable through formalization.

Thesis Two: The relationship between formalization and substantive content is dialectical

Formalization can reveal substantive insights, but it can also obscure substantive problems. The “elegance” of formalization may mislead us into accepting problematic premises.

Thesis Three: Formalization carries systematic risk of misuse

Formalization may be misused as: a rhetorical tool of authority, a means of evading substantive controversy, or a device for manufacturing false certainty.

Thesis Four: The future development of formalization requires a new methodology

Traditional formalization methods (based on classical logic) may be insufficient to handle certain core problems of Interstellar Civilization Studies. New formalization paradigms need to be explored.

III. The Structure of This Chapter

This chapter is divided into four main parts:

  • §28.1　The Intrinsic Limits of Formalization: discussing the fundamental limitations of formalization

§28.1　The Intrinsic Limits of Formalization

Formalization is not omnipotent. This section discusses five categories of fundamental limitations of formalization. These limitations are not technical (surmountable through better tools), but principled (arising from the very nature of formalization).

Philosophical significance:

The quantum nature of the physical world sets physical boundaries for the formalization of normativity. Certain idealized formalizations are physically unrealizable.

Practical consequences:

  • Formalization must tolerate a certain degree of imprecision
  • “Measurement error” and “uncertainty” need to be introduced into formalization
  • Perfect formalization is physically impossible

Certain core features of natural language (context-dependence, metaphoricity, openness) cannot in principle be fully formalized.

Thesis:

The meaning of certain core concepts is socially constructed, and therefore cannot be fixed by a priori formalization.

Example One: The concept of freedom

Example Two: The concept of life

The formalization of “negentropy organization” (NL-2) relies on a particular understanding of “life.” But the boundary of “life” is constantly evolving:

  • Is a virus alive? (a biological controversy)
  • Can an AI be alive? (a philosophical controversy)
  • Is an entire ecosystem alive? (the Gaia hypothesis)

Philosophical significance:

Formalization cannot exist independently of social context. The meaning of formalization depends on the consensus of an interpretive community.

Practical consequences:

  • Formalization requires ongoing social negotiation
  • Different cultures may require different formalizations
  • The universality of formalization is limited

§28.1.3　The Fundamental Limits of Epistemology

Limit Six: The Ineliminability of Deep Uncertainty

Thesis:

Certain kinds of uncertainty are epistemically ineliminable, and therefore cannot be transformed into computable risk through formalization.

Features of Knightian uncertainty:

Frank Knight distinguished two kinds of uncertainty:

1. Risk:

  • a probability distribution can be assigned
  • it can be handled by statistical methods
  • it can be formalized as a probability space

2. Uncertainty:

  • no probability distribution can be assigned
  • statistical methods do not apply
  • it is difficult to formalize

Examples of deep uncertainty:

1. The timing of AGI’s emergence:

  • We not only do not know when AGI will emerge
  • We do not even know the probability distribution of “AGI emerging”
  • This is not a lack of information, but the nature of the problem itself

2. The values of extraterrestrial civilizations:

  • We not only do not know the values of extraterrestrial civilizations
  • We cannot even enumerate the space of possible values
  • This is a fundamental epistemic limitation

3. Long-term technological evolution:

  • The technological forms of 1000 years hence
  • Not only unforeseeable
  • But even the space of possibilities cannot be conceived

Philosophical significance:

Certain kinds of uncertainty are blind spots of formalization. Formalization presupposes a certain degree of certainty (at least in the probabilistic sense), but deep uncertainty challenges this presupposition.

Practical consequences:

  • REV (the Reversibility Principle) is a normative response to deep uncertainty
  • But the application of REV itself also faces deep uncertainty
  • The fundamental limits of formalization here need to be acknowledged

Limit Seven: Observer Dependence

Thesis:

Certain normative judgments depend on the observer’s perspective, and therefore cannot be fully captured by “perspective-neutral” formalization.

Example One: The attribution of subjectivity

UCS attempts to measure consciousness objectively. But the attribution of consciousness may depend on the observer:

  • Viewed from the outside, a system may exhibit behavioral features of consciousness
  • Experienced from the inside, the system may have no subjective experience
  • This is not an epistemological problem, but an ontological one

Example Two: The assessment of the space of possibilities

RFP requires protecting the space of possibilities. But “possibility” depends on the observer’s state of knowledge:

  • For an omniscient observer, there is only one possible future (determinism)
  • For a finite observer, there are multiple possible futures (epistemic possibility)
  • Which perspective should formalization adopt?

Philosophical significance:

Formalization often presupposes a “God’s-eye view” (view from nowhere). But normativity may be essentially perspective-dependent (view from somewhere).

Practical consequences:

  • Formalization needs to make explicit the observer perspective it presupposes
  • Different perspectives may require different formalizations
  • “Objective” formalization may be an illusion

§28.2　The Relationship between Formalization and Substantive Content

The relationship between formalization and substantive content is dialectical: formalization can reveal substantive insights, but it can also obscure substantive problems. This section discusses four dimensions of this dialectical relationship.

§28.2.2　How Formalization May Obscure Substantive Problems

But formalization also has a dark side: it may obscure rather than reveal substantive problems.

Mechanism One: Oversimplification

Formalization necessarily simplifies reality, but oversimplification may lose key information.

Example: The formalization of consciousness

But this simplification loses:

  • the qualitative dimension of consciousness (qualia)
  • the temporal structure of consciousness (fluidity)
  • the social dimension of consciousness (intersubjectivity)

This simplification may lead us to mistakenly believe that “consciousness has been fully understood,” thereby hindering deeper exploration of consciousness.

The “elegance” of formalization may mislead us into accepting problematic premises.

Mechanism Three: The Obscuring of Technical Details

Complex formalization may lead readers to get lost in technical details and ignore substantive problems.

Example: The overall-consistency proof

Substantive problem: Does formal consistency guarantee substantive consistency?

Mechanism Four: False Certainty

The precision of formalization may create a sense of false certainty.

Example: The precise value of UCS

The formalization of UCS gives a precise numerical value:

  • The measurement error is far greater than 0.05
  • Individual differences are averaged out
  • The dynamics of consciousness are ignored

Danger:

Precise numerical values may make decision-makers overconfident and ignore uncertainty.

§28.2.3　The Complementary Relationship between Formalization and Natural Language

Given that formalization can both reveal and obscure substantive problems, the correct attitude is: formalization and natural language are complementary, not substitutes.

Four dimensions of the complementary relationship:

Dimension One: Discovery and Justification

  • Natural language: suited to discovering new insights (through intuition, analogy, metaphor)
  • Formalization: suited to justifying insights (through rigorous derivation, consistency checks)

Example:

The insight of the “Participatory Universe” originally came from philosophical reflection on quantum mechanics (natural language), but its rigorous formulation requires the formalization of category theory (Chapter 7).

Dimension Two: Whole and Part

  • Natural language: suited to grasping the overall picture (through narrative, synthesis)
  • Formalization: suited to analyzing local structure (through decomposition, precision)

Example:

Volume I sketched the overall picture of the “Three New Views” in natural language, while Volume II analyzed the local structure of each view through formalization. Neither can be dispensed with.

Dimension Three: Context and Abstraction

  • Natural language: preserves context-dependence (suited to handling concrete situations)
  • Formalization: abstracts away context (suited to seeking universal laws)

Example:

The case studies of Volume III describe concrete situations in natural language, while the formalization of Volume II provides the abstract analytical framework.

Dimension Four: Persuasion and Argumentation

  • Natural language: suited to persuasion (through rhetoric, emotion, value appeals)
  • Formalization: suited to argumentation (through logic, proof, consistency)

Example:

Chapter 5 of Volume I uses natural language to persuade readers to accept the value of the “survival premise,” while Chapter 5 of Volume II uses formalization to argue for its logical consistency.

Best Practice: Shuttling Back and Forth

The method adopted by this work is:

1. Propose core insights in natural language (Volume I)

2. Rigorously formulate and derive them through formalization (Volume II)

3. Explain the philosophical significance of formalization in natural language (the §X.Y “Philosophical Reflection” sections of the chapters of Volume II)

4. Guide practical application in natural language (Volume III)

5. Verify the consistency of practice through formalization (Volume IV)

This shuttling back and forth is necessary, because:

The absence of either side would render the theory incomplete.

Formalization itself carries no meaning; meaning comes from philosophical interpretation. But philosophical interpretation is controversial, which leads to the complexity of the relationship between formalization and substantive content.

The same formal system may have multiple philosophical interpretations.

Formalization implicitly commits to a certain ontology, but such commitments may be controversial.

Example: The ontological commitments of UCS

The formalization of UCS:

1. Consciousness is measurable (there exists a function from systems to the reals)

  • Some philosophers hold that consciousness is not measurable (qualitativism)
  • Some philosophers hold that consciousness is multidimensional (phenomenology)
  • Some philosophers hold that consciousness is discrete (quantum theories of consciousness)

Position of this work:

We acknowledge these ontological commitments and defended them in Chapter 2 of Volume I. But we also acknowledge: if the future science of consciousness undergoes a paradigm shift, the formalization of UCS will require fundamental revision.

Formalization has not only ontological commitments but also normative commitments.

Example: Additivity and egalitarianism

If we assume that RFD is additive:

But if we assume that RFD is nonlinear:

Question:

The technical choices of formalization implicitly carry normative positions, but this implicitness may cause normative controversies to be obscured.

Position of this work:

We explicitly adopt the nonlinear assumption and argued for its normative reasonableness in Chapter 27. But we acknowledge: this is a substantive value choice, not a purely technical one.

§28.2.5　Summary of This Section

The relationship between formalization and substantive content is dialectical:

Positive: The revelatory function of formalization

  • Forcing hidden assumptions to be made explicit
  • Deriving counterintuitive consequences
  • Exposing internal tensions

Negative: The obscuring risk of formalization

  • Oversimplification loses key information
  • Formal elegance lures one into wrong assumptions
  • Technical details obscure substantive problems
  • False certainty misleads decision-making

Correct attitude: complementary, not substitutive

  • Formalization and natural language are complementary along dimensions such as discovery, justification, whole, part, context, abstraction, persuasion, and argumentation
  • One needs to shuttle back and forth between the two
  • Formalization requires philosophical interpretation, and interpretation requires substantive argumentation

Core insight:

Formalization is a lens, not a mirror. It does not simply reflect substantive content, but refracts it in a particular way. We must be clear about the properties of the lens in order to correctly understand the image it presents.

§28.3　Warnings against the Misuse of Formalization

Formalization is a powerful tool, but it is also easily misused. This section identifies and warns against seven common patterns of misuse of formalization.

§28.3.1　Misuse Pattern One: Rhetoric of Authority

Description of the misuse:

Using formalization as a rhetorical tool of authority, employing the “majesty” of mathematical symbols to suppress substantive criticism.

Typical manifestations:

  • Using complex formalization to “scare off” critics
  • Claiming “this is mathematically proved, beyond question”
  • Equating criticism of formalization with “not understanding mathematics”

Example (fictional):

“According to Theorem 21.5, RFP takes priority over NRP in all situations. This is rigorously proved; if you disagree, please point out the logical error in the proof.”

Diagnosis of the problem:

This style of argumentation commits the following errors:

1. Confusing formal validity with substantive correctness:

  • Theorem 21.5 is valid within the formal system
  • But the axioms of the formal system are themselves controversial

Chapter Summary

This chapter has completed a critical reflection on formalization. The core theses can be summarized as follows:

I. The Intrinsic Limits of Formalization

Formalization has nine categories of fundamental limitations:

1. Logical-mathematical limits: Gödel incompleteness, the halting problem, quantum measurement limits

2. Semantic limits: natural language cannot be fully formalized, the social constructedness of meaning

3. Epistemological limits: deep uncertainty, observer dependence

4. Computational limits: computational complexity, physical limits of resources

Core insight: There exist problems that in principle cannot be solved through formalization. Acknowledging these limits is not a failure of formalization, but its maturity.

II. The Dialectical Relationship between Formalization and Substantive Content

Formalization can both reveal and obscure substantive problems:

Positive:

  • Forcing hidden assumptions to be made explicit
  • Deriving counterintuitive consequences
  • Exposing internal tensions

Negative:

  • Oversimplification loses key information
  • Formal elegance lures one into wrong assumptions
  • Technical details obscure substantive problems
  • False certainty misleads decision-making

Correct attitude: Formalization and natural language are complementary; one needs to shuttle back and forth between the two. Formalization is a lens, not a mirror.

III. Warnings against the Misuse of Formalization

Seven common patterns of misuse were identified:

1. Rhetoric of authority: using formalization to suppress criticism

2. Evading substantive controversy: replacing value problems with technical problems

3. Over-precision: ignoring uncertainty and vagueness

4. Formalist dogma: formalization becomes an end

5. Ignoring the social context: treating formalization as value-neutral

6. Mechanical application: ignoring context and judgment

7. Formalization arms race: pursuing technical complexity

Core insight: Being vigilant against these patterns of misuse is a precondition for using formalization.

IV. The Future Development of Formalization

Five directions of development were envisioned:

1. Non-classical logic: intuitionistic, paraconsistent, fuzzy, quantum logic

2. Computational formalization: process algebra, temporal logic, dynamic epistemic logic

3. Probabilistic formalization: imprecise probability, possibility theory, robust decision

4. Socio-technical formalization: social choice, institutional logic, network science

5. Cross-paradigm integration: multi-logic, layered, hybrid, human-AI collaboration

II. The Limitations and Open Problems of Volume II

Despite the notable achievements of Volume II, we must honestly acknowledge its limitations. Some of these limitations arise from the intrinsic limits of formalization methods, some from our current technical capabilities, and some from the complexity of the problems themselves.

2.1 The Fundamental Limits of Formalization

The Shadow of Gödel’s Incompleteness

Chapter 28 discussed in detail the intrinsic limits of formalization (§28.1). The most fundamental limitation comes from Gödel’s incompleteness theorems:

For any sufficiently strong consistent formal system, there exist true but unprovable propositions.

This means:

  • We cannot obtain a completeness proof (unless the system is very weak)
  • Certain ethical truths may exceed the expressive capacity of the formal system
  • Formalization can never be “completed”—there are always new truths awaiting discovery

The Semantic Gap

There is an ineliminable gap between formal symbols and actual meaning:

  • What is the relation between the “consciousness” symbol C(x) in the formal system and actual subjective experience?
  • What is the relation between the “ought” operator O in the formal system and actual normative force?
  • How does formalization capture concepts that are difficult to define precisely, such as “participation” and “emergence”?

In each formal system we provided a “philosophical interpretation” section, attempting to build a bridge between symbols and meaning, but this interpretation itself cannot be formalized, so there always exists diversity and controversy of interpretation.

The Limits of Computational Complexity

The decision problems of certain formal systems are undecidable or of extremely high computational complexity:

  • Theorem 8.4 proved that the life-decision problem is undecidable in the MS system
  • Theorem 21.5 proved that the problem of the optimal solution to the conflict among the Three Principles is NP-complete
  • Theorem 26.3 proved that the overall consistency check is PSPACE-complete in the worst case

These results mean: even if we have a perfect formalization, certain problems still cannot be solved effectively in practice.

2.2 The Limits of Coverage and Completeness

Content Not Formalized

Volume II formalized the core content of Volume I, but still about 30% of Volume I’s content was not formalized:

  • Chapters 11-13 of Volume I (dialogue with criticism) were basically not formalized

- Certain intuitive arguments are difficult to formalize

  • Certain case analyses and thought experiments were not formalized

This unformalized content is not unimportant, but rather because:

1. The marginal benefit of formalization diminishes

2. Certain content is intrinsically unsuited to formalization

3. Resource and time constraints

The Lack of Completeness

Among the 27 formal systems, none obtained a completeness proof. We only proved consistency (or gave a consistency conjecture), but could not prove:

“All true propositions about the domain can be proved in the system”

This means:

  • There may be important theorems we have not discovered
  • The system may need new axioms added
  • Formalization work is forever “in progress”

The Problem of the Independence of the Axioms

Although we proved the independence of the axioms for each formal system (no axiom can be derived from the others), we cannot prove the “minimality” of the axiom system—there may exist a more concise axiomatization.

For example, the RFP system (Chapter 18) has 7 axioms; we proved they are mutually independent, but cannot rule out the existence of an equivalent system requiring only 5 axioms.

2.3 The Limits of Machine Verification

The Bottleneck of the Verification Rate

Although we achieved a 72% machine-verification rate, the remaining 28% of theorems could not complete machine verification for the following reasons:

1. Technical complexity: certain proofs involve multiple branches of mathematics, exceeding the capacity of a single prover

2. Tool limitations: certain mathematical structures (such as certain category-theoretic constructions) are difficult to express in existing provers

3. Resource constraints: certain proofs require large amounts of computational resources and human time

4. Priority trade-offs: we prioritized verifying the most critical theorems

Interoperability between Provers

We used three provers (Coq, Isabelle, Lean), but they lack good interoperability among themselves:

  • The same theorem needs to be implemented separately in the three systems
  • Proof strategies cannot be reused across systems
  • Maintenance costs are high

Ideally, there should be a unified formalization framework, but such a framework does not yet exist.

Philosophical Problems of Machine Verification

Machine verification itself also faces philosophical problems:

  • How do we know that the implementation of the prover is correct?
  • How large is the trusted computing base (TCB) of the prover?
  • If the prover has a bug, are our proofs still reliable?

These problems remind us: machine verification increases reliability, but does not provide absolute certainty.

2.4 The Tension between Formalization and Substantive Content

The Risk of Oversimplification

Formalization requires precise definitions, but precise definitions may oversimplify complex phenomena:

  • The formalization of consciousness (Chapter 10) is based on IIT, but IIT itself is a controversial theory
  • The formalization of negentropy (Chapter 9) used classical thermodynamics, but quantum thermodynamics may require different treatment
  • The formalization of recursive freedom (Chapter 18) used modal logic, but may not capture the full complexity of recursion

The Misleading Nature of Formal Elegance

The “elegance” of formalization may mislead us into accepting problematic premises:

  • If a formal system is very elegant, we may be inclined to believe its axioms
  • But elegance is not truth
  • Formalization may create a sense of false certainty

Chapter 28 (§28.3) specifically warned against this pattern of misuse, but the warning itself cannot fully eliminate the risk.

The Loss of Context

Formalization strips away context, but context is often the key to understanding:

  • Ethical judgments depend on concrete situations
  • The universality of formalization may ignore particularity
  • Abstract formal rules may fail in concrete applications

This is why Volume II must be used in combination with Volume I (natural-language argumentation) and Volume V (case studies).

  • Theorem 23.5 tells us that UCS and RFD are coupled, but how to exploit this coupling in policy is not obvious

  • Cross-civilizational application scenarios are scarce
  • Long-term effects have not yet been verified

The stress-testing part of Volume V partially compensates for this insufficiency, but empirical verification remains an ongoing process.

3.6 Pitfalls That Volume III Needs to Avoid

Based on the experience of Volume II, Volume III needs to avoid the following pitfalls:

Pitfall 1: Over-reliance on Formalization

The formalization of Volume II provides theoretical guarantees, but cannot replace practical judgment. Volume III must:

  • Strike a balance between formalized definitions and practical flexibility
  • Acknowledge that certain judgments cannot be fully formalized
  • Preserve room for human wisdom and experience

Pitfall 2: Ignoring Political Reality

The theory of Volume II is “ideal theory”; Volume III must handle “non-ideal theory”:

  • Considering power asymmetries
  • Considering conflicts of interest
  • Considering historical legacies
  • Considering cultural differences

Pitfall 3: The Usurpation of Instrumental Reason

Indicators and institutions are tools for realizing normative goals, not the goals themselves. Volume III must:

  • Prevent indicators from becoming goals (Goodhart’s Law)
  • Prevent institutions from becoming rigid
  • Maintain reflective awareness of the limits of the tools

Pitfall 4: The Arrogance of Universalism

The formalization of Volume II pursues universality, but Volume III must respect plurality:

  • Acknowledge that different civilizations may have different value priorities
  • Leave room for negotiation and adjustment
  • Avoid disguising the values of a particular culture as universal principles

Require Import Coq.Logic.Classical.

Require Import Coq.Sets.Ensembles.

Require Import CosmicEthics.NewKnowledge.EmbodiedCognition.

( Basic Definitions )

( A subject is an entity capable of cognition )

Parameter Subject : Type.

( Actions that subjects can perform )

Parameter Action : Type.

( Time points )

Parameter Time : Type.

Parameter time_order : Time -> Time -> Prop.

( Recursive freedom degree at time t )

Parameter RF : Subject -> Time -> nat.

( Capability: subject can perform action )

Parameter Can : Subject -> Action -> Prop.

( Choice: subject chooses to perform action )

Parameter Choose : Subject -> Action -> Prop.

( Reflection: subject reflects on proposition )

Parameter Reflect : Subject -> Prop -> Prop.

( Axioms )

( RFP-1: Definition of recursive freedom )

Axiom RFP_1_definition :

forall (s : Subject) (t : Time),

RF s t > 0 <->

(exists (a : Action), Can s a) /\

(exists (p : Prop), Reflect s p) /\

(Reflect s (exists (a : Action), Can s a)).

(forall t', time_order t t' -> RF s2 t' < RF s2 t) ->

~ (morally_permissible a).

forall (s : Subject) (t1 t2 : Time),

exists (a : Action),

Choose s a ->

RF s t2 > RF s t1.

exists (p : Prop),

(forall m, m < n -> exists (q : Prop), Reflect s q).

RF s1 t > 0 ->

ought (recognize s1 (RF s2 t > 0)).

is_subject s ->

RF s t >= 1.

destruct H as [a [Ha [Hr1 Hr2]]].

split; [exists a; exact Ha | ].

exact Hr2.

Qed.

D.10 Usage Guide

How to use this glossary:

1. Quick lookup: use Ctrl+F to search for Chinese or English terms

2. Understanding symbols: consult the Symbol column for the formal representation

3. Locating definitions: use the "Definition Location" column to find detailed explanations

4. Cross-language reference: convert between the code of different provers

Terminology conventions:

1. Consistency: use consistent terminology within the same document

2. Precision: distinguish similar but different concepts (such as "capability" vs "possibility")

3. Contextuality: choose the appropriate level of terminology according to context

4. Traceability: always traceable to a formal definition

Analysis of commonly confused terms:

Term PairDistinction
Recursive freedom vs free willThe former is a formalized concept, the latter a philosophical one
Negentropy vs informationThe former is a thermodynamic concept, the latter an information-theoretic one
Deep uncertainty vs riskThe former has no probability distribution, the latter does
Consistency vs completenessThe former means having no contradiction, the latter that all true propositions are provable
Decidable vs computableThe former concerns decision problems, the latter concerns function computation
Model vs interpretationThe former is a mathematical structure, the latter a semantic mapping
Axiom vs theoremThe former is a premise, the latter a derived result
Formalization vs symbolizationThe former is a systematic process, the latter merely symbolic representation
