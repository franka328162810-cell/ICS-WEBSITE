II. The Core Thesis of This Chapter

Core Thesis:

The revisability of knowledge is not a defect of knowledge but an essential feature of knowledge. Knowledge has an intrinsic version structure and can be formalized as a rigorous versioned cognitive system RK (Revisable Knowledge), which contains:

  • The version space of knowledge states
  • The revision relation between versions
  • The rationality conditions of revision
  • The epistemological foundation of versioned governance

From RK we can rigorously derive:

1. The non-monotonicity of knowledge (new information may overturn old conclusions)

2. The epistemological necessity of versioned governance

3. The normative requirement of epistemic humility

4. The intrinsic connection between deep uncertainty and knowledge revision

§15.1　The Philosophical Motivation for the Revisability of Knowledge

§15.1.1　The Formulation of the Problem

In §3.8 of Chapter 3 of Volume One, we proposed the fourth core feature of the New View of Cognition:

NK-4 (Revisability of Knowledge): knowledge is not a static set of truths but a dynamic, revisable sequence of versions.

This claim faces two basic problems:

Problem One: the conceptual problem

If knowledge is revisable, what then distinguishes "knowledge" from "belief"? Traditional epistemology defines knowledge as "justified true belief" (Justified True Belief, JTB), where "true" implies non-revisability. If knowledge is revisable, does this mean abandoning the truth condition ...

Problem Two: the normative problem

If knowledge is revisable, how should we revise knowledge? What revision is rational? What revision is irrational? This requires a normative theory of revision.

The goal of this chapter is to answer these two questions and to provide a rigorous formalization foundation for the revisability of knowledge.

§15.1.2　The Predicament of Traditional Epistemology

Traditional epistemology has faced a series of predicaments since the "Gettier problem." But the deeper predicament lies in this: traditional epistemology presupposes the staticity and certainty of knowledge.

The staticity presupposition:

Traditional epistemology treats knowledge as a set of propositions:

where each is either knowledge or not knowledge. This presupposition ignores the temporal dimension of knowledge.

The certainty presupposition:

Traditional epistemology requires that knowledge must be "true." But under deep uncertainty (DU), we cannot determine the truth value of a proposition. This leads to:

  • Either we have almost no knowledge (skepticism)
  • Or we lower the standard of knowledge (fallibilism)

Neither is satisfactory.

§15.1.3　Revisability as a Solution

The solution proposed in this chapter is: to abandon the staticity and certainty presuppositions of knowledge and to acknowledge the version structure of knowledge.

Core insight:

Knowledge is not a set of propositions but a versioned belief system:

  • is the knowledge state (version) at time
  • is the revision from version to version
  • Revision is subject to rationality constraints

This framework has the following advantages:

1. It preserves the pursuit of truth: although an individual version may not be "true," a sequence of versions can approximate the truth

2. It accommodates deep uncertainty: under deep uncertainty, we can revise knowledge without abandoning knowledge claims

3. It provides normative guidance: the rationality conditions of revision supply epistemological norms

4. It supports versioned governance: it provides an epistemological foundation for the versioned governance of Chapter 11 of Volume One

§15.1.4　The Relation to Belief Revision Theory

The formalization of this chapter is built on AGM belief revision theory (Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, Makinson, 1985), but makes three important extensions:

Extension One: from belief to knowledge

AGM theory deals with "belief," whereas this chapter extends to "knowledge." The key difference lies in this:

  • Belief may be arbitrary
  • Knowledge must satisfy additional conditions of justification

Extension Two: from single revision to version sequence

AGM theory deals with a single revision (from to ), whereas this chapter extends to a version sequence (). This allows us to study:

  • The historical dependence of versions
  • The rollback mechanism of versions
  • The branching and merging of versions

Extension Three: from certainty to deep uncertainty

AGM theory assumes that new information is certain, whereas this chapter extends to revision under deep uncertainty. This requires:

  • The propagation of uncertainty
  • The reversibility of revision
  • The precautionary principle

Rule RK-R1 (Revision Propagation):

Intuitive explanation: if the evidence is still valid in the new version, the justification relation is inherited.

Rule RK-R3 (Rollback Rule):

Intuitive explanation: when rolling back to an old version, the knowledge of the old version is restored.

§15.4.2　Theorem 15.2: The Epistemological Necessity of Versioned Governance

Under deep uncertainty (DU), if a knowledge system is to remain rational, it must adopt a versioned structure.

Formal statement:

Proof:

Step 1: the definition of deep uncertainty (from Chapter 14)

Step 2: the definition of rationality

A knowledge system is rational if and only if:

1. is consistent

2. is sensitive to new evidence

3. minimizes belief revision

Step 3: reductio ad absurdum

  • Choice A: reject (failing rational condition 2: sensitivity to new evidence)
  • Choice B: accept and delete the conflicting beliefs, but this cannot record the old version (failing rational condition 3: minimizing revision, since rollback is impossible)

Step 4: the necessity of versioning

Therefore, a versioned structure is a necessary condition of rationality.

This theorem provides an epistemological foundation for the versioned governance of Chapter 11 of Volume One. Versioning is not a technical choice but an epistemological necessity.

§15.9　The Philosophical Significance of RK

§15.9.1　The Challenge to Traditional Epistemology

RK poses three fundamental challenges to traditional epistemology:

Challenge One: the static view of knowledge

Traditional epistemology (from Plato to Gettier) treats knowledge as a static "justified true belief." RK proves:

  • Knowledge is essentially dynamic (Theorem 15.1)
  • The static view of knowledge is unworkable under deep uncertainty (Theorem 15.2)

Challenge Two: the necessity of truth

Traditional epistemology requires that knowledge must be "true." RK proposes:

  • An individual version may not be true
  • But a version sequence can approximate the truth (Theorem 15.7)
  • This is a "process view of truth" rather than a "state view of truth"

Challenge Three: foundationalism

Traditional epistemology seeks an "indubitable foundation." RK proves:

  • All knowledge is revisable (Theorem 15.3)
  • There is no absolute epistemological foundation
  • Justification is network-like rather than foundational (axiom RK-13)

  • Or exploration of different possibilities through version branching

Appendix 15E: Open Problems and Research Directions

Theoretical problems:

1. The completeness problem: is the full RK system complete?

  • Difficulty: high
  • Method: may need to draw on the incompleteness proof of temporal logic

2. The decidability problem: are the theorems of RK decidable?

  • Difficulty: medium
  • Method: reduce to known decidable/undecidable problems

3. The expressiveness problem: which epistemological concepts can RK express?

  • Difficulty: medium
  • Method: compare with other epistemic logics

Computational problems:

4. The complexity problem: the computational complexity of versioned knowledge management?

  • Difficulty: medium
  • Method: analyze the time/space complexity of revision, rollback, and branching

5. Approximation algorithms: does an efficient approximation algorithm exist?

  • Difficulty: medium
  • Method: draw on approximation algorithm theory

Application problems:

6. Empirical validation: does human knowledge revision conform to RK?

  • Difficulty: high
  • Method: cognitive psychology experiments

7. AI implementation: how can RK be implemented in large-scale AI systems?

  • Difficulty: high
  • Method: distributed systems, blockchain technology

8. Cross-cultural application: is RK applicable to different cultural traditions?

  • Difficulty: high
  • Method: cross-cultural epistemology research

Philosophical problems:

9. The truth problem: does RK's "process view of truth" hold up?

  • Difficulty: high
  • Method: dialogue with various theories of truth

10. The relativism problem: does RK lead to epistemological relativism?

  • Difficulty: high
  • Method: clarify the difference between revisability and relativism
