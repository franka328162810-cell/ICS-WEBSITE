Reversibility Principle: Staying Open Under Deep Uncertainty

The Reversibility Principle states: under deep uncertainty, decisions and actions should remain as reversible as possible, avoiding irreversible lock-in of future development paths. It is a wise response to our fundamental ignorance and provides guidance for acting responsibly under uncertainty.

Principle Statement

Reversibility Principle (REV)

Under deep uncertainty, decisions and actions should remain as reversible as possible, avoiding irreversible lock-in of future development paths.

Formal Expression
Prefer(Action A, Action B) if Reversibility(A) > Reversibility(B)
When all other conditions are equal, prefer the action with higher reversibility.

Justification of the Principle

Derived from the New Cognition View

The Reversibility Principle directly follows from the New Cognition View’s understanding of deep uncertainty:

Argument steps:

  • We face deep uncertainty — outcomes, probabilities, and utilities are all indeterminate (NK-3).
  • Under deep uncertainty, we may make wrong decisions.
  • If a decision is irreversible, the error cannot be corrected.
  • Conclusion: we should prioritize reversible decisions and preserve the possibility of error correction.

Derived from the Recursive Freedom Principle

The Reversibility Principle can also be derived from the Recursive Freedom Principle:

Argument steps:

  • We should not systematically undermine the freedom conditions of future agents (RFP).
  • Irreversible decisions may lock in future paths and erode future agents’ space of choice.
  • Conclusion: we should maintain reversibility of decisions and preserve option space for the future.

Understanding Reversibility

What Is Reversibility?

Type Definition Example
Physical Reversibility Ability to restore a previous physical state Recyclable materials vs. nuclear waste
Technical Reversibility Ability to undo via technical means Software rollback vs. gene editing
Social Reversibility Ability to change social arrangements Amendable laws vs. constitutional commitments
Path Reversibility Ability to shift development pathways Maintaining diversity vs. technological lock-in

Degrees of Reversibility

Reversibility is not binary, but a matter of degree:

Absolutely Prohibited

The Reversibility Principle does not require all actions to be fully reversible. Instead, it emphasizes that we should:

  • Increase reversibility as much as possible within realistic constraints.
  • Be more cautious with irreversible actions.
  • Absolutely avoid crossing forbidden red lines.

Practical Guidance

How Does the Reversibility Principle Guide Action?

Decision Context Guidance
Technology Choices Prioritize architectures that allow rollback and adjustment.
Policy Design Build in review and revision mechanisms.
Institutional Design Maintain possibilities for amendment and rollback.
Resource Use Prioritize renewable and restorable resources.

Working with the Protocol Stack Methodology

The Reversibility Principle is institutionalized within the protocol stack methodology through:

  • Versioned governance: institutions can have versions and be upgraded.
  • Rollback mechanisms: the ability to revert to previous versions when necessary.
  • Layered decoupling: lower layers remain stable while upper layers stay flexible, preserving overall adaptability.

Limits and Exceptions

Limits of Reversibility

The Reversibility Principle is not absolute:

  • Some valuable actions may inherently be irreversible.
  • Excessive pursuit of reversibility may lead to decision paralysis.
  • Trade-offs between reversibility and other values are sometimes necessary.

Relation to the Forbidden Red Lines

Forbidden red lines demarcate the boundary of reversibility trade-offs:

  • Within the red-line boundary, reversibility can be weighed against other values.
  • The forbidden red lines themselves are non-negotiable and must not be crossed.
