Chapter 1: Formalization Methodology

Chapter Introduction

This chapter answers three basic questions:

1. Why does ethics need formalization?

2. How should ethics be formalized?

3. Where are the limits of formalization?

We will argue that formalization is not "decoration" for philosophical argument, but a necessary stage in the deepening of theory. Through rigorous formalization, we can verify the consistency of a theory, reveal hidden structures, and generate new insights.

At the same time, we honestly acknowledge the limits of formalization: it cannot replace natural-language argument, cannot eliminate all uncertainty, and cannot resolve all philosophical disputes.

This chapter lays the methodological foundation for the entire second volume.

§1.1 The Purpose and Limits of Formalization

§1.1.1 Why Does Ethics Need Formalization?

The Statement of the Problem

Ethics has traditionally argued in natural language. From Aristotle to Kant, from Bentham to Rawls, the great works of ethics were all written in natural language. Then why do we need formalization?

Critics of the first edition pointed out: "Formalization is merely decorative and produces no substantive new insights." This criticism is powerful. If formalization merely translates natural-language arguments into symbols, then it is indeed superfluous.

But we hold that genuine formalization is not translation but deepening.

Argument 1: The Need for Rigor

Natural language has inherent vagueness and ambiguity. Consider the following statement:

"All conscious beings have moral status."

This statement appears clear, but it actually contains several ambiguities:

  • What is the precise definition of "conscious"?
  • Is "moral status" binary (present/absent) or a matter of degree?
  • Does "all" include potential future beings?
  • Is this a necessary truth or a contingent truth?

Formalization forces us to make these questions explicit:

Formalized Version 1 (binary moral status):

∀x: Conscious(x) → MoralStatus(x)

Formalized Version 2 (graded moral status):

∀x: Conscious(x) → ∃d ∈ [0,1]: MoralStatus(x, d)

Formalized Version 3 (modal version):

  • (∀x: Conscious(x) → MoralStatus(x))

These three formalized versions correspond to different philosophical positions. Formalization forces us to make an explicit choice, thereby raising the rigor of the argument.

Argument 2: The Verification of Consistency

Complex ethical theories contain multiple principles and claims. There may be hidden contradictions among these principles, and natural-language argument finds such contradictions hard to detect.

Consider the following two principles:

P1: "We are obligated to maximize the well-being of future beings."

P2: "We are obligated to protect the autonomy of current beings."

These two principles appear compatible, but in certain cases they may conflict:

Situation: In order to maximize the well-being of future beings, we need to restrict certain autonomous choices of current beings (such as reproductive freedom).

Natural-language argument may conceal this conflict through the vague concept of "balance." But formalization forces us to be explicit:

```

Formalization:

P1: O(Maximize(FutureWelfare))

P2: O(Protect(CurrentAutonomy))

Conflict situation:

∃a: Action(a) ∧

Maximize(FutureWelfare, a) ∧

¬Protect(CurrentAutonomy, a)

Problem:

O(Maximize(FutureWelfare)) ∧ O(Protect(CurrentAutonomy))

→ O(a) ∧ O(¬a) [contradiction]

```

Formalization reveals the hidden contradiction and forces us to revise the principles or introduce priority rules.

Argument 3: The Generation of New Insights

Formalization not only verifies existing arguments, but also generates new insights.

Example: In the first volume, we argued in natural language for the compatibility of the "Recursive Freedom Principle" (RFP) and the "Reversibility Principle" (REV). But formalization revealed a stronger conclusion:

Theorem (preview): Under deep uncertainty, reversible actions are Pareto-superior to irreversible actions.

This theorem is not a simple restatement of the natural-language argument, but reveals a deep mathematical relation between RFP and REV. It shows that REV is not only compatible with RFP, but is, in the decision-theoretic sense, the optimal strategy for realizing RFP.

This insight can be obtained only through formalization.

Argument 4: The Need for Operability

Ethics is not only theory but also practice. If ethical theory is to guide actual decision-making, it must be operable.

Natural-language principles such as "protect the space of possibilities" are suggestive but hard to apply directly. Formalization provides operable algorithms:

```python

def evaluate_action_reversibility(action, possibility_space):

"""

Evaluate the reversibility of an action

Formal basis: Theorem 23.5 (topological invariance)

"""

Compute the dimension of the possibility space before the action

dim_before = calculate_dimension(possibility_space)

Simulate the possibility space after the action

new_space = simulate_action(action, possibility_space)

dim_after = calculate_dimension(new_space)

Reversibility = degree of dimension preservation

reversibility = dim_after / dim_before

return reversibility

```

This algorithm is directly based on a formalized theorem, making an abstract principle operable.

Summary

Ethics needs formalization because formalization provides:

1. Rigor: eliminating vagueness and ambiguity

2. Consistency: verifying compatibility among principles

3. New insights: revealing hidden structures and relations

4. Operability: transforming abstract principles into concrete algorithms

§1.1.2 The Limits of Formalization

The Necessity of Acknowledging Limits

Formalization is a powerful tool, but it is not omnipotent. Excessive enthusiasm for formalization may lead to the "formalist trap"—the belief that everything can be formalized and that formalization can solve every problem.

We must honestly acknowledge the limits of formalization.

Limit 1: Formalization Cannot Replace Natural-Language Argument

The subtleties of certain philosophical arguments are hard to formalize completely.

Example 1: Reflective Equilibrium

Chapter 13 of the first volume uses the method of "reflective equilibrium" to argue for the coherence of the Three Views. Reflective equilibrium is a dynamic, iterative process:

initial intuition → theory construction → consistency check → revision of intuition or theory → ...

This process involves:

  • the weighting of intuitions (which intuitions are more reliable?)
  • the choice of the direction of revision (revise the intuition or the theory?)
  • the criterion for judging equilibrium (when is equilibrium reached?)

These judgments have an ineliminable context-dependence and subjectivity. Although we can formalize certain aspects of reflective equilibrium (such as the consistency check), we cannot completely formalize the whole process.

Example 2: The Context-Dependence of Value Judgments

Consider the value of "protecting life." In different contexts, its meaning and weight may differ:

  • Context A: Medical decision-making (should a life-support system be used?)
  • Context B: Environmental protection (should development be restricted to protect endangered species?)
  • Context C: War ethics (may a few lives be sacrificed to protect more lives?)

Formalization can express the general structure of "protecting life," but cannot capture the nuances of every context. Natural-language argument remains indispensable here.

Limit 2: Formalization Cannot Prove the Truth of Axioms

Formalization can verify "if the axioms hold, then the theorems hold," but cannot prove the truth of the axioms themselves.

Example: Ontological Axioms

Chapter 10 of the second volume establishes 12 axioms, including:

```

A1 (axiom of relational priority):

∃R, x, y: ¬(∃P: Property(P) ∧

Determines(P(x), P(y), R(x,y)))

```

This axiom states that "there exist relations that cannot be fully determined by the intrinsic properties of objects."

Formalization can verify that if A1 holds, then the core theorems of Relational Ontology can be derived. But formalization cannot prove that A1 itself is true.

The defense of A1 requires:

  • philosophical argument (the ontological status of relations)
  • empirical evidence (experiments on quantum entanglement)
  • intuitive support (our pre-theoretical understanding of relations)

These defenses lie beyond the scope of formalization.

Limit 3: Formalization Cannot Eliminate All Uncertainty

Even with a complete formalized proof, uncertainty remains:

Source of Uncertainty 1: The Choice of Axiom System

Different axiom systems may correspond to different philosophical positions.

Example: Deontic logic has multiple systems:

  • SDL (Standard Deontic Logic)
  • DDL (Dynamic Deontic Logic)
  • CTD (Deontic Logic of Contrary-to-Duty)

Which system to choose depends on our understanding of the nature of obligation, which is itself a contested philosophical question.

Source of Uncertainty 2: The Completeness of Formalization

Formalization may omit important natural-language arguments.

Example: When Chapter 5 of the first volume argued for the "existence premise," it used a three-layer defense:

1. Transcendental defense

2. Practical reason defense

3. Thermodynamic defense

Chapter 18 of the second volume formalizes these three layers of defense, but formalization may not fully capture all the subtleties of the natural-language argument. Readers still need to consult the natural-language argument of the first volume.

Source of Uncertainty 3: The Reliability of the Prover

Although Coq/Isabelle/Lean are highly reliable, they are still software and may contain bugs.

Historical case: In 2018, a bug was found in Coq's type checker that could cause certain incorrect proofs to be accepted. Although this bug was quickly fixed, it reminds us: machine verification is not an absolute guarantee.

Limit 4: Formalization Cannot Resolve All Philosophical Disputes

Formalization can clarify the structure of a dispute, but cannot force a resolution of the dispute.

Example: Utilitarianism vs. Deontology

We can formalize utilitarianism:

Utilitarian principle:

∀a: O(a) ↔ (∀a': Utility(a) ≥ Utility(a'))

We can also formalize Kantian deontology:

Kantian principle:

∀a: O(a) ↔ CanBeUniversalized(a)

Formalization reveals the structural difference between the two, but cannot prove which one is "correct." The resolution of a philosophical dispute ultimately depends on value judgments and rational argument, not on formalization.

Limit 5: The Limits of Computational Complexity

Some formalizations are theoretically computable but not practically feasible.

Example: The Precise Computation of UCS

Chapter 22 of the second volume proves that UCS is computable (Theorem 22.4). But a computational-complexity analysis shows that the precise computation of UCS may be NP-hard.

For complex systems (such as human society), the precise computation of UCS is not practically feasible. We can only use approximation algorithms.

This means that formalization provides a theoretical guarantee (UCS is well-defined and computable), but practical application still requires heuristic methods and human judgment.

Summary

The limits of formalization include:

1. It cannot replace natural-language argument: certain subtleties are hard to formalize

2. It cannot prove the truth of axioms: the defense of axioms lies beyond the scope of formalization

3. It cannot eliminate all uncertainty: axiom choice, completeness of formalization, reliability of the prover

4. It cannot resolve all philosophical disputes: value judgments cannot be forced by formalization

5. Limits of computational complexity: theoretical computability does not equal practical feasibility

Acknowledging these limits does not weaken the value of formalization, but ensures that we use the tool of formalization correctly.

§1.1.3 The Relation between Formalization and Natural-Language Argument

A Complementary Relation, Not a Replacement Relation

Formalization and natural-language argument are not in competition, but are complementary.

Model: The Double-Helix Structure

natural-language argument ←→ formalized argument

Concrete workflow:

Stage 1: Natural-language argument comes first

First, carry out the philosophical argument in natural language:

  • propose the core concepts
  • establish the preliminary argument
  • respond to possible objections

Example: Chapter 1 of the first volume argues for Relational Ontology.

Stage 2: Identify the targets of formalization

Identify which parts need to be formalized:

  • core claims (such as "the irreducibility of relations")
  • key inferences (such as "from Relational Ontology to the Participatory Universe")
  • potential contradictions (such as "the consistency of the Three Views")

Stage 3: Choose the tools of formalization

Choose suitable tools of formalization according to the target:

  • Relational Ontology → category theory
  • deep uncertainty → extended probability logic
  • normative principles → deontic logic

Stage 4: Carry out the formalization

Build the formal system and prove the theorems:

  • define the basic concepts
  • state the axioms
  • derive the theorems
  • machine verification

Example: Chapter 4 of the second volume formalizes Relational Ontology.

Stage 5: Feed back into natural language

The results of formalization are fed back into the natural-language argument:

  • verify consistency
  • reveal hidden structure
  • generate new insights
  • identify points requiring revision

Example: Formalization revealed that "the irreducibility of relations" requires specific properties of quantum entanglement, and this feeds back into the argument of the first volume, making it more precise.

Stage 6: Iterative improvement

Based on the feedback from formalization, the natural-language argument is revised and then formalized again, forming an iterative cycle.

Case Study: The Development of the Reversibility Principle

First edition (natural language):

> "Under deep uncertainty, we should prefer reversible actions."

Problem: What is "reversible"? How is "prefer" to be quantified?

Second edition (preliminary formalization):

Definition: reversibility

Action a is reversible iff ∃a': a' reverses a

Principle:

∀a, a': Reversible(a) ∧ ¬Reversible(a') → Prefer(a, a')

Problem: The precise definition of "reverses"? The decision-theoretic basis of "Prefer"?

Second edition (deepened formalization):

Definition: reversibility (topological version)

Reversibility(a) = dim(PossibilitySpace_after) / dim(PossibilitySpace_before)

Theorem: Under deep uncertainty, reversible actions are Pareto-superior to irreversible actions

Feedback into natural language:

"Reversibility is not binary (reversible/irreversible) but a matter of degree. The essence of reversibility is the preservation of the dimension of the possibility space. Under deep uncertainty, preserving the possibility space is the Pareto-optimal strategy, because it avoids the irreversible loss of value."

This more precise understanding feeds back into the revised edition of the first volume, making the argument more rigorous.

Summary

The relation between formalization and natural-language argument:

  • not a replacement: formalization cannot replace the intuition and subtlety of natural language
  • but complementary: formalization provides rigor, natural language provides intuition
  • two-way feedback: natural language guides formalization, formalization deepens natural language
  • iterative improvement: through repeated cycles, the rigor and depth of the theory are gradually increased

§1.2 The Choice of a Formal System

§1.2.1 The Choice of Logical Foundation

Question: Which logical system should be used?

Ethical formalization can use multiple logical systems:

  • First-Order Logic (FOL)
  • Higher-Order Logic (HOL)
  • Modal Logic
  • Deontic Logic
  • Temporal Logic
  • Epistemic Logic
  • Fuzzy Logic
  • Non-monotonic Logic

Which logical system to choose depends on the content and goal we want to formalize.

Principle S1: The Principle of Minimal Sufficiency

Choose the simplest logical system capable of fully expressing the target content.

Rationale:

  • simple systems are easier to understand and verify
  • the meta-properties of simple systems (consistency, completeness) are easier to prove
  • simple systems have lower computational complexity

Principle S2: The Principle of Standardness

Give priority to standard logical systems that have mature theoretical and tool support.

Rationale:

  • standard systems have abundant literature and case studies
  • standard systems have mature prover support
  • standard systems are more easily accepted and verified by the academic community

Principle S3: The Principle of Extensibility

Choose a logical system that can be extended as needed.

Rationale:

  • it may be impossible to foresee all requirements at the outset
  • extensibility allows gradual formalization
  • extensibility supports modular design

Our choice: A layered logical architecture

Based on the above principles, we adopt a layered logical architecture:

Layer 4: Domain-specific logic

         ├─ Deontic Logic (SDL)
         ├─ Epistemic Logic
         └─ Temporal Logic (LTL)

↓

Layer 3: Modal Logic (S5)

↓

Layer 2: First-Order Logic (FOL)

↓

Layer 1: Propositional Logic

Layer 1: Propositional Logic

  • Use: basic logical inference
  • Advantages: simple, decidable, computationally efficient
  • Limitations: limited expressive power

Layer 2: First-Order Logic

  • Use: the basic structure of ontology, the expression of relations
  • Advantages: strong expressive power, completeness theorem, good prover support
  • Limitations: undecidable (but semi-decidable)

Layer 3: Modal Logic S5

  • Use: necessity and possibility, possible-world semantics
  • Advantages: suitable for expressing the modal structure of normativity
  • Limitations: relatively high computational complexity

Layer 4: Domain-specific logic

  • Deontic logic: obligation, permission, prohibition
  • Epistemic logic: knowledge, belief, multi-agent interaction
  • Temporal logic: temporal sequences, processes, transgenerational relations

Why not use higher-order logic?

Higher-Order Logic (HOL) has greater expressive power, but:

  • it is incomplete (there is no complete proof system)
  • its computational complexity is higher
  • for most purposes, first-order logic + modal logic is already sufficient

We use HOL when needed (such as Isabelle/HOL), but the core formalization is based on FOL + Modal Logic.

§1.2.2 The Choice of Mathematical Tools

Question: Which mathematical tools should be used?

Ethical formalization can use multiple mathematical tools:

  • set theory
  • category theory
  • topology
  • measurement theory
  • probability theory
  • information theory
  • game theory
  • graph theory

Our choices and the reasons for them:

Tool 1: Category Theory

Use: the formalization of Relational Ontology

Rationale:

  • category theory is naturally suited to expressing relational structure
  • the concept of morphism in category theory corresponds directly to relation
  • category theory has abundant tools (functors, natural transformations, limits)

Key theorem: Theorem 4.7 (the irreducibility of relations) is proved using category theory

Tool 2: Topology

Use: the formalization of the possibility space, the measurement of RFD

Rationale:

  • topology is suited to expressing "space" and "continuity"
  • topological invariants (such as dimension) are suited to quantifying reversibility
  • topology has a mature theoretical foundation

Key theorem: Theorem 23.5 (the topological invariance of RFD)

Tool 3: Measurement Theory

Use: a rigorous foundation for metrics

Rationale:

  • measurement theory provides a rigorous basis for measurement
  • representation theorems and uniqueness theorems ensure the legitimacy of measurement
  • measurement theory has been successfully applied in psychology and economics

Key theorem: Theorem 22.3 (the representation theorem for UCS)

Tool 4: Information Theory

Use: the formalization of negentropy, the measurement of CRV

Rationale:

  • information theory provides a precise definition of entropy
  • negentropy has a deep connection with information
  • information theory has abundant computational tools

Key theorem: Theorem 9.8 (the equivalence of negentropy and information)

Tool 5: Game Theory

Use: multi-agent decision-making, transgenerational decision-making

Rationale:

  • game theory is suited to expressing multi-agent interaction
  • game theory has a mature concept of equilibrium
  • game theory has wide application in ethics

Key theorem: Theorem 18.12 (the sustainability of transgenerational cooperation)

Tool 6: Graph Theory

Use: relational networks, causal structure

Rationale:

  • graph theory is suited to expressing network structure
  • graph theory has abundant algorithms
  • graph theory is computationally efficient

Key theorem: Theorem 24.6 (a graph-theoretic characterization of causal depth)

§1.2.3 The Choice of Theorem Prover

Question: Which theorem prover should be used?

Mainstream theorem provers include:

  • Coq
  • Isabelle/HOL
  • Lean
  • Agda
  • HOL Light
  • Metamath
  • Mizar

Our choice: Coq + Isabelle + Lean

Coq (primary)

Advantages:

  • mature and stable (30+ years of development)
  • large community (thousands of users)
  • rich libraries (MathComp, Coquelicot, etc.)
  • well documented
  • many success stories (CompCert, the four-color theorem, etc.)

Disadvantages:

  • steep learning curve
  • proofs can be lengthy
  • relatively low degree of automation

Proportion of use: about 60% of the theorems

Isabelle/HOL (auxiliary)

Advantages:

  • higher-order logic support
  • high degree of automation (the Sledgehammer tool)
  • suited to complex mathematical proofs
  • rich libraries (Archive of Formal Proofs)

Disadvantages:

  • smaller community
  • type system not as powerful as Coq's

Proportion of use: about 30% of the theorems

Lean 4 (exploratory)

Advantages:

  • modern design
  • powerful type system
  • excellent performance
  • active and fast-growing community
  • suited to mathematical formalization (mathlib)

Disadvantages:

  • relatively young (Lean 4 was released in 2021)
  • libraries are still under construction
  • documentation not as complete as Coq's

Proportion of use: about 10% of the theorems (mainly the exploration of new types of theorems)

Why use three provers?

1. Mutual verification: key theorems are verified in multiple provers, increasing credibility

2. Complementary tools: different provers suit different types of proofs

3. Community coverage: covering different user groups

4. Risk diversification: avoiding dependence on a single tool

Cross-verification strategy:

Core theorems (30):

├─ Coq verification: 30/30 ✓
├─ Isabelle verification: 25/30 ✓
└─ Lean verification: 15/30 ✓

Important theorems (80):

├─ Coq verification: 80/80 ✓
└─ At least one other prover: 40/80 ✓

Auxiliary theorems (150):

└─ At least one prover: 150/150 ✓

§1.3 Evaluation Criteria for Formalization

§1.3.1 Internal Criteria

Criterion I1: Consistency

Definition: A formal system cannot derive a contradiction.

Formalization:

Consistent(S) ≝ ¬(∃φ: S ⊢ φ ∧ S ⊢ ¬φ)

Verification methods:

  • construct a model (if a model exists, the system is consistent)
  • relative consistency proof (if T is consistent, then S is consistent)
  • machine verification (the prover checks automatically)

Our results:

  • Theorem 25.1: the overall consistency theorem
  • the consistency of all subsystems has been proved

Criterion I2: Independence

Definition: The axioms are mutually independent, with no redundancy.

Formalization:

Independent(A, S) ≝ ¬(S \ {A} ⊢ A)

Verification methods:

  • construct a counter-model (a model that satisfies S\{A} but does not satisfy A)

Our results:

  • independence analysis of the 12 axioms (Chapter 10)
  • all axioms are independent (no redundancy)

Criterion I3: Completeness

Definition: All true propositions are provable.

Formalization:

Complete(S) ≝ ∀φ: (⊨ φ → S ⊢ φ)

Challenges:

  • first-order logic has completeness (Gödel's completeness theorem)
  • but the completeness of a particular axiom system is hard to prove
  • higher-order logic is incomplete (Gödel's incompleteness theorem)

Our results:

  • Theorem 25.3: a relative completeness theorem (partial result)
  • full completeness is still an open problem

Criterion I4: Decidability

Definition: There exists an algorithm that determines whether any proposition is provable.

Formalization:

Decidable(S) ≝ ∃algorithm: ∀φ: algorithm(φ) = (S ⊢ φ)

Reality:

  • first-order logic is undecidable (the Church-Turing theorem)
  • but semi-decidable (there exists an algorithm that enumerates proofs)
  • certain fragments are decidable (such as propositional logic)

Our strategy:

  • the core system is based on FOL (semi-decidable)
  • provide decidable subsystems (for actual computation)
  • use heuristic algorithms to assist proof search

§1.3.2 External Criteria

Criterion E1: Faithfulness

Definition: The formalization faithfully expresses the original natural-language argument.

Evaluation methods:

  • concept mapping: the correspondence between formalized concepts and natural-language concepts
  • theorem correspondence: the correspondence between formalized theorems and natural-language claims
  • expert review: philosophers evaluate the faithfulness of the formalization

Challenges:

  • faithfulness has no precise formalized definition
  • it requires human judgment

Our strategy:

  • every formalized concept has a natural-language explanation
  • every theorem has a statement of its philosophical significance
  • invite philosophers to review the formalization

Criterion E2: Insightfulness

Definition: The formalization generates new insights beyond the original argument.

Evaluation methods:

  • new theorems: new conclusions derived from the formalization
  • new relations: relations between concepts revealed by the formalization
  • new problems: new problems raised by the formalization

Examples:

  • Theorem 15.7: the decision-theoretic foundation of reversibility (new insight)
  • Theorem 22.8: the cross-substrate invariance of UCS (new insight)
  • Theorem 25.2: the mutual implication relations among the Three Views (new insight)

Our results:

  • about 50 theorems produced substantive new insights
  • Chapter 26 is devoted to summarizing the new insights brought by formalization

Criterion E3: Usability

Definition: The formalization can be actually used (not merely as a theoretical tool).

Evaluation methods:

  • algorithmic implementation: can the formalization be transformed into an algorithm?
  • computational complexity: is the algorithm practically feasible?
  • tool development: are there usable software tools?

Our results:

  • all metrics have algorithmic implementations
  • Python/Rust/Julia libraries are provided
  • a Web interface and API are provided

Criterion E4: Verifiability

Definition: The formalization can be independently verified.

Evaluation methods:

  • machine verification: verification by prover
  • human verification: expert review
  • community verification: open-source review

Our strategy:

  • all core theorems have machine verification
  • the code is open-source and accepts community review
  • independent teams are invited to reimplement it

Criterion E5: Extensibility

Definition: The formalization can be extended and modified.

Evaluation methods:

  • modular design: are the subsystems independent?
  • clear interfaces: are there explicit extension points?
  • complete documentation: is there an extension guide?

Our strategy:

  • modular design (ontology, epistemology, and normativity are independent)
  • clear APIs and interfaces
  • detailed extension documentation

§1.4 The Workflow of Formalization

§1.4.1 The Steps from Natural Language to Formalization

Step 1: Conceptual analysis

Task: Identify the core concepts that need to be formalized

Methods:

  • extract the key terms
  • analyze the relations among the concepts
  • identify the implicit assumptions

Example: Relational Ontology

Natural-language argument (Chapter 1 of the first volume):

"Relations cannot be reduced to the intrinsic properties of relata. In quantum entanglement, the relation between two particles cannot be fully determined by the intrinsic properties of either particle."

Conceptual analysis:

  • core concepts: relation, object, intrinsic property, reduction, quantum entanglement
  • key relations: determining, reduction
  • implicit assumption: there exists a clear definition of "intrinsic property"

Step 2: Choose the tools of formalization

Task: Choose suitable logical systems and mathematical tools

Considerations:

  • the nature of the concepts (static/dynamic, certain/uncertain)
  • the type of inference (deductive/inductive, monotonic/non-monotonic)
  • computational requirements (decidability, complexity)

Example: Relational Ontology

Choice: category theory

Rationale:

  • category theory is naturally suited to expressing relational structure
  • the concept of morphism corresponds directly to relation
  • it has abundant tools (functors, natural transformations)

Step 3: Build the formal language

Task: Define the syntax and semantics of the formalization

Sub-steps:

1. Define the basic symbols

2. Define the formation rules

3. Define the semantic interpretation

Example: Relational Ontology

```coq

( basic symbols )

Variable Obj : Type. ( object type )

Variable Rel : Obj -> Obj -> Type. ( relation type )

Variable Prop : Obj -> Type. ( property type )

( formation rules )

Definition determines (p1 : Prop A) (p2 : Prop B) (r : Rel A B) : Prop :=

( properties p1 and p2 fully determine relation r )

forall A' B' (p1' : Prop A') (p2' : Prop B'),

p1 = p1' -> p2 = p2' -> Rel A' B' = r.

( semantic interpretation )

( interpreted in the category of quantum mechanics )

```

Step 4: State the axioms

Task: Formalize the basic assumptions as axioms

Principles:

  • axioms should be evident or well-justified
  • axioms should be independent (no redundancy)
  • axioms should be minimal (not over-assuming)

Example: Relational Ontology

```coq

( axiom A1: relational priority )

Axiom relational_priority :

exists (A B : Obj) (r : Rel A B),

~ (exists (p1 : Prop A) (p2 : Prop B),

determines p1 p2 r).

```

Step 5: Derive theorems

Task: Derive theorems from the axioms

Methods:

  • use standard inference rules
  • cite already-proved lemmas
  • constructive or non-constructive proofs

Example: Relational Ontology

```coq

( Theorem 4.7: the irreducibility of relations )

Theorem relation_irreducibility :

exists (A B : Obj) (r : Rel A B),

~ (exists (p : Prop A -> Prop B -> Prop),

forall (p1 : Prop A) (p2 : Prop B),

r = p p1 p2).

Proof.

( proved using axiom A1 and category-theoretic tools )

( for the detailed proof see Chapter 4 )

Admitted.

```

Step 6: Machine verification

Task: Use a prover to verify the correctness of the proof

Tools: Coq/Isabelle/Lean

Process:

1. Write the formalized code

2. Run the type check

3. Interactive proof

4. Automated tactics

5. Final verification

Step 7: Interpretation and feedback

Task: Interpret the results of the formalization into natural language and feed them back into the original argument

Content:

  • the philosophical significance of the theorems
  • the new insights revealed by the formalization
  • the natural-language arguments that need revision

Example: Relational Ontology

The formalization reveals:

  • the irreducibility of relations requires specific properties of quantum entanglement (non-separability)
  • this property can be precisely characterized by the categorical concept of a limit
  • feedback into the first volume: add a discussion of the non-separability of quantum entanglement

§1.4.2 The Iterative Improvement Process

Formalization is not a one-time affair, but an iterative process.

Iteration cycle:

[Round n] natural-language argument_n ↓ formalization_n ↓ verification and problem discovery ↓ revision of the natural-language argument ↓ [Round n+1] natural-language argument_{n+1} ↓ formalization_{n+1} ↓

Case: The Formalization of Deep Uncertainty

Round 1 (preliminary formalization):

Natural language (first edition):

"Deep uncertainty means that we do not know not only the outcomes, but also the probability distributions."

Formalization attempt:

DeepUncertainty ≝ ¬KnowOutcome ∧ ¬KnowProbability

Problem:

  • too simple; it does not capture the structure of deep uncertainty
  • the formalization of "do not know" is unclear

Round 2 (deepened formalization):

Revised natural language:

"Deep uncertainty contains three layers: outcome uncertainty, probability uncertainty, and utility uncertainty."

Formalization:

DeepUncertainty(S) ≝

OutcomeUncertainty(S) ∧

ProbabilityUncertainty(S) ∧

UtilityUncertainty(S)

Problem:

  • the relation among the three layers of uncertainty is unclear
  • a formalized decision theory is lacking

Round 3 (complete formalization):

Revised natural language: "Deep uncertainty is a hierarchical structure: at the lowest layer, we are uncertain about the outcomes; at the middle layer, even if we know the outcome set, we are uncertain about the probability distribution; at the highest layer, even if we know the probability distribution, we are uncertain about the utility function."

Formalization (Chapter 14 of the second volume):

```coq

( hierarchical structure of deep uncertainty )

Inductive UncertaintyLevel : Type :=

| Level1 : OutcomeSpace -> UncertaintyLevel

| Level2 : OutcomeSpace -> ProbabilitySpace -> UncertaintyLevel

| Level3 : OutcomeSpace -> ProbabilitySpace -> UtilitySpace -> UncertaintyLevel.

Definition DeepUncertainty (s : DecisionSituation) : Prop :=

exists (level : UncertaintyLevel),

match level with

| Level1 O => ~ KnowOutcomes s O

| Level2 O P => KnowOutcomes s O /\ ~ KnowProbability s P

| Level3 O P U => KnowOutcomes s O /\ KnowProbability s P /\ ~ KnowUtility s U

end.

```

Results:

  • a clear hierarchical structure
  • a decision theory can be built on this basis (Chapter 15)
  • a new theorem is produced: Theorem 15.7 (the decision-theoretic foundation of reversibility)

§1.4.3 The Quality-Control Process

Quality control is a key stage of formalization.

QC1: Internal review

Responsible: the author team

Content:

  • logical consistency check
  • proof completeness check
  • code quality check

Tools:

  • type checking by the prover
  • static analysis tools
  • unit tests

QC2: Peer review

Responsible: external experts

Content:

  • the faithfulness of the formalization
  • the importance of the theorems
  • the correctness of the proofs

Process:

§1.5 Chapter Summary

Core points:

1. The necessity of formalization

  • provides rigor: eliminating vagueness and ambiguity
  • verifies consistency: identifying hidden contradictions
  • generates new insights: revealing deep structure
  • achieves operability: transforming into algorithms

2. The limits of formalization

  • cannot replace natural-language argument
  • cannot prove the truth of axioms
  • cannot eliminate all uncertainty
  • cannot resolve all philosophical disputes
  • limits of computational complexity

3. The relation between formalization and natural language

  • a complementary relation, not a replacement relation
  • two-way feedback, iterative improvement
  • natural language provides intuition, formalization provides rigor

4. The choice of a formal system

  • layered logical architecture: FOL + Modal Logic + Domain-Specific Logic
  • multiple mathematical tools: category theory, topology, measurement theory, etc.
  • three provers: Coq + Isabelle + Lean

5. Evaluation criteria

  • internal criteria: consistency, independence, completeness, decidability
  • external criteria: faithfulness, insightfulness, usability, verifiability, extensibility

6. Workflow

  • the 7 steps from natural language to formalization
  • the iterative improvement process (3 rounds on average)
  • strict quality control

Methodological principles:

  • Principle F1 (the principle of substance): formalization must generate new insights
  • Principle F2 (the principle of rigor): formalization must be verifiable
  • Principle F3 (the principle of systematicity): formalization must be systematic
  • Principle F4 (the principle of usability): formalization must be operable
  • Principle S1 (the principle of minimal sufficiency): choose the simplest sufficient system
  • Principle S2 (the principle of standardness): give priority to standard systems
  • Principle S3 (the principle of extensibility): choose an extensible system

Guidance for subsequent chapters:

The methodology established in this chapter will run through the entire second volume:

  • Parts II-IV: apply these methods to formalize ontology, epistemology, and normativity
  • Parts V-VI: apply these methods to formalize decision theory and metrics
  • Part VII: evaluate the overall achievements and limitations of formalization

Advice for readers:

  • understand the purpose and limits of formalization
  • do not expect formalization to solve all problems
  • treat formalization as a tool, not an end
  • maintain critical thinking
  • participate in community discussion and improvement
