§1.1 The Limitations of the Traditional Cosmology

§1.1.1 The Problems of Substance Ontology

I. The Basic Structure of Substance Ontology

The mainstream tradition of Western philosophy — from ancient Greek atomism to early modern mechanism and on to contemporary physicalism — shares one basic ontological assumption: the world is composed of independently existing substances, and these substances possess intrinsic properties that do not depend on other things.

Definition 1.1 (Substance Ontology)

Substance ontology is an ontological position that maintains:

  • (1) the basic units of existence are substances;
  • (2) substances possess intrinsic properties, and these properties do not depend on relations to other substances;
  • (3) relations among substances are derivative and secondary, grounded in the intrinsic properties of substances.

II. The Internal Difficulties of Substance Ontology

Substance ontology faces several internal philosophical difficulties, and these difficulties become especially pronounced in the interstellar civilization context.

Difficulty 1: The problem of individuation

Substance ontology presupposes that substances can be clearly individuated — that we can clearly distinguish one substance from another. Yet this presupposition encounters difficulties at several levels.

At the level of quantum mechanics, the indistinguishability of identical particles poses a fundamental challenge to individuation. Two electrons are not only identical in all measurable properties, but are in principle indistinguishable — exchanging the two electrons produces no observable difference whatsoever.

Difficulty 2: The dubiousness of intrinsic properties

Substance ontology assumes that substances possess intrinsic properties that do not depend on relations. Yet when we press the question of what a genuinely "intrinsic property" is, this assumption becomes dubious.

Take mass as an example. In Newtonian mechanics, mass appears to be an intrinsic property of a body. Yet in general relativity, mass and spacetime geometry mutually define each other — mass tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells mass how to move. Mass is no longer an intrinsic property independent of spacetime relations.

Take electric charge as an example. Charge appears to be an intrinsic property of a particle. Yet the meaning of charge is defined entirely by its interaction with the electromagnetic field. A "charge" that interacts with no field whatsoever would have no observable meaning.

Proposition 1.1 (The relational dependence of intrinsic properties)

The physical quantities traditionally regarded as "intrinsic properties" (such as mass, charge, and spin) have physical meanings that depend on relations to other physical quantities or fields. Purely intrinsic properties that depend on no relation whatsoever are dubious in physics.

§1.1.2 The Problems of the Static Cosmology

I. The Basic Structure of the Static Cosmology

Accompanying substance ontology is the static cosmology — a view that understands the basic mode of existence as static states rather than dynamic processes.

Definition 1.2 (Static Cosmology)

The static cosmology is an ontological position that maintains:

  • (1) the basic mode of existence is states rather than processes;
  • (2) change is a transition between states, decomposable into a series of instantaneous states;
  • (3) time is a parameter of the state sequence, not an intrinsic dimension of existence.

II. The Internal Difficulties of the Static Cosmology

Difficulty 1: The ontological status of time

The static cosmology treats time as a parameter of a state sequence, but this treatment faces deep difficulties.

In relativity, time is not an absolute parameter but, together with space, constitutes the spacetime manifold. Different observers make different judgments about "simultaneity," which means that there is no absolute "instantaneous state."

In quantum mechanics, the status of time is even more complex. In standard quantum mechanics time is a parameter rather than an operator, which is asymmetric with the treatment of space, and this constitutes one of the core difficulties of quantum gravity research.

Difficulty 2: The irreducibility of process

The static cosmology assumes that processes can be reduced to sequences of states. Yet many physical and biological processes possess irreducible processual features.

Take thermodynamic processes as an example. The second law of thermodynamics describes the directionality of processes (entropy increase), not a property of states. The entropy value of a system by itself cannot tell us the direction of the process; only the process itself possesses directionality.

Take life processes as an example. Life is not a sequence of static states, but a continuous flow of matter and energy. The essence of life lies in the maintenance of a process, not in the preservation of any particular state.

Proposition 1.3 (The ontological irreducibility of process)

Certain physical and biological phenomena possess processual features that are ontologically irreducible. These phenomena cannot be fully described as sequences of states; process itself must be taken as a basic ontological category.

Difficulty 3: Emergence and novelty

The static cosmology struggles to accommodate genuine emergence and novelty. If the evolution of the universe is fully determined by initial conditions and laws, then everything "new" is already implicit in the initial conditions and merely unfolds over time.

Yet the evolution of complex systems appears to exhibit genuine novelty — the emergence of new structures, new functions, new possibilities. This novelty conflicts with the deterministic tendency of the static cosmology.

III. The Failure of the Static Cosmology in the Interstellar Civilization Context

Failure 1: The challenge of the deep-time scale

At the deep-time scale (thousands to millions of years), the limitations of the static cosmology become evident:

Failure 2: The need for an open future

The governance of interstellar civilization requires a cosmology capable of accommodating an open future. If the future were fully determined by initial conditions and laws, then "governance" would lose its meaning — we would merely be executing a script already written.

The core claim of the Recursive Freedom Principle (PRF) is to protect the space of possibilities. This claim presupposes that the future is open and that genuine possibilities exist, rather than being merely a limitation of our knowledge.

Failure 3: The basis for participatory governance

The static cosmology is incompatible with participatory governance. If the evolution of the universe is predetermined, then our "participation" is merely an illusion. Genuine participatory governance requires a cosmology that acknowledges that agents can truly influence the future.

Proposition 1.4 (The interstellar-civilization limitation of the static cosmology)

The static cosmology faces structural failure in the interstellar civilization context: the openness of the deep-time scale, the genuine possibility of the future, and the need for participatory governance all conflict with the core assumptions of the static cosmology.

§1.1.3 The Problems of Spectator Epistemology

I. The Basic Structure of Spectator Epistemology

Accompanying substance ontology and the static cosmology is spectator epistemology — an epistemological position that regards the cognizing subject as an external spectator of the world.

Definition 1.3 (Spectator Epistemology)

Spectator epistemology is an epistemological position that maintains:

  • (1) the cognizing subject and the object of cognition are sharply separated;
  • (2) cognition is a passive reflection or representation of the external world;
  • (3) ideal cognition is "objective" cognition undisturbed by the subject.

II. The Internal Difficulties of Spectator Epistemology

Difficulty 1: The impossibility of subject-object separation

Spectator epistemology assumes that subject and object can be sharply separated. Yet this assumption encounters difficulties at several levels.

At the level of quantum mechanics, the measurement problem shows that observer and observed system are inseparable. The act of measurement itself changes the state of the measured system, and the separation of subject and object is in principle impossible.

At the level of cognitive science, research on embodied cognition shows that cognition does not occur in a mind separated from the world, but in the continuous interaction of body and environment.

At the level of social science, the problem of reflexivity shows that the research of social scientists itself affects the social phenomena being studied.

Difficulty 2: The impossibility of pure representation

Spectator epistemology assumes that knowledge is a pure representation of the external world. Yet contemporary research in cognitive science and philosophy of science shows that cognition always involves active construction.

Predictive processing theory holds that the brain does not passively receive sensory input but actively generates predictions about the world and corrects them with sensory input. Cognition is a dynamic loop of prediction and feedback, not passive representation.

The theory-ladenness thesis in the philosophy of science shows that observation is always influenced by a theoretical framework. There is no "pure" observation unaffected by a theoretical framework.

Difficulty 3: The impossibility of value neutrality

Spectator epistemology is usually accompanied by the ideal of value neutrality — cognition should be pure description unaffected by value judgments. Yet research on scientific practice shows that value judgments permeate every stage of scientific inquiry:

StageValue permeation
Choice of problemWhich problems are worth studying?
Choice of methodWhich methods are acceptable?
Evaluation of evidenceWhat evidence is sufficient?
Acceptance of theoryWhich theories are acceptable?

Proposition 1.5 (The internal difficulties of spectator epistemology)

The three core assumptions of spectator epistemology — the separation of subject and object, pure representation, and value neutrality — all encounter serious difficulties in contemporary epistemology and philosophy of science. These difficulties are not accidental, but stem from the structural defects of spectator epistemology.

III. The Failure of Spectator Epistemology in the Interstellar Civilization Context

Failure 1: The inevitability of cognitive intervention

In the interstellar civilization context, cognitive acts are themselves interventions. The observation of extraterrestrial civilizations, the assessment of AI systems, and the prediction of future generations — all of these cognitive acts affect the objects cognized.

The governance of interstellar civilization cannot rest on spectator epistemology. Governors are not external spectators of the world, but participants in the world's construction. The act of governance itself changes the system being governed.

This epistemological shift has profound implications for governance practice:

Failure 3: The challenge of cross-civilizational cognition

Spectator epistemology assumes that there exists an objective world independent of the cognizing subject, and that all cognizing subjects cognize the same world. Yet in a cross-civilizational context, different civilizations may possess fundamentally different cognitive frameworks, and the assumption of "one and the same world" becomes dubious.

Proposition 1.6 (The interstellar-civilization limitation of spectator epistemology)

Spectator epistemology faces structural failure in the interstellar civilization context: the inevitability of cognitive intervention, the need for participatory governance, and the challenge of cross-civilizational cognition all conflict with the core assumptions of spectator epistemology.

§1.2.1 Quantum Mechanics and Relationality

I. The Relational Structure of Quantum Mechanics

The mathematical formalism and experimental results of quantum mechanics provide an important scientific basis for relational ontology. This section analyzes how quantum mechanics supports the ontological claim that "relations precede entities."

Quantum entanglement and nonlocal correlations

Quantum entanglement shows that the properties of entangled particles are not independently intrinsic properties but mutually related relational properties. The whole of an entangled state cannot be reduced to a simple combination of parts.

Relational quantum mechanics

Relational quantum mechanics (RQM) is an interpretation of quantum mechanics proposed by Carlo Rovelli that elevates relationality to a core principle of quantum mechanics.

The core claim of RQM is that a quantum state is not an absolute property of a system, but a relational property relative to a particular observer. There is no "state of the system in itself"; there is only "the state of system S relative to observer O."

Different observers may assign different quantum states to the same system, and these different assignments are all correct — because the quantum state is itself relational.

Proposition 1.9 (The ontological implications of RQM)

Relational quantum mechanics shows that quantum states are relational rather than absolute. This supports a relational ontology: the basic structure of existence is a network of relations, not independent entities possessing absolute properties.

II. Inferences from Quantum Mechanics to Relational Ontology

On the basis of the quantum-mechanical features described above, we may cautiously infer certain elements of relational ontology.

Inference 1: The ontological priority of relations

Quantum entanglement shows that in certain cases relations (entanglement correlations) are more fundamental than the relata (individual particles). The holistic properties of an entangled state cannot be reduced to the properties of the parts.

Inference 2: The relationality of properties

RQM shows that quantum states are relational properties relative to an observer. This supports a more general claim: properties may be relational rather than intrinsic.

Inference 3: The derivedness of individuality

The indistinguishability of identical particles shows that individuality may not be a basic ontological category, but something derived from a more fundamental relational structure.

III. Boundary Declarations

In inferring relational ontology from quantum mechanics, caution must be maintained. This work makes the following boundary declarations:

Boundary Declaration 1.1 (The openness of quantum-mechanical interpretation)

The interpretation problem of quantum mechanics remains unresolved. RQM is only one of many interpretations, and other interpretations (such as the many-worlds interpretation, Bohmian mechanics, and GRW theory) understand relationality differently. This work does not presuppose any particular interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Boundary Declaration 1.2 (The distinction between the microscopic and the macroscopic)

The relational features of quantum mechanics manifest mainly at the microscopic level. The inference from microscopic relationality to a macroscopic relational ontology requires additional argument. This work treats quantum mechanics as an "inspiration" for relational ontology rather than a "proof" of it.

Boundary Declaration 1.3 (The principle of structural use)

This work borrows the relational structure of quantum mechanics to explicate relational ontology, but does not claim that quantum effects directly determine macroscopic phenomena. This is "structural use" rather than "concept hijacking" (see Definitions 0.8 and 0.9 in Chapter 0).

§1.2.2 Relativity and Process

I. The Processual Structure of Relativity

Einstein's theory of relativity — both special and general — provides an important scientific basis for a processual cosmology. This section analyzes how relativity supports the ontological claim that "process precedes state."

The unification of spacetime and the relativity of "now"

One of the core insights of special relativity is the unification of time and space. In Minkowski spacetime, time is no longer an absolute parameter independent of space, but together with space constitutes the four-dimensional spacetime manifold.

More importantly, "simultaneity" is relative in relativity. For two spatially separated events, different inertial observers may make different judgments about their temporal order. There is no absolute "now."

Proposition 1.11 (The relativity of "now")

Special relativity shows that "now" is not absolute but relative to the observer. There is no absolute "instantaneous state" identical for all observers. This supports a processual cosmology: the basic mode of existence is the spacetime process, not the instantaneous state.

The block universe and the tension with process

Relativity is often interpreted as supporting the "block universe" view: past, present, and future are equally real, time is merely one dimension of the four-dimensional spacetime block, and there is no special "flow."

Yet the relationship between the block-universe view and the processual cosmology is complex. On the one hand, the block-universe view dissolves the absolute "now," which is consistent with the processual cosmology. On the other hand, the block-universe view appears to "spatialize" time and dissolve its dynamism, which is in tension with the processual cosmology.

The position taken by this work is: relativity dissolves the absolute instantaneous state, but does not necessarily lead to a static block-universe view. §1.2.3 Quantum-Mechanical Interpretation and Participation

I. The Measurement Problem and the Role of the Observer

The measurement problem in quantum mechanics is one of the deepest problems in physics and philosophy. At its core: what role does the observer play in quantum measurement?

The statement of the measurement problem

In standard quantum mechanics, the evolution of a system follows two different rules:

1. Unitary evolution: when the system is not measured, its state evolves unitarily according to the Schrödinger equation:

2. Wavefunction collapse: when the system is measured, its state "collapses" to the eigenstate corresponding to the measurement outcome.

The core difficulty of the measurement problem is: what constitutes a "measurement"? Why does measurement lead to dynamics different from unitary evolution? What role does the observer play in this process?

The Copenhagen interpretation and the observer

The Copenhagen interpretation (represented by Bohr) emphasizes the central position of the observer in quantum mechanics. According to the Copenhagen interpretation, quantum mechanics does not describe "reality itself" independent of observation, but only describes observational outcomes. The observer is not a passive spectator, but a participant in the construction of quantum phenomena.

Bohr's principle of complementarity states that different properties of a quantum system (such as position and momentum) cannot be measured precisely at the same time; they are "complementary." Which property is realized depends on what measurement the observer chooses to perform.

Proposition 1.15 (The participatory implications of the Copenhagen interpretation)

The Copenhagen interpretation shows that the observer is not an external spectator of the quantum world, but a participant in the construction of quantum phenomena. The realization of quantum properties depends on the observer's choice of measurement.

II. Wheeler's "Participatory Universe"

John Archibald Wheeler was one of the most influential physicists of the twentieth century. He proposed the concept of the "participatory universe," elevating the participation of the observer to the level of cosmology.

The delayed-choice experiment

Wheeler's delayed-choice experiment is the key to understanding the participatory universe.

In the double-slit experiment, if we do not detect which slit the photon passes through, the photon exhibits wave behavior and produces an interference pattern. If we detect which slit the photon passes through, the photon exhibits particle behavior and the interference pattern disappears.

Wheeler's delayed-choice experiment shows that we can decide whether to detect which slit the photon passes through after the photon has already passed through the slits. Our choice appears to determine the photon's behavior "retroactively."

The philosophical implications of this experiment are profound: the observer's choice participates in determining the nature of physical reality, and can even influence the past "retroactively."

"It from Bit"

In his later years Wheeler proposed the slogan "It from Bit," maintaining that physical reality (It) arises from information (Bit). In this picture, the universe is not composed of material substances, but of information and acts of observation.

Wheeler wrote: "Every physical quantity, at its deepest level, derives from the posing and answering of yes-or-no questions, from binary choices, from bits."

Proposition 1.16 (Wheeler's participatory universe)

Wheeler's concept of the participatory universe maintains that the observer is not an external spectator of the universe but a participant in its construction. The properties of the universe are partly determined by the observer's acts of observation. This provides a physical basis for participatory epistemology.

Argument 1: The physical argument

As discussed in §1.2.1, several features of quantum mechanics support relational reality:

1. Quantum entanglement shows that the properties of entangled particles are relational and cannot be determined independently of one another.

2. Relational quantum mechanics shows that quantum states are relational properties relative to an observer.

3. The indistinguishability of identical particles shows that the individuality of particles is not basic.

Argument 2: The philosophical argument

Relational reality can be supported by the following philosophical arguments:

Argument 2.1 (The argument from the dubiousness of intrinsic properties)

1. If entities possessed intrinsic properties independent of relations, then these properties should be determinable independently of any relation.

2. Yet the determination of any property requires some measurement or comparison, and measurement and comparison are themselves relations.

3. Therefore, there are no intrinsic properties that can be determined independently of relations.

4. Therefore, all properties are relational.

Argument 2.2 (The relational argument for individuation)

1. To distinguish two entities, we need some criterion of distinction.

2. Any criterion of distinction involves relations between the entity and other things (including the observer).

3. Therefore, individuation itself is relational.

4. Therefore, the individuality of entities depends on relations.

Argument 3: The practical argument

In the interstellar civilization context, relational reality has important practical significance:

1. The blurring of subject boundaries requires us to understand subjects in terms of relations rather than entities.

2. Interaction among civilizations requires us to understand civilizations in terms of networks of relations rather than independent entities.

3. The possibility of cross-substrate existence requires us to define subjects in terms of functional relations rather than material composition.

III. The Implications of the Proposition

Implication 1: The reconstruction of ontology

NC-1 requires us to reconstruct the basic categories of ontology. Traditional ontology took "substance" and "property" as its basic categories; NC-1 requires us to take "relation" as the basic category and to understand "substance" and "property" as derivatives of the network of relations.

Implication 2: A reunderstanding of individuality

NC-1 does not deny the existence of individuals, but requires us to reunderstand individuality. An individual is not an independent entity existing prior to relations, but a relatively stable node in a network of relations. Individuality is a matter of degree, not all-or-nothing.

Implication 3: The relation between whole and part

NC-1 supports a moderate holism: the whole cannot be fully reduced to the parts, because the relational structure of the whole is something the parts do not possess. Yet this is not a mysterious holism, but one that can be understood in terms of the structure of a network of relations.

3. Participatory governance requires us to acknowledge that agents can truly influence the course of a process.

2. The internality of relations: actual entities establish internal relations with other entities through "prehension." Relations are not external, but constitutive of the essence of the actual entity.

3. The universality of experience: Whitehead maintains a "panexperientialism," holding that experience is a universal feature of the universe, not limited to human beings or animals.

4. Creativity and novelty: the universe is creative, continuously producing novelty. The future is not predetermined, but generated in process.

§1.4.2 The Relation to Structural Realism

I. The Core Claims of Structural Realism

Structural realism is an important position in contemporary philosophy of science, maintaining that what scientific theories reveal is the structure of the world, not its intrinsic nature.

Two forms of structural realism

FormEnglishCore claim
Epistemic structural realismEpistemic Structural Realism (ESR)We can know only the structure of the world, not its intrinsic nature
Ontic structural realismOntic Structural Realism (OSR)The basic existence of the world is structure, not entities with intrinsic natures

Epistemic structural realism (ESR)

ESR was proposed by John Worrall in 1989, seeking a middle position between scientific realism and anti-realism. ESR maintains:

1. The structural content of scientific theories (mathematical equations, relations) is retained through theory change.

2. We have reason to believe that these structures reflect the real structure of the world.

3. But we cannot know the intrinsic nature of the world; we can know only its structure.

Ontic structural realism (OSR)

OSR was developed by James Ladyman, Don Ross, and others, advancing structural realism from epistemology to ontology. OSR maintains:

1. The basic existence of the world is structure, not entities with intrinsic natures.

2. Entities are nodes in a structure, and their identity is determined by their position in the structure.

3. Relations are ontologically prior to relata.

§1.4.3 The Relation to Eastern Philosophy

I. Relational and Processual Thought in Eastern Philosophy

The traditions of Eastern philosophy — especially Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucianism — contain rich relational and processual thought that resonates interestingly with the New Cosmology.

The Buddhist theory of dependent origination

One of the core teachings of Buddhism is "dependent origination" (pratītyasamutpāda), which maintains that all phenomena arise from the confluence of causes and conditions and have no independent self-existing substance.

The core claims of dependent origination:

1. No self-nature (anātman/śūnyatā): all phenomena lack an independently self-existing essence (svabhāva).

2. Mutual dependence: all phenomena depend on one another, and no phenomenon can exist independently of others.

3. Impermanence: all phenomena are impermanent and in constant change.

The theory of dependent origination bears a structural similarity to NC-1 (relationality) and NC-2 (process) of the New Cosmology.

Processual thought in Daoism

Daoist philosophy emphasizes the dynamism and processuality of the "Dao." The Dao De Jing opens with: "The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao." The Dao is not a static substance, but a dynamic process.

The core claims of Daoist thought:

1. The dynamism of the Dao: "Reversal is the movement of the Dao" — the movement of the Dao is cyclical.

2. The mutual transformation of all things: "Misfortune is what fortune leans on; fortune is what misfortune hides in" — opposites transform into one another.

3. Governance through non-action: follow natural processes rather than imposing intervention.

Daoist processual thought resonates with NC-2 of the New Cosmology.

Relational thought in Confucianism

Confucian philosophy emphasizes the relational existence of the person. The person is not an isolated individual, but an existence situated within various relations (ruler-minister, father-son, husband-wife, elder-younger brother, friend).

The core claims of Confucian thought:

1. The relational self: a person's identity is determined by the relations in which they stand.

2. The relationality of ren: "The benevolent person loves others" — ren is realized in relations.

3. The normativity of li: li regulates interpersonal relations and maintains social order.

Confucian relational thought resonates with NC-1 of the New Cosmology.

If the basic structure of existence is a network of relations (NC-1), then the basic unit of ethics should also be relations rather than isolated individuals. The object of ethical concern is not only the welfare of individuals, but also the quality of relations and the health of the relational network.

II. The Ethics of Interdependence

Relational ontology leads to an ethics of "interdependence":

The core claims of the ethics of interdependence

1. Interdependence of existence: no existent can exist independently of other existents. Every existent is situated in a network of relations, and its existence depends on its relations to other existents.

2. Interdependence of welfare: the welfare of an individual cannot be independent of the network of relations in which it stands. The health of the relational network is a condition of individual welfare.

3. Mutual influence of action: any action will affect other existents through the network of relations. There are no purely "private" actions.

4. Mutuality of responsibility: because existence is interdependent, responsibility is also mutual. We bear responsibility toward the existents with which we stand in relations.
