§0.1.1 A Theory as a "Provisional Convention"

I. Introducing the Concept of a "Provisional Convention"

Definition 0.1 (Provisional Convention)

A provisional convention is a theoretical framework that:

  • (1) is regarded, under current epistemic conditions, as the best available option;
  • (2) explicitly acknowledges its own fallibility and revisability;
  • (3) sets out definite revision-triggering conditions and revision procedures;
  • (4) does not claim eternal validity or the status of absolute truth.

II. Why This Work Is a "Provisional Convention"

This work positions itself as a "provisional convention" on three grounds:

Reason One: An epistemic reason — grounded in the New View of Cognition (NK)

According to the New View of Cognition (NK), to be argued in detail in Chapter 3, knowledge has the following features:

  • NK-1 (participatory cognition): The knower is not a spectator of the world but a participant. The act of cognition itself alters the object of cognition.
  • NK-2 (social knowledge): Knowledge is a social achievement, depending on the collaboration and verification of an epistemic community.
  • NK-4 (epistemic humility): Given the participatory and social character of cognition, the knower should remain humble about his or her cognitive claims.

Applying these principles to this work itself, we obtain:

Proposition 0.1 (The Self-Application Principle)

The epistemic stance of this work (the New View of Cognition) requires this work to treat itself as a provisional convention. Any theory that claims the status of absolute truth contradicts the core propositions of the New View of Cognition.

Reason Two: A historical reason — lessons from the history of science

The history of science shows that even the most successful theories may be revised or superseded:

TheoryStatusSubsequent development
Newtonian mechanics200 years of "absolute truth"Revised by relativity and quantum mechanics
Classical thermodynamicsFoundation of engineeringDeepened by statistical mechanics
Darwinian evolutionFoundation of biologyExtended by the modern evolutionary synthesis

The problem this work addresses — the normative foundations of interstellar civilization — is far more speculative than any of the theories above. If physical theories require revision, this work should expect revision all the more.

Reason Three: A practical reason — openness toward the future

The core concern of this work is the governance of interstellar civilization. This concern points to future scenarios that have not yet arrived:

  • the emergence of AGI (artificial general intelligence)
  • possible contact with extraterrestrial intelligence
  • the interstellar expansion of human civilization
  • the emergence of new kinds of intelligent agents

The specific features of these scenarios cannot be fully foreseen at present. This work must therefore remain open, so that it can be adaptively revised as new scenarios arise.

IV. Distinguishing a "Provisional Convention" from "Relativism"

An important clarification: positioning this work as a "provisional convention" is not equivalent to adopting a relativist stance.

Definition 0.2 (Relativism)

Relativism holds that there is no objective truth or normative standard that is cross-cultural, cross-era and cross-agent. All truths and norms are relative to a particular culture, era or agent.

Definition 0.3 (Fallibilism)

Fallibilism holds that our knowledge claims may be mistaken, but this does not mean that there is no objective truth. We should pursue truth while acknowledging that we may not yet have attained it.

This work adopts a fallibilist stance, not a relativist one:

DimensionRelativismFallibilism (the stance of this work)
Objective truthDoes not existExists, but we may not yet have attained it
Progress of knowledgeImpossible (only change)Possible (approximating truth through revision)
Standard of criticismNo cross-cultural standardExists, but requires continual revision
Practical attitudeAny view is equally validSome views are better than others

Formal statement:

Proposition 0.2 (Compatibility of Fallibilism and the Provisional Convention)

The status of provisional convention is compatible with fallibilism and incompatible with relativism. This work acknowledges that it may be mistaken, but it does not acknowledge that all views are equally valid. Revision of this work aims at a better approximation of truth, not mere change.

§0.1.3 This Work as an "Open System"

I. Introducing the Concept of an "Open System"

Definition 0.5 (Open System)

An open system is a theoretical framework that:

  • (1) maintains continuous interaction with its external environment (the scholarly community, fields of practice, new scenarios);
  • (2) can absorb external inputs (criticism, new evidence, new insights) and make internal adjustments;
  • (3) produces external outputs (research agendas, policy recommendations, conceptual tools);
  • (4) evolves and develops through this interaction.

This concept borrows from the notion of an open system in thermodynamics, but applies it in the context of a theoretical framework.

Formal statement:

II. The Link between the Open System and the Three New Views

Positioning this work as an "open system" is highly consistent with the core propositions of the Three New Views:

Link with the New Cosmology (NC):

The New Cosmology emphasizes relationality (NC-1) and processuality (NC-2). Treating this work as an open system means acknowledging the relationality between this work and its environment (the scholarly community, fields of practice), and the processuality of this work as a continuous process of evolution.

Link with the New View of Life (NL):

The New View of Life emphasizes negentropic persistence (NL-2). As a "conceptual living entity," a theoretical framework likewise depends for its persistence on drawing "conceptual negentropy" from its environment — new insights, criticism, evidence — in order to maintain and increase its organizational complexity.

Link with the New View of Cognition (NK):

The New View of Cognition emphasizes the social character of knowledge (NK-2) and its negotiability (NK-4). Treating this work as an open system means acknowledging that its development depends on the collaboration of the scholarly community, and that its content is negotiable and revisable.

Proposition 0.4 (Self-Application of the Three New Views)

The framework of the Three New Views in this work requires this work to treat itself as an open system. A theoretical framework that asserts relationality, processuality and sociality would fall into self-contradiction if it treated itself as closed, static and independent.

I. The Necessity of Interdisciplinary Integration

The problem this work addresses — the normative foundations of interstellar civilization — is essentially interdisciplinary. It involves:

PhysicsThe basic structure of the cosmos, thermodynamic constraints, the boundaries of information theory

No single discipline can answer the normative problems of interstellar civilization on its own. Interdisciplinary integration is therefore not an optional methodological preference but an intrinsic requirement of the problem itself.

Proposition 0.6 (The Necessity of Interdisciplinary Integration)

The normative problems of interstellar civilization are essentially interdisciplinary. Any attempt to answer them from a single disciplinary perspective will be incomplete.

Formal statement:

II. Three Operating Principles of Interdisciplinary Integration

To prevent interdisciplinary integration from degenerating into "shallow collage" or "pseudo-scientific analogy-hunting," this work lays down three strict operating principles:

Principle A: The empirical core is not to be disturbed

Definition 0.7 (Empirical Core)

The empirical core is the body of well-validated empirical propositions and theoretical structures within a discipline that are widely accepted by its scholarly community.

Content of the principle: This work does not propose competing revisions to the empirical core of mature scientific theories.

Specific constraints:

Type of constraintSpecific contentExample
PhysicalDoes not challenge the empirical predictions of thermodynamics, relativity or quantum mechanicsDoes not claim that "negentropy can increase without limit"
BiologicalDoes not challenge evolution or the core findings of molecular biologyDoes not claim that "life can violate thermodynamics"
Cognitive-scientificDoes not challenge the core empirical findings of cognitive scienceDoes not claim that "consciousness is independent of physical processes"

Formal statement:

Principle B: Structural use, not concept hijacking

Definition 0.8 (Structural Use)

Structural use is the borrowing of the formal structure of one discipline (e.g. a mathematical model or logical framework) for analysis in another domain, while making explicit the analogical character and the boundaries of such borrowing.

Definition 0.9 (Concept Hijacking)

Concept hijacking is the transplanting of a technical term from one discipline into another domain while stripping it of its original precise meaning, in order to manufacture a false sense of scientific authority.

Content of the principle: This work engages only in structural use; concept hijacking is strictly forbidden.

Specific constraints:

Information theoryBorrowing "information entropy" to analyse cognitive systemsClaiming that "consciousness just is information" while ignoring the hard problem

Formal statement:

Principle C: Interdisciplinary bridges are truncatable

Content of the principle: The interdisciplinary analogies and structural mappings in this work can be partly or entirely removed while preserving the normative and institutional backbone.

Specific meaning:

II. The Necessity of Prudence

Speculative thinking must be constrained by prudence, for the following reasons:

Reason for prudenceSpecific content

Speculative thinking must be constrained by prudence. Unconstrained speculation may lead to mistaken conclusions and improper action.

III. Strategies for Managing the Tension

Strategy One: Labelling the level of speculation

This work labels propositions with their level of speculation:

Level of speculationDefinitionExample
S1 (low)Inference based on sufficient evidenceThermodynamic constraints will continue to hold
S2 (moderate)Inference based on reasonable extrapolationAI capabilities will continue to grow
S3 (high)Inference based on limited evidenceAGI may emerge in this century
S4 (very high)Inference based on pure theoryExtraterrestrial civilizations may exist

Strategy Two: Contextual conditioning

Speculative propositions are stated in conditional form, making their underlying assumptions explicit:

Example:

"If AGI emerges in this century (condition), then a framework for assessing agent status needs to be established in advance (conclusion)."

Strategy Three: Robustness first

Under speculative scenarios, prefer strategies that are effective across many possible scenarios (robust strategies) over strategies that are optimal only in a specific scenario.

Strategy Four: Preference for reversibility

Under speculative scenarios, prefer reversible decisions, so that they can be corrected if the speculation proves mistaken.

This strategy is highly consistent with the "Reversibility Principle" to be argued in Chapter 13 of this work.

§0.2.4 The Relation between the Normative and the Descriptive

I. The Classic Formulation of the "Is-Ought" Problem

Hume's Law states that one cannot derive an "ought" (a normative proposition) from a pure "is" (a descriptive proposition).

Formal statement:

This law poses a challenge for this work: if the Three New Views are descriptive (descriptions of the cosmos, life and cognition), how can normative principles be derived from them?

II. The Response Strategy of This Work

This work does not seek to violate Hume's Law, but adopts the following strategies:

Strategy One: Introducing bridge principles

This work introduces "bridge principles." They are not purely descriptive propositions but mixed propositions containing a normative component.

Definition 0.10 (Bridge Principle)

A bridge principle is an intermediary principle connecting descriptive and normative propositions. It contains a minimal normative assumption about "rational agents," which makes the transition from description to norm possible.

Strategy Two: The constitutive argument

This work adopts a "constitutive argument" strategy: certain normative requirements are constitutive conditions of rational agency.

Structure of the argument:

1. Premise One (descriptive): X is a rational agent

2. Premise Two (constitutive): The constitutive conditions of rational agency include C

3. Conclusion (normative): X ought to satisfy C

This argument does not violate Hume's Law, because Premise Two is not purely descriptive but is a constitutive claim about rational agency.

Strategy Three: Reflective equilibrium

This work adopts a Rawlsian method of "reflective equilibrium": seeking a mutually supportive equilibrium among concrete intuitions, mid-level principles and abstract theory.

This work expressly declares that it does not promise the following:

This work does not promise to provide a complete answer to the normative problems of interstellar civilization. It is an open research programme, not a closed theoretical system.

This work does not promise that its framework can be used immediately for actual decision-making. It is a concept prototype that requires further development and validation.

This work does not promise value neutrality. It contains normative claims, and these claims reflect particular value orientations.

Non-promise Five: Universal acceptance

This work does not promise that its views will be universally accepted. Many of its claims are controversial.

Chapter 0 Notes

[1] Semantic Versioning: Semantic Versioning is a standard method of software version management, proposed by Tom Preston-Werner. See: Preston-Werner, T. (2013). "Semantic Versioning 2.0.0." https://semver.org/. In this work, this software-engineering concept is applied to the version management of a theoretical framework; this is an instance of "structural use" (Definition 0.8).

[2] Technology Readiness Level (TRL): TRL is a standard framework developed by NASA for assessing technology maturity. See: Mankins, J. C. (1995). "Technology Readiness Levels." NASA White Paper. The "Conceptual Readiness Level" (CRL) developed in this work is an analogical application of TRL in the domain of theoretical frameworks.

[3] Hume's Law: In A Treatise of Human Nature, Hume pointed out that one cannot derive "ought" from "is." See: Hume, D. (1739). A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part I, Section I. For a contemporary discussion of Hume's Law, see: Pigden, C. R. (Ed.). (2010). Hume on Is and Ought. Palgrave Macmillan.

[4] Naturalistic Fallacy: Moore proposed this concept in Principia Ethica. See: Moore, G. E. (1903). Principia Ethica. Cambridge University Press. On the relation between the naturalistic fallacy and Hume's Law, see: Frankena, W. K. (1939). "The Naturalistic Fallacy." Mind, 48(192), 464-477.

[5] Reflective Equilibrium: Rawls developed this methodological concept in A Theory of Justice. See: Rawls, J. (1971). A Theory of Justice. Harvard University Press, pp. 48-51. For a detailed discussion of reflective equilibrium, see: Daniels, N. (1979). "Wide Reflective Equilibrium and Theory Acceptance in Ethics." Journal of Philosophy, 76(5), 256-282.

[6] Fallibilism: The classic formulation of fallibilism comes from Peirce. See: Peirce, C. S. (1897). "Fallibilism, Continuity, and Evolution." In Collected Papers of Charles Sanders Peirce, Vol. 1. For a discussion of contemporary fallibilism, see: Haack, S. (1979). "Fallibilism and Necessity." Synthese, 41(1), 37-63.

[7] Open System: The concept of an open system originates from von Bertalanffy's general systems theory. See: von Bertalanffy, L. (1968). General System Theory: Foundations, Development, Applications. George Braziller. In this work, this systems-theory concept is applied to the self-understanding of a theoretical framework.

[8] Constitutive Argument: The constitutive argument is a form of Kantian transcendental argument. On the application of the constitutive argument in ethics, see: Korsgaard, C. M. (1996). The Sources of Normativity. Cambridge University Press.

[9] Interdisciplinary research methodology: For a methodological discussion of interdisciplinary research, see: Repko, A. F., & Szostak, R. (2020). Interdisciplinary Research: Process and Theory (4th ed.). SAGE Publications. On the challenges of interdisciplinary integration, see: Klein, J. T. (1990). Interdisciplinarity: History, Theory, and Practice. Wayne State University Press.

[10] Conceptual Prototype and Design Thinking: The methodology of the conceptual prototype comes from the design-thinking tradition. See: Brown, T. (2009). Change by Design: How Design Thinking Transforms Organizations and Inspires Innovation. Harper Business. On the role of prototypes in theory development, see: Morgan, M. S., & Morrison, M. (Eds.). (1999). Models as Mediators: Perspectives on Natural and Social Science. Cambridge University Press.
