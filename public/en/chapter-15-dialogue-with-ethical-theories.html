§15.1.1 The Core Claims of Utilitarianism

I. The Definition of Utilitarianism

Definition 15.1 (Utilitarianism)

Utilitarianism is a theory of normative ethics that holds that the moral rightness of an action depends on its consequences—specifically, on its contribution to overall well-being or utility.

Formal statement:

II. The Core Propositions of Utilitarianism

Proposition 15.1 (The core propositions of utilitarianism)

Utilitarianism includes the following core propositions:

  • Consequentialism: the moral value of an action depends entirely on its consequences
  • Welfarism: the only thing with intrinsic value is well-being
  • Aggregationism: the well-being of different subjects can be aggregated into overall well-being
  • Maximization: overall well-being ought to be maximized

III. The Advantages of Utilitarianism

Proposition 15.2 (The advantages of utilitarianism)

Utilitarianism has the following theoretical advantages:

  • Simplicity: it provides a single criterion of evaluation
  • Universality: it applies to all subjects capable of experiencing well-being
  • Comparability: it provides a basis for comparing different actions
  • Reformatory character: it can criticize existing institutions and practices

IV. The Challenges of Utilitarianism

Proposition 15.3 (The challenges facing utilitarianism)

Utilitarianism faces the following major challenges:

  • The utility-measurement problem: how are the utilities of different subjects measured and compared?
  • The distributive-justice problem: does aggregation neglect the fairness of distribution?
  • The rights problem: may individual rights be sacrificed for overall well-being?
  • The deep-uncertainty problem: how is it applied when consequences are unpredictable?

Proposition 15.19 (The core propositions of deontology)

§15.4 Dialogue with Contractarianism

§15.4.1 The Core Claims of Contractarianism

I. The Definition of Contractarianism

Definition 15.4 (Contractarianism)

Contractarianism/Contractualism is a theory of normative ethics that holds that the justification of moral principles derives from a (hypothetical) contract or agreement among rational agents.

Formal statement:

II. The Core Propositions of Contractarianism

Proposition 15.51 (The core propositions of contractarianism)

Contractarianism includes the following core propositions:

  • Contractual basis: the justification of moral principles derives from agreement
  • Rational agents: the participants in the agreement are rational agents
  • Hypothetical contract: the contract is hypothetical rather than actual
  • Acceptability criterion: principles ought to be reasonably acceptable

Proposition 15.52 (The advantages of contractarianism)

Contractarianism has the following theoretical advantages:

  • Democratic character: it emphasizes agreement and consent
  • Fairness: it guarantees fairness through procedure
  • Justifiability: principles can be justified to others
  • Institutional applicability: it suits institutional design

Contractarianism faces the following major challenges:

  • The future-generations problem: how do future generations participate in the contract?

  • The cross-species problem: how do non-human subjects participate in the contract?

The completion of this volume marks the establishment of the normative foundation of interstellar civilization studies. But this is only a beginning, not an end.

This work invites the reader to:

1. Criticize: offer criticisms of the arguments of this volume, pointing out logical gaps or empirical discrepancies

2. Supplement: provide perspectives and arguments that this volume has not covered

3. Apply: try to apply the principles of this volume to concrete situations

4. Develop: develop new theories and methods on the basis of this volume

As the New View of Cognition emphasizes, knowledge is a social achievement. This work is not a closed dogma but an open research program. Its value lies not in providing final answers but in opening an exploration that can be continuously developed.
