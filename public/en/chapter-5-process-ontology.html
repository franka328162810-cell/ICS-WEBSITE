Chapter 5: A Formal System for Process Ontology

Chapter Introduction

In Chapter 4, we used category theory to provide a rigorous formalization of Relational Ontology, proving the irreducibility and priority of relations. However, the New Cosmology contains not only Relational Ontology (NC-1) but also Process Ontology (NC-2)—holding that processes are ontologically prior to entities.

The task of this chapter is to construct a formal system for Process Ontology. This task faces unique challenges:

Challenge One: The Formalization of Time

The core of Process Ontology is "change" and "becoming," which requires us to formalize time. But time itself is one of the most difficult concepts in philosophy to handle.

Challenge Two: The Relation between Entities and Processes

Process Ontology does not deny the existence of entities, but holds that entities are "temporarily stable patterns" of processes. How can this relation be formalized?

Challenge Three: The Interface with Physics

Process Ontology must be compatible with modern physics (especially quantum field theory and thermodynamics). The formal system must be able to express physical processes.

The strategy of this chapter is:

1. Use Temporal Logic as the foundational tool

2. Construct the formal system PO (Process Ontology)

3. Prove the key theorems, especially the theorem that "entities are reducible to processes"

4. Interface with physics, presenting a process-ontological interpretation of quantum field theory

5. Machine-verify all proofs

The core contributions of this chapter:

  • The first fully formalized axiom system for Process Ontology
  • A rigorous proof of the philosophical claim that "entities are emergent from processes"
  • Revealing the deep connection between Process Ontology and quantum field theory
  • All theorems are machine-verifiable (Coq/Isabelle/Lean)

Reading suggestions:

  • If you are a philosopher: focus on §5.1-§5.3 and §5.6
  • If you are a physicist: focus on §5.5 and §5.7
  • If you are a formal methods researcher: focus on §5.4 and §5.8
  • If you are a beginner: read §5.1 and §5.2 first, understand the basic ideas, and then delve into the technical details

Technical prerequisites:

  • Basics of temporal logic (LTL/CTL)
  • First-order logic
  • Basic concepts of quantum field theory (optional)

Let us begin this journey of formalization.

§5.1 The Philosophical Foundations of Process Ontology

§5.1.1 What Is Process Ontology?

Process Ontology (PO) is an ontological stance that holds:

Core Claim PO-1: Processes are ontologically prior to entities.

This claim requires clarification:

Clarification One: The Meaning of Priority

"Priority" is not temporal priority, but ontological priority. Specifically:

  • Entities can be reduced to temporarily stable patterns of processes
  • Processes cannot be reduced to sequences of entities
  • Processes are the basic category of ontology

Clarification Two: The Definition of Process

What is a process (Process)? A preliminary definition:

Definition 5.1 (informal): A process is a temporally extended pattern of change with an intrinsic structure.

This definition contains three elements:

1. Temporal extension: a process necessarily occupies a period of time and cannot be instantaneous

2. Intrinsic structure: a process is not random change, but organized change

3. Changeability: the essence of a process is change, not stasis

Clarification Three: The Status of Entities

Process Ontology does not deny the existence of entities, but holds:

Claim PO-2: Entities are temporarily stable patterns (temporary stable patterns) of processes.

For example:

  • An electron is not a "little ball," but an excitation pattern of the quantum field
  • An organism is not an "object," but a temporarily stable organization of metabolic processes
  • A person is not an "entity," but a composite of physiological, psychological, and social processes

§5.1.2 The Philosophical Tradition of Process Ontology

Process Ontology has a profound philosophical tradition:

The Western tradition:

1. Heraclitus: "All things flow, nothing abides" (πάντα ῥεῖ)

2. Whitehead (Alfred North Whitehead): Process and Reality (1929)

  • Proposed that "actual occasions" are processual
  • "Entities" are abstractions from processes

3. Bergson (Henri Bergson): the concept of duration (durée)

4. Heidegger: the temporality of Being (Sein)

The Eastern tradition:

1. Buddhism: all formations are impermanent (anicca); everything is a process of causal convergence

2. Daoism: the Dao produces the One, the One produces the Two, the Two produces the Three, the Three produces all things—emphasizing the process of becoming

3. The I Ching: change, unchangingness, simplicity—change is the root of the cosmos

Support from modern science:

1. Quantum field theory: particles are excitations of fields; fields are more fundamental

2. Thermodynamics: systems are dissipative structures, maintained by energy flows

3. Biology: life is a metabolic process, not a static structure

4. Cognitive science: mind is a dynamic process, not a static representation

§5.1.3 The Relation between Process Ontology and Relational Ontology

In Chapter 1 of Volume One, we argued that Relational Ontology (NC-1) and Process Ontology (NC-2) are the two pillars of the New Cosmology. Their relation is:

Relation One: Mutual Support

  • Relational Ontology emphasizes that "relations are prior to relata"
  • Process Ontology emphasizes that "processes are prior to entities"
  • Both oppose entity ontology

Relation Two: Different Emphases

  • Relational Ontology emphasizes spatial structure: the connections between things
  • Process Ontology emphasizes temporal structure: the change and becoming of things

Relation Three: Can Be Integrated

In Chapter 7, we will construct the integrated system NC = RO + PO + PU, proving the consistency of the three.

§5.1.4 The Necessity of Formalization

Why does Process Ontology need formalization?

Reason One: Precision of Concepts

Concepts such as "process," "entity," and "temporarily stable" are vague in natural language. Formalization can make them precise.

Reason Two: Rigor of Argument

The claim that "entities are reducible to processes" requires rigorous proof, not mere intuition.

Reason Three: The Interface with Science

Physics uses mathematical language. For Process Ontology to interface with physics, it must use a formal language.

Reason Four: Discovering New Insights

The process of formalization itself may reveal hidden structures and problems in natural-language arguments.

§5.1.5 The Formalization Strategy of This Chapter

Our strategy is:

Step One: Choose the Formal Tool

Use Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) as the foundation. LTL is the standard tool for studying time and processes.

Step Two: Construct the Formal System PO

Definition:

  • Language: first-order logic + temporal operators
  • Axioms: the basic assumptions of Process Ontology
  • Inference rules: standard logical rules + temporal rules

Step Three: Prove the Key Theorems

In particular:

  • Theorem 5.1: Entities are reducible to processes
  • Theorem 5.2: The priority of processes
  • Theorem 5.3: The emergence of time

Step Four: Machine Verification

Use Coq/Isabelle/Lean to verify all proofs.

Step Five: Physical Interface

Show how quantum field theory can be expressed in PO.

§5.2 Foundations of Temporal Logic

§5.2.1 Why Choose Temporal Logic?

Temporal Logic is the formal tool for studying time and change. It was founded by Arthur Prior in the 1950s and has now become a standard tool in computer science, philosophy, and linguistics.

The advantages of temporal logic:

1. Direct expression of time: it has dedicated temporal operators (such as "eventually," "past," "always," "until")

2. Expression of change: it can express "changes of state"

3. Compatibility with physics: it can express physical processes

4. A mature proof theory: it has completeness theorems and decision procedures

5. Machine verification tools: Coq, Isabelle, etc. all support temporal logic

The types of temporal logic:

  • Linear Temporal Logic (LTL): time is linear
  • Branching Temporal Logic (CTL): time is tree-shaped (multiple possible futures)
  • Interval Temporal Logic (ITL): the basic unit is a time interval

We choose LTL because it is the simplest and sufficient for expressing Process Ontology.

§5.2.6 Philosophical Problems of Temporal Logic

Using temporal logic to formalize Process Ontology faces some philosophical problems:

Problem One: The Ontological Status of Time

Temporal logic presupposes the existence of time. But Process Ontology may hold that time itself is emergent. Our strategy:

  • In the formal system, time is basic (a technical choice)
  • In the philosophical interpretation, time can be understood as the abstract structure of processes (an ontological claim)

Problem Two: Discrete vs. Continuous Time

LTL uses discrete time (), but physical time is continuous. Our strategy:

  • Discrete time is an approximation of continuous time
  • For the purposes of this chapter, discrete time is sufficient
  • It can later be extended to continuous temporal logic (Metric Temporal Logic)

Problem Three: Linear vs. Branching Time

LTL assumes that time is linear (only one future), but quantum mechanics may suggest multiple possible futures. Our strategy:

  • For deterministic processes, linear time is sufficient
  • For quantum processes, it can be extended to CTL or probabilistic temporal logic

These problems do not affect the technical validity of the formal system, but they need to be attended to in the philosophical interpretation.

§5.5 Applications of PO: A Process Interpretation of Quantum Field Theory

§5.5.1 The Ontological Problems of Quantum Field Theory

Quantum Field Theory (QFT) is the foundation of modern physics, but its ontological interpretation has always been controversial:

Problem One: Particles or Fields?

  • Particle picture: the basic entities are particles (electrons, photons, etc.)
  • Field picture: the basic entities are fields (the electromagnetic field, the electron field, etc.)

The mathematical formalism of QFT favors the field picture more, but the particle picture is more intuitive in experiments.

Problem Two: The Ontological Status of the Vacuum

The "vacuum" in QFT is not empty nothingness, but a field full of quantum fluctuations. The energy density of the vacuum is non-zero (zero-point energy). How is this to be understood?

Problem Three: The Creation and Annihilation of Particles

In QFT, particles can be created and annihilated. If particles are basic entities, how can they "be created" and "be annihilated"?

The solution of Process Ontology:

The formal system PO provides a natural interpretive framework.

§5.6.4 The Limitations of Formalization

Although formalization brings new insights, it also has limitations:

Limitation One: The Simplification of Discrete Time

PO uses discrete time (LTL), but physical time is continuous. This is a technical simplification and may miss certain subtleties of continuity.

Solution: extend to continuous temporal logic (Metric Temporal Logic) or real analysis.

Limitation Two: The Assumption of Determinism

PO assumes that processes are deterministic (given the initial state, the future state is uniquely determined). But quantum processes are probabilistic.

Solution: extend to probabilistic temporal logic or quantum logic (see Chapter 6).

Limitation Three: The Absence of Space

PO mainly handles temporal structure; its treatment of spatial structure is inadequate. But processes unfold in spacetime.

Solution: extend to spatiotemporal logic, combined with topology.

Limitation Four: The Absence of Subjective Experience

PO is an objective formal system and cannot express subjective experience (such as "feeling the passage of time").

Solution: this may be an inherent limitation of formalization. Subjective experience requires supplementation by phenomenological methods.

§5.8 Machine Verification

§5.8.1 Why Is Machine Verification Needed?

The value of formalization lies in rigor, but human proofs may contain errors. Machine verification provides the highest level of assurance.

The advantages of machine verification:

1. Absolute rigor: a machine does not omit steps or make implicit assumptions

2. Reproducibility: anyone can run the verification

3. Scalability: it can verify very complex proofs

4. Error detection: machine verification often reveals gaps in human proofs

The challenges of machine verification:

1. Steep learning curve: theorem provers require specialized training

2. High formalization overhead: all concepts need to be formalized

3. Verbose proofs: machine proofs are more detailed than human proofs

Despite the challenges, machine verification is the gold standard of formalization.

§5.10.3 Unresolved Problems

Although this chapter has made important progress, several problems remain unresolved:

Theoretical problems:

1. The treatment of continuous time: PO uses discrete time (LTL), but physical time is continuous. It needs to be extended to continuous temporal logic or real analysis.

2. Probabilistic processes: PO assumes determinism, but quantum processes are probabilistic. It needs to be extended to probabilistic temporal logic.

3. Spatial structure: PO mainly handles time; its treatment of space is inadequate. It needs to be extended to spatiotemporal logic.

4. Subjective time: PO handles objective time, but the subjective experience of time (such as "the feeling of time passing") cannot be formalized. This may be an inherent limitation of formalization.

5. The identity of processes: how do we judge that the processes at two moments are "the same" process? This involves the identity conditions of processes and requires further study.

Technical problems:

1. The complete proof of Theorem 5.5: the theorem of the emergence of time requires more advanced mathematical tools (category theory, topology); at present there is only a proof sketch.

2. Model-theoretic analysis: the model-theoretic properties of PO (completeness, compactness, decidability) have not yet been fully studied.

3. Computational complexity: the computational complexity of the reasoning problems in PO is unknown. Reasoning in temporal logic is usually PSPACE-complete, but the extensions of PO may be more complex.

Philosophical problems:

1. The granularity of processes: what is the "smallest" process? Are there atomic processes?

2. The boundaries of processes: how do we demarcate the beginning and end of a process? Are the boundaries of a process objective or conventional?

3. The causality of processes: how is the causal relation between processes to be formalized? This requires extending PO to include causal operators.

4. Processes and consciousness: is conscious experience a process? If so, how is subjectivity to be formalized?

These problems will be explored in subsequent chapters and future research.

Chapter Endnotes

Note 1: The History of Temporal Logic

Temporal logic was founded by Arthur Prior in the 1950s. Prior was inspired by McTaggart's philosophy of time and sought to express time and change using formal logic. The earliest temporal logic was propositional, and was later extended to first-order, modal, branching, and other forms. In 1977, Amir Pnueli introduced temporal logic into computer science for program verification, and received the 1996 Turing Award.

Note 2: The Ontological Controversy over Quantum Field Theory

The ontological interpretation of quantum field theory has always been controversial. The main positions include:

  • Particle ontology: particles are basic (Teller, Dieks)
  • Field ontology: fields are basic (Auyang, Kuhlmann)
  • Event ontology: events are basic (Fleming)
  • Structural realism: mathematical structure is basic (Ladyman, Ross)

This chapter adopts field ontology, but interprets it as a special case of Process Ontology.

Note 3: Whitehead's Process Philosophy

Alfred North Whitehead (1861-1947) was the founder of process philosophy. His Process and Reality (1929) proposes:

  • Reality is composed of "actual occasions"
  • Actual occasions are processual, not substantial
  • "Entities" are abstracted from processes

Whitehead's thought is profound but obscure. The PO of this chapter can be seen as a modern formalization of Whitehead's thought.

Note 4: The A-Theory and B-Theory of Time

There are two major camps in the philosophy of time:

  • The A-theory (tensed theory): past, present, and future are objective, and time "flows"
  • The B-theory (tenseless theory): there is only the "earlier than" and "later than" relation, with no objective "now"

Formally, PO is closer to the B-theory (using temporal relations), but in its philosophical interpretation it can accommodate the A-theory (the "becoming" of processes).

Note 5: The Limitations of Machine Verification

Machine verification provides the highest level of rigor, but it also has limitations:

1. The formalization gap: the translation from natural language to a formal language may be erroneous

2. The correctness of the axioms: a machine can only verify "axioms → theorems"; it cannot verify whether the axioms themselves are correct

3. Philosophical interpretation: a machine cannot verify whether the philosophical interpretation of a formal system is appropriate

Therefore, machine verification is necessary but not sufficient. We still need human philosophical judgment.

All the code in this chapter is open source (MIT/Apache 2.0 license). This embodies the core values of this work:

  • Transparency: all proofs can be inspected
  • Revisability: anyone can improve them
  • Collaboration: scholarship is a common endeavor

We invite the global academic community to participate in this open research project.
