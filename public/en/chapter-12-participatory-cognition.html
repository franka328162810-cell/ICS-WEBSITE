Chapter 12: A Formal System for Participatory Cognition

Chapter Introduction

1. The Position of This Chapter in the Book

This chapter opens Part IV of Volume II, "The Formalization of the New View of Cognition," and undertakes the task of providing a rigorous formal foundation for participatory cognition. If Chapter 3 of Volume I provided the philosophical argument for the New View of Cognition, this chapter translates those philosophical insights into an operational formal system.

In the protocol stack architecture, this chapter is mainly concerned with:

  • The L₁ layer (life–cognition foundation): the ontological characteristics of cognition and their formal expression
  • The L₂ layer (normative–ethical foundation): how the view of cognition lays the groundwork for the subsequent formalization of ethics
  • The formalization layer: providing a rigorous mathematical reconstruction of the philosophical arguments of Volume I

The central task of this chapter is to construct the formal system PC (Participatory Cognition), which formally expresses the core propositions of the New View of Cognition:

  • NK-1 (embodiment): cognition is not computation inside the brain but is embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive (4E cognition)
  • NK-2 (participation): cognition is not passive representation but active participation in constituting the world
  • NK-3 (sociality): knowledge is the product of social negotiation, not individual discovery
  • NK-4 (revisability): knowledge has a versioned structure and can be revised and rolled back

2. Core Innovations of the Second Edition

Relative to the first edition (which did not include this volume), the core innovations of this chapter include:

Innovation 1: A Complete Connection from Philosophy to Formalization

Chapter 3 of Volume I provided the philosophical argument for the New View of Cognition but lacked a formal expression. By constructing the formal system PC, this chapter achieves a complete transformation from philosophy to mathematics.

Innovation 2: A Topological Expression of 4E Cognition

Traditional cognitive science treats cognition as symbol manipulation inside the brain. This chapter uses topological tools to formalize the cognition–environment boundary as a fuzzy boundary in a topological space, and rigorously proves the "cognitive boundary unlocatability" theorem.

Innovation 3: A Category-Theoretic Expression of Participation

The core claim of participatory cognition is that "the cognizer and the cognized object mutually constitute each other." This chapter uses adjoint functors from category theory to formalize this relation of mutual constitution and proves the "cognition–world adjunction theorem."

Innovation 4: A Formalization of Knowledge Versioning

This chapter is the first to apply version-control theory from software engineering to epistemology, constructing a versioned formal system of knowledge and proving the "knowledge rollback theorem."

Innovation 5: A Rigorous Correspondence with the Arguments of Volume I

Every formal theorem in this chapter corresponds explicitly to some philosophical argument in Chapter 3 of Volume I, achieving a two-way validation of philosophy and formalization.

§12.1 Construction of the Formal System PC

§12.1.1 Motivation and Strategy of Formalization

Why do we need to formalize participatory cognition?

Participatory Cognition is the core claim of the New View of Cognition, but in the philosophical argument of Volume I this claim relied mainly on:

1. Empirical evidence: experimental results from 4E cognitive science

2. Conceptual analysis: critical analysis of concepts such as "representation" and "computation"

3. Phenomenological description: direct description of cognitive experience

Although these argumentative approaches are powerful, they have the following limitations:

Limitation 1: Conceptual vagueness

Concepts such as "embodied," "embedded," "extended," "enactive," and "participatory" lack precise definitions in natural language. Different scholars may understand these concepts differently.

Limitation 2: Non-formality of the arguments

Philosophical arguments rely on intuitions and cases and are difficult to systematically test for consistency and completeness.

Limitation 3: Difficulty of comparison with traditional theories

Computationalist cognitive science has a mature formal framework (Turing machines, computational complexity theory, etc.), while participatory cognition lacks corresponding formal tools, making it difficult to compare the two at the same level.

Limitation 4: Insufficient guidance for application

Philosophical arguments are difficult to use to directly guide the design and evaluation of cognitive systems.

The goal of formalization

This chapter constructs the formal system PC, aiming to:

1. Make concepts precise: provide mathematical definitions for concepts such as "embodied," "embedded," "extended," "enactive," and "participatory"

2. Systematize arguments: transform philosophical arguments into formal theorems and provide rigorous proofs

3. Comparability: make participatory cognition and computationalism comparable at the formalized level

4. Operability: make formalization results capable of guiding the design of actual cognitive systems

The strategy of formalization

Strategy 1: Integration of multiple tools

Participatory cognition involves multiple dimensions (spatial, temporal, social, versioned), and a single mathematical tool can hardly express it completely. This chapter adopts the strategy of integrating multiple tools:

  • Topology: expressing the fuzziness of the cognition–environment boundary (embodiment, embeddedness, extension)
  • Dynamical systems theory: expressing the temporal evolution of cognition (enactiveness)
  • Category theory: expressing the mutual constitution of cognizer and world (participation)
  • Graph theory: expressing the structure of the epistemic community (sociality)
  • Version-control theory: expressing the revisability of knowledge

Strategy 2: Gradual formalization

This chapter adopts a gradual formalization strategy, from simple to complex:

1. First formalize the individual dimensions of 4E cognition (§12.2-12.5)

2. Then formalize participation (§12.6)

3. Then formalize sociality (§12.7)

4. Finally formalize revisability (§12.8)

5. Ultimately integrate them into a unified axiom system (§12.9)

Strategy 3: Two-way validation

Formalization and philosophical argument validate each other:

  • Forward: philosophical arguments guide the construction of the formalization
  • Backward: problems found in the formalization are fed back to the philosophical level

For example, during the formalization we found that "extension" and "embeddedness" are in tension under certain circumstances, and this finding was fed back to Volume I, prompting us to clarify the relation between these two concepts in the third edition.

Strategy 4: Machine verification

All core theorems are machine-verified using theorem provers (Coq/Isabelle/Lean) to ensure the rigor of the proofs.

§12.1.4 The Philosophical Motivation of Formalization

Before entering the concrete formalization, we need to clarify the philosophical motivation of formalization.

Why choose topology?

Traditional cognitive science treats cognitive systems as entities with clear boundaries (e.g., the brain). But 4E cognitive science shows that cognitive boundaries are fuzzy:

  • Is the body part of cognition?
  • Is the environment part of cognition?
  • Is a tool part of cognition?

Topology provides a natural tool for expressing fuzzy boundaries:

  • Open sets: parts that clearly belong to the interior of the cognitive system
  • Closed sets: the cognitive system including its boundary
  • Boundary: , belonging neither wholly to the interior nor wholly to the exterior

Using topology, we can rigorously express the intuition that "the cognitive boundary cannot be located."

Why choose dynamical systems theory?

Traditional cognitive science treats cognition as static symbol manipulation. But Enactive Cognition emphasizes that cognition is a dynamic process:

  • Cognition is not a state but the evolution of states
  • Cognition is not the retrieval of representations but continuous coupling with the environment

Dynamical systems theory provides a natural tool for expressing temporal evolution:

  • State space: the set of all possible cognitive states
  • Vector field: how states evolve over time
  • Attractor: the stable pattern toward which the system tends
  • Coupled dynamics: the mutual influence of the cognitive system and the environment

Using dynamical systems theory, we can rigorously express the intuition that "cognition is an enactive process."

Why choose category theory?

The core claim of participatory cognition is that the cognizer and the cognized object mutually constitute each other. This is not a one-way representational relation but a two-way constitutive relation.

Category theory provides a natural tool for expressing mutual constitution:

  • Adjoint Functors: denotes a special relation between two functors
  • If (the cognition functor) and (the constitution functor) form an adjunction, then they are mutually "optimal approximations"
  • The adjunction captures the two-way character of "the cognizer constitutes the world through cognition, and the world constitutes the cognizer through being cognized"

Using category theory, we can rigorously express the intuition of "participation."

Why choose graph theory?

The core claim of the sociality of knowledge is that knowledge is the product of the epistemic community. The epistemic community has a network structure:

  • Cognizers are nodes
  • Epistemic relations (communication, collaboration, criticism) are edges
  • Knowledge propagates and evolves in the network

Graph theory provides a natural tool for expressing network structure:

  • Graph:
  • Path: the path along which knowledge propagates
  • Connectivity: the degree of integration of the epistemic community
  • Centrality: the position of a cognizer in the community

Using graph theory, we can rigorously express the intuition of the "sociality of knowledge."

Why choose version-control theory?

The core claim of the revisability of knowledge is that knowledge has a versioned structure. This is highly analogous to version control in software engineering:

  • Knowledge has multiple versions
  • Versions have revision relations among them
  • One can roll back to an earlier version
  • One can merge different branches

Version-control theory (from software engineering) provides a natural tool for expressing versioned structure:

  • Version graph: a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
  • Commit: creating a new version
  • Rollback: returning to an old version
  • Merge: integrating different branches

Using version-control theory, we can rigorously express the intuition of the "revisability of knowledge."

§12.1.5 The Hierarchical Structure of Formalization

The formalization of PC is divided into three levels:

Level 1: Local formalization (§12.2-12.8)

Each dimension (embodiment, embeddedness, extension, enactiveness, participation, sociality, revisability) is formalized separately, using the mathematical tool best suited to that dimension.

Level 2: Integrated formalization (§12.9)

The individual dimensions are integrated into a unified axiom system, ensuring consistency among the dimensions.

Level 3: Metatheoretic analysis (§12.11)

The meta-properties of the whole formal system (consistency, expressive power, completeness) are analyzed.

This hierarchical structure ensures the modularity and extensibility of the formalization.

§12.2 Formalization of Embodied Cognition

§12.2.1 The Philosophical Background of Embodiment

Embodied Cognition is the first "E" of 4E cognitive science, and its core claim is:

Cognition is not abstract computation inside the brain but is deeply rooted in the physical structure of the body and in sensorimotor experience.

This claim challenges the brain-centrism of traditional cognitive science, which holds that:

  • Cognition = symbol manipulation in the brain
  • The body is merely an input/output device
  • Cognition can exist independently of the body (e.g., uploaded to a computer)

The empirical evidence for embodied cognition includes:

1. Sensorimotor coupling: cognitive tasks depend on specific bodily action patterns

2. Body schema: the spatial structure of the body affects the spatial representation of cognition

3. The bodily basis of emotion: emotional states are tightly coupled with bodily states

4. The metaphorical basis of concepts: abstract concepts are rooted in bodily experience (Lakoff & Johnson)

  • Accessibility: the system responds quickly and is easy to access
  • Automaticity: reduce the user's cognitive load
  • Endorsement: build user trust

Principle 2: Support the sensorimotor loop (based on Axiom A7)

Interaction should be a dynamic sensation–action loop rather than static information retrieval.

Concrete design:

  • Real-time feedback: user actions immediately produce sensory feedback
  • Continuous interaction: support a continuous flow of interaction rather than discrete query–response
  • Multimodality: integrate visual, auditory, tactile, and other sensory channels

Principle 3: Support participation (based on Theorem 12.5)

The user and the system mutually constitute each other, rather than the user one-way controlling the system.

Concrete design:

  • Adaptivity: the system adjusts itself according to user behavior
  • Co-evolution: the user and the system develop together
  • Two-way influence: the user influences the system, and the system influences the user

Participatory AI assistant:

§12.15.2 Limitations of Formalization

Although formalization brings new insights, it also has important limitations:

Limitation 1: The difficulty of formalizing empirical content

Some axioms of PC (such as A1-A8) have empirical content, and their truth values depend on empirical evidence.

Formalization can express these axioms but cannot prove them (since they are empirical propositions).

Example:

Axiom A2 (Fuzziness of the boundary) claims:

This is an empirical proposition that needs to be verified through experiments in neuroscience and cognitive science.

Formalization can explore the logical consequences of this axiom (such as Theorem 12.1) but cannot prove the axiom itself.

Philosophical significance:

Formalization cannot replace empirical research. It is a supplement to empirical research, not a substitute.

Limitation 2: The difficulty of formalizing the phenomenological dimension

Some dimensions of participatory cognition (especially the phenomenological dimension) are difficult to formalize.

Example:

Varela emphasizes the first-person experience of cognition.

But formalization is essentially third-person (objective mathematical structure).

How to capture first-person experience in formalization? This is an open question.

Possible directions:

  • Use modal logic to express the first-person perspective
  • Use a formalization of the phenomenological reduction (Husserl's epoché)
  • Acknowledge that some dimensions cannot be formalized and preserve the tension between philosophy and formalization

Limitation 3: The complexity of dynamics

PC uses dynamical systems theory to express the temporal evolution of cognition.

But actual cognitive dynamics are extremely complex (high-dimensional, nonlinear, stochastic) and difficult to formalize completely.

Example:

The human brain has about neurons, each with about connections.

A complete dynamical model would require a -dimensional state space.

This is not computable in practice.

Solutions:

  • Use dimensionality-reduction techniques (such as manifold learning)
  • Use coarse-graining
  • Focus on qualitative properties (such as the existence of attractors) rather than quantitative details

Limitation 4: The simplification of the social dimension in formalization

PC uses graph theory and game theory to express the sociality of knowledge.

But actual social epistemic processes involve complex factors such as power, ideology, and culture, which are difficult to formalize completely.

Example:

Epistemic injustice involves the influence of social power structures on knowledge production.

Centrality metrics from graph theory can partially capture this but cannot capture the subtle workings of power.

Solutions:

  • Acknowledge the simplifying nature of formalization
  • Treat formalization as a starting point rather than an endpoint
  • Combine insights from disciplines such as sociology and anthropology

§12.15.3 Feedback on the Arguments of Volume I

The formalization process reveals some places in the arguments of Volume I that need clarification:

Feedback 1: The relation between embodiment and extension

During the formalization, we found a potential tension between embodiment (Axioms A1-A3) and extension (Axiom A6).

Question:

If the cognitive system can extend to tools (Axiom A6), then where is the boundary of the "body"?

Does the extended system still count as "embodied"?

Clarification:

Embodiment emphasizes that cognition depends on some body (not necessarily a biological body).

Extension shows that the body can include tools.

Therefore, extension is an expansion of embodiment, not a contradiction.

Recommendation:

In the third edition of Volume I, the relation between embodiment and extension should be clearly clarified.

Feedback 2: The relation between enactiveness and representation

When formalizing Theorem 12.4, we found that the meaning of "non-representationality" needs a more precise definition.

Question:

Axiom A8 claims that cognition cannot be reduced to representation.

But does this mean that cognition has no representational component at all?

Or does it mean that representation is not central?

Clarification:

What enactive cognition rejects is representationalism, that is, the view that representation is the core and essence of cognition.

But enactive cognition does not deny that some form of "representation" (such as neural activation patterns) may be involved in cognitive processes.

Key difference:

  • Representationalism: representation precedes action
  • Enactive cognition: representation (if it exists) emerges from the sensorimotor loop

Recommendation:

In the third edition of Volume I, the meaning of "non-representationality" should be defined more precisely.

But does this mean:

  • (Strong interpretation) the cognizer's cognitive acts affect the ontological existence of the world?

Participatory cognition adopts the strong interpretation:

The cognizer not only affects the way the world presents itself but also participates in constituting the ontological existence of the world.

§12.15.4 The Relation between Formalization and Philosophy

Finally, we reflect on the relation between formalization and philosophy.

Formalization is not a substitute for philosophy

Formalization is a tool of philosophical argument, not a substitute for it.

Philosophy provides:

  • Conceptual analysis
  • Intuitive insights
  • Value judgments

Formalization provides:

  • Precision
  • Rigor
  • Systematization

The two are mutually complementary.

The value of formalization

1. Clarifying concepts: formalization forces us to define concepts precisely

2. Checking consistency: formalization reveals inconsistencies in arguments

3. Exploring consequences: formalization enables us to systematically explore the logical consequences of arguments

4. Interdisciplinary dialogue: formalization provides a common language for dialogue with disciplines such as mathematics and computer science

The dangers of formalization

1. Oversimplification: formalization may lose the subtlety of philosophical arguments

2. Technicism: formalization may lead to excessive attention to technical details while neglecting philosophical issues

3. Exclusivity: formalization may exclude philosophers unfamiliar with mathematics

The way of balance

The strategy adopted in this chapter:

1. Dual track: philosophical argument (Volume I) and formalization (Volume II) proceed in parallel

2. Mutual validation: philosophy guides formalization, and formalization feeds back to philosophy

3. Accessibility: provide multi-level reading paths (§12.0.5)
