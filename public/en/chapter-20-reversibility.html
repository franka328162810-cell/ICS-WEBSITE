§20.1 A Precise Formalization of the Concept of Reversibility

§20.1.1 Review and Diagnosis of the Volume One Argument

The Core Claim of Volume One

In Chapter 9 of Volume One, we proposed the Reversibility Principle (REV):

The Reversibility Principle (REV, Natural-Language Version):

Under conditions of deep uncertainty, when several courses of action are roughly equivalent in their known consequences, a rational agent should prefer the option with higher reversibility, so as to preserve the future capacity for error correction and the space of options.

The argument structure of Volume One is as follows:

Premise 1: We face deep uncertainty (NK-1)

Premise 2: Knowledge is revisable (NK-3)

Premise 3: Irreversible actions close off future options (a corollary of RFP)

Premise 4: Preserving the space of options has value (bridge principle BP-1)

────────────────────────────────────────

Conclusion: Reversible actions should be preferred (REV)

Diagnosis of the Gaps in the Argument

The First Edition argument contains the following elements that need strengthening:

Gap 1: The Vagueness of the Concept of Reversibility

When the First Edition used the term “reversibility,” it did not sufficiently distinguish different levels of reversibility:

  • Physical reversibility: Can the physical consequences of an action be reversed?
  • Institutional reversibility: Can the institutional consequences of an action be undone?
  • Cognitive reversibility: Does the action preserve room for learning and correction?

Critics have pointed out that these three kinds of reversibility may be mutually independent, or even mutually conflicting.

Gap 2: The Boundary of Deep Uncertainty Is Unclear

The First Edition proposed the concept of “deep uncertainty” (Deep Uncertainty) but did not give a precise definition:

  • How are “deep uncertainty” and “ordinary risk” to be distinguished?
  • Is deep uncertainty binary (present/absent) or a matter of degree?
  • What is the epistemological status of deep uncertainty?

Gap 3: The Difficulty of Judging Equivalence

REV requires that reversible options be preferred “when the known consequences are roughly equivalent.” But:

  • How are two options judged to be “roughly equivalent”?
  • What is the criterion of equivalence?
  • If one option is superior in known consequences but lower in reversibility, how is the trade-off to be made?

Gap 4: The Relation of REV to RFP and NRP Is Unclear

The First Edition argued for REV, RFP, and NRP, but the relations among the three were not fully clarified:

  • Is REV a corollary of RFP or an independent principle?
  • How is a conflict between REV and NRP to be coordinated?
  • Is there a deeper unifying principle?

§20.1.4 A Response to the “Complete Reversibility” Critique

Critique 1: Complete Reversibility Is Impossible

Content of the Critique:

The second law of thermodynamics shows that any real physical process increases entropy, so complete physical reversibility is impossible. Is REV's requirement to “prefer reversible actions” an unrealizable ideal?

Response:

REV does not require “complete reversibility,” but rather “among comparable options, prefer the option with higher reversibility.” This is a comparative principle, not an absolute one.

Formal Statement:

Even if the reversibility of all available options is low, REV remains meaningful: it requires us to choose the relatively more reversible option.

Critique 2: The Trade-off between Reversibility and Efficiency

Content of the Critique:

In many cases, an option with higher reversibility is less efficient. For example, reversible energy technologies (such as solar power) may cost more than irreversible technologies (such as nuclear power). Would REV lead to excessive conservatism?

Response:

§20.2.1 The Epistemological Basis of Deep Uncertainty

The Epistemological Ladder from Risk to Deep Uncertainty

In decision theory, we can distinguish four levels of uncertainty:

Level 1: Certainty

The agent knows completely the consequences of each action:

Level 2: Risk

The agent knows the possible consequences of each action and their probability distribution:

This is the domain of application of traditional expected utility theory.

Level 3: Knightian Uncertainty

The agent knows the possible consequences but does not know the probability distribution:

This is the classic distinction proposed by Frank Knight (1921).

Level 4: Deep Uncertainty

The agent not only does not know the probability distribution, but does not even know the complete set of possible consequences, or does not know how to set up a probability framework:

This is the core concept of this chapter.

The Five Sources of Deep Uncertainty

According to Chapter 3 of Volume One (the New View of Cognition) and Chapter 14 of Volume Two (the formal system of deep uncertainty), deep uncertainty has five sources:

Source 1: Radical Evidential Insufficiency

The available evidence is, in principle, insufficient to establish a probability distribution.

Example:

  • The first release of self-replicating nanotechnology
  • The first contact with an extraterrestrial civilization
  • The first creation of AGI

Source 2: Conceptual Framework Uncertainty

We are uncertain which conceptual framework should be used to describe the problem.

Example:

  • The nature of consciousness (physicalism vs. dualism vs. panpsychism)
  • The quantum measurement problem (Copenhagen vs. many-worlds vs. pilot wave)

Source 3: Radical System Evolution

The system itself is undergoing a radical evolution, and past regularities no longer apply.

Example:

  • The world after a technological singularity
  • An ecosystem crossing a tipping point
  • A paradigm shift in social institutions

Source 4: Observer Participation

The observer's own action changes the system, so that an “objective” probability cannot be defined.

Example:

  • Financial markets (observation and prediction change the market)
  • Social-science experiments (the Hawthorne effect)
  • Quantum measurement (the observer effect)

Source 5: Value Uncertainty

We are uncertain which value framework should be used to evaluate consequences.

Example:

  • Intergenerational justice (the weight of present vs. future people)
  • Cross-species ethics (the moral status of humans vs. animals vs. AI)

The Assumptions of Expected Utility Theory

Traditional expected utility theory (EUT) is based on the following assumptions:

Assumption 1: probabilities are definable

Assumption 2: utilities are quantifiable

Assumption 3: rationality is maximizing expected utility

Failure under Deep Uncertainty

Theorem 20.21 (The Inapplicability of EUT under Deep Uncertainty):

Under conditions of deep uncertainty, all three assumptions of expected utility theory may fail:

Proof:

By the definition of deep uncertainty (Definition 20.5), at least one of the following cases holds:

1. Probabilities are not definable (Assumption 1 fails):

  • If it is radical evidential insufficiency (ERI), then no probability distribution can be established
  • If it is radical system evolution (RSE), then past frequencies do not apply to the future

2. Utilities are not quantifiable (Assumption 2 fails):

  • If it is value uncertainty (VU), then it is not known which utility function should be used
  • If it is conceptual framework uncertainty (CFU), then it is not known how to define “consequences”

3. Maximization is not feasible (Assumption 3 fails):

  • If both probabilities and utilities are uncertain, then expected utility cannot be computed
  • Even if it can be computed, maximization may lead to overconfidence (epistemic arrogance)

Therefore, EUT does not apply under deep uncertainty. □

  • Requires the ability to assign prior probabilities
  • Fails under conceptual framework uncertainty (it is not known on which probability space to define the prior)

  • Excessively pessimistic, may lead to excessive conservatism
  • Ignores probability information (even when partially available)

  • Excessively optimistic, may lead to reckless risk-taking
  • Ignores risk

§20.10 Philosophical Reflection and New Insights

§20.10.1 New Insights Revealed by the Formalization

The formalization work of this chapter is not only a rigorousification of the intuitive argument of Volume One, but reveals five important new insights:

Insight 1: The Multi-Dimensional Nature of Reversibility

Volume One's intuition: reversibility is “whether an action can be undone.”

The formalization reveals: reversibility contains at least three independent dimensions (physical, institutional, cognitive), which are mutually independent but complementary.

Philosophical Significance:

This reveals that reversibility is not a single property, but a multi-dimensional cluster of concepts. Reversibility in different dimensions may conflict (for example, institutionally reversible but physically irreversible), which requires us to adopt a geometric mean rather than an arithmetic mean in assessment, ensuring that extremely low reversibility in any dimension is fully taken into account.

Practical Implications:

When designing reversibility mechanisms, one cannot focus on only one dimension. For example, the “kill switch” of an AI system provides institutional reversibility, but if the AI's knowledge has already spread, physical reversibility remains very low.

Insight 2: The Irreducibility of Deep Uncertainty

Volume One's intuition: deep uncertainty is “not knowing the probabilities.”

The formalization reveals: deep uncertainty cannot be reduced to risk by acquiring more information (Theorem 20.2); it is qualitatively different, not quantitatively different.

Philosophical Significance:

This challenges the universality of Bayesian subjective probability. Bayesians hold that any uncertainty can be represented by subjective probabilities. But the formalization shows that in certain cases (especially conceptual framework uncertainty and radical system evolution), the probability framework itself does not apply.

Epistemological Implications:

This supports a pluralistic epistemology: different types of uncertainty require different epistemological tools. Risk calls for probability theory, Knightian uncertainty for interval probabilities or fuzzy sets, and deep uncertainty for robust decision-making and the Reversibility Principle.

Insight 3: The Time Value of Reversibility

Volume One's intuition: reversibility is more important in long-term decisions.

The formalization reveals: the value of reversibility grows with time and with the square root of uncertainty (Theorem 20.23):

Philosophical Significance:

This provides a quantitative theory of the value of reversibility. Reversibility is not merely a qualitative “caution,” but has a precise economic value. This formula resembles the Black-Scholes formula for financial options, showing that reversibility can be regarded as a kind of “real option.”

Practical Implications:

In cost-benefit analysis, the value of reversibility should be quantified and included in the calculation. An option with higher reversibility, even if its direct cost is higher, may have a higher total value because of its option value.

Volume One's intuition: long causal chains carry higher risk.

The formalization reveals: the reversibility of an action sequence is the product of the reversibilities of the individual steps (Theorem 20.11):

Practical Implications:

§20.10.2 The Limits of Formalization

Although formalization brings new insights, we must acknowledge its limits:

Limitation 1: The Subjectivity of the Reversibility Measure

The measurement of the three dimensions of reversibility (physical, institutional, cognitive) involves subjective judgment:

  • How is the “distance of physical states” to be quantified?
  • How is the “cost of institutional change” to be quantified?
  • How is “learning value” to be quantified?

Formalization provides a framework, but the specific numerical values still require expert judgment and empirical data.

Limitation 2: The Arbitrariness of the Equivalence Threshold

REV's condition of application is that “actions are equivalent in their known consequences.” But the choice of the equivalence threshold is somewhat arbitrary.

Different thresholds lead to different decisions:

  • (strict equivalence): the scope of application of REV is narrower
  • (loose equivalence): the scope of application of REV is broader

This needs to be adjusted in practice according to the specific context.

Limitation 3: The Blurry Boundary of Deep Uncertainty

The boundary between deep uncertainty and Knightian uncertainty is not always clear. In actual decision-making, judging whether a situation is one of “deep uncertainty” may be controversial.

This means that the applicability of REV may itself be uncertain (meta-uncertainty).

Limitation 4: The Oversimplification of Formalization

Formalization necessarily involves simplification. For example:

  • The reversibility measure assumes additivity and comparability
  • The option value formula assumes a certain probability structure
  • The coordination algorithm assumes the quantifiability of priorities

These simplifications may not hold in certain situations.

§20.10.3 Open Questions

The formalization work of this chapter opens several open questions:

Open Question 1: A Dynamic Theory of Reversibility

The current reversibility measure is static (assessed at a certain moment). But reversibility itself is dynamic:

  • Reversibility decays over time (Lemmas 20.1, 20.2)
  • New technologies may increase reversibility
  • Social learning may change institutional reversibility

Question: how is a dynamic theory of reversibility to be constructed? How is REV to be applied in a dynamic environment?

Open Question 2: Collective Reversibility

The current formalization focuses mainly on the decisions of a single agent. But many important decisions are collective:

  • International climate agreements
  • Global AI governance
  • Intergenerational decisions

Question: how is reversibility in collective decision-making to be formalized? How are different agents' assessments of reversibility to be coordinated?

Open Question 3: The Tension between Reversibility and Innovation

High reversibility may inhibit innovation:

  • If the reversible option is always chosen, breakthrough innovations may be missed
  • Certain major advances (such as the Industrial Revolution) are irreversible

Question: how is a balance to be struck between REV and encouraging innovation? Is there an “optimal irreversibility”?

Open Question 4: Cross-Substrate Reversibility

In cross-substrate situations (for example, digital mind uploading), the concept of reversibility needs to be extended:

  • Is the transition from a biological substrate to a digital substrate reversible?
  • How is the reversibility of a cross-substrate transition to be assessed?

Question: how is the formalization of reversibility to be extended to cross-substrate situations?

Open Question 5: The Ethical Status of Reversibility

REV treats reversibility as an instrumental value (protecting future options). But does reversibility also have intrinsic value?

Question: does reversibility have a moral status independent of its consequences? If so, how is it to be expressed in the formal system?
