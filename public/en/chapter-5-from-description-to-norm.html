§5.1 Re-examining the “is-ought” problem

§5.1.1 The Classic Formulation of Hume’s Problem

I. Hume’s Original Statement

David Hume, in A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–1740), put forward the famous “is-ought” distinction. This distinction constitutes the core obstacle to deriving normative propositions from descriptive ones.

Hume’s original text (A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part I, Section I):

“In every system of morality which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, ’tis necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.”

Definition 5.1 (Hume’s Problem)

Hume’s Problem refers to the problem of deriving normative propositions (“ought” propositions) from descriptive propositions (“is” propositions). Hume points out that, logically, one cannot directly derive propositions that prescribe action from propositions that purely describe facts, unless an additional normative premise is introduced.

Formalized formulation:

Let be the set of descriptive propositions and the set of normative propositions.

Hume’s problem asserts:

That is: no descriptive proposition can by itself logically entail any normative proposition.

II. The Logical Structure of Hume’s Problem

Hume’s problem can be stated more precisely as the following logical argument:

Argument 5.1 (The logical structure of Hume’s problem)

Premise 1: A valid logical derivation cannot introduce into the conclusion a concept not present in the premises

Premise 2: Normative concepts (“ought,” “good,” “right,” etc.) and descriptive concepts

(“is,” “exists,” “occurs,” etc.) are concepts of different types

Premise 3: Purely descriptive premises contain only descriptive concepts

Conclusion: No normative conclusion can be validly derived from purely descriptive premises

Proposition 5.1 (The core claim of Hume’s problem)

The core claim of Hume’s problem is: there exists a logical gap from “is” to “ought” (logical gap). This gap cannot be crossed by purely logical derivation; it requires some additional bridge.

III. The Philosophical Significance of Hume’s Problem

Hume’s problem has far-reaching philosophical significance:

Table 5.1 The philosophical significance of Hume’s problem

Dimension of significanceSpecific content
Metaethical significanceChallenges the possibility of moral knowledge
Epistemological significanceDistinguishes factual judgments from value judgments
Methodological significanceRequires ethical arguments to make their normative premises explicit
Practical significanceWarns of the danger of deriving moral conclusions directly from scientific facts

Proposition 5.2 (The challenge Hume’s problem poses to this work)

Hume’s problem poses a direct challenge to this work: this work attempts to derive normative principles (normative guidance for action) from the Three New Views (a descriptive ontological-epistemological framework). If Hume’s problem holds, such a derivation is logically impossible.

§5.1.2 The Challenge of the Naturalistic Fallacy

I. Moore’s Argument from the Naturalistic Fallacy

G. E. Moore, in Principia Ethica (1903), introduced the concept of the “naturalistic fallacy,” further reinforcing Hume’s problem.

Definition 5.2 (Naturalistic Fallacy)

The Naturalistic Fallacy is the error of equating or defining an ethical concept such as “good” with some natural property (such as pleasure, evolutionary fitness, desire satisfaction, etc.). Moore holds that “good” is a simple, unanalyzable concept, and that any attempt to define “good” by natural properties commits the naturalistic fallacy.

II. The Open Question Argument

Moore uses the “Open Question Argument” to support the existence of the naturalistic fallacy.

Argument 5.2 (The Open Question Argument)

Premise 1: If “good” could be defined as some natural property N, then the question

“Is N good?” would be a closed question (its answer being analytically true)

Premise 2: But for any natural property N, “Is N good?” is an open question

(its answer is not analytically true; it requires substantive argument)

Conclusion: Therefore, “good” cannot be defined as any natural property

Illustrations:

  • “Is pleasure good?”—this is an open question and can be meaningfully denied
  • “Is evolutionary fitness good?”—this is an open question and can be meaningfully denied
  • “Is desire satisfaction good?”—this is an open question and can be meaningfully denied

Proposition 5.3 (The challenge the naturalistic fallacy poses to this work)

The naturalistic fallacy poses a challenge to this work: if this work attempts to derive normative conclusions from natural facts (such as negentropy flow, information integration, the space of possibilities), then it may commit the naturalistic fallacy.

The naturalistic fallacy is closely related to Hume’s problem, but the two are not identical.

Proposition 5.4 (The relation between the two problems)

Hume’s problem and the naturalistic fallacy are related but distinct problems. Hume’s problem concerns the validity of derivation; the naturalistic fallacy concerns the correctness of definition. Solving Hume’s problem does not require solving the naturalistic fallacy, and vice versa. This work deals mainly with Hume’s problem, providing a derivational path from description to norm through the bridge principles.

§5.1.3 This Work’s Response Strategy

I. The Overall Framework of the Response Strategy

This work adopts the following response strategy toward the “is-ought” problem:

Strategy 1: Acknowledge the logical gap

This work acknowledges that normative conclusions cannot be validly derived from purely descriptive premises. Hume’s problem is logically correct.

Strategy 2: Introduce bridge principles

This work introduces bridge principles (Bridge Principles) to connect description and norm. The bridge principles have a hybrid nature: they contain both descriptive components and normative components.

Strategy 3: Independently defend the bridge principles

The bridge principles are not derived from natural facts; rather, they are defended through independent arguments. The defense comes from several paths: the survival-conditions argument, the reflective-equilibrium argument, and the practical-reason argument.

§5.2.1 The Survival Rationality Principle

I. The Proposal of the Survival Rationality Principle

Definition 5.4 (The Survival Rationality Principle, BP-1)

The Survival Rationality Principle (Survival Rationality Principle, BP-1): a rational agent with a will to survive ought to maintain its survival conditions.

where denotes the “ought” operator.

II. The Argument for the Survival Rationality Principle

Argument 5.3 (The argument for the Survival Rationality Principle)

Premise P1: Rational agents have goals and preferences

Premise P2: The realization of any goal presupposes the survival of the agent

Premise P3: If X is a necessary condition for realizing Y, and the agent pursues Y, then the agent has reason to pursue X

Premise P4: Survival conditions are a necessary condition for the agent’s survival

Intermediate conclusion C1: The agent has reason to maintain its survival conditions (from P2, P3, P4)

Premise P5: If an agent has reason to do X, and X is feasible, then the agent ought to do X

Conclusion C2: The agent ought to maintain its survival conditions (from C1, P5)

Proposition 5.6 (The core claim of the Survival Rationality Principle)

The core claim of the Survival Rationality Principle is: survival is the precondition for a rational agent to realize any other goal. Therefore, maintaining survival conditions is a basic requirement of rationality. This does not mean that survival is the highest value, but that survival is a necessary condition for realizing other values.

IV. The Boundaries of the Survival Rationality Principle

Boundary Declaration 5.2 (The boundaries of the Survival Rationality Principle)

The Survival Rationality Principle has the following boundaries:

  • BP-1 does not claim that survival is the highest value; it claims only that survival is the precondition for realizing other values

§5.2.2 The Epistemic Humility Principle

I. The Proposal of the Epistemic Humility Principle

Definition 5.5 (The Epistemic Humility Principle, BP-2)

The Epistemic Humility Principle (Epistemic Humility Principle, BP-2): the fallibility of cognition requires that decisions preserve reversibility so that errors can be corrected.

Formalized formulation:

That is: if the agent’s cognition is fallible, then the agent’s decisions ought to be reversible, or they require special justification.

II. The Argument for the Epistemic Humility Principle

Argument 5.4 (The argument for the Epistemic Humility Principle)

Premise P1: The cognition of all finite cognitive agents is fallible

Premise P2: Fallible cognition may lead to erroneous decisions

Premise P3: Erroneous decisions may cause harm

Premise P4: If a decision is reversible, errors can be corrected and harm can be mitigated

Premise P5: If a decision is irreversible, errors cannot be corrected and harm cannot be mitigated

Intermediate conclusion C1: Reversible decisions cope better with the fallibility of cognition than irreversible decisions

Premise P6: Rational agents ought to adopt strategies that cope with their limitations

Conclusion C2: Rational agents ought to prefer reversible decisions, or provide special justification for irreversible decisions

Proposition 5.7 (The core claim of the Epistemic Humility Principle)

The core claim of the Epistemic Humility Principle is: the fallibility of cognition is a universal feature of finite cognitive agents. Faced with this fallibility, the rational response is to preserve the reversibility of decisions and leave room for correcting errors. Irreversible decisions require special justification, because they deprive us of the possibility of correcting errors.

§5.2.3 The Intersubjectivity Principle

I. The Proposal of the Intersubjectivity Principle

Definition 5.6 (The Intersubjectivity Principle, BP-3)

The Intersubjectivity Principle (Intersubjectivity Principle, BP-3): in multi-agent situations, an agent ought to adopt principles that can be reasonably accepted by other rational agents.

Formalized formulation:

That is: in multi-agent situations, an agent’s principles ought to be reasonably acceptable to other rational agents.

II. The Argument for the Intersubjectivity Principle

Argument 5.5 (The argument for the Intersubjectivity Principle)

Premise P1: Rational agents exist in multi-agent situations

Premise P2: In multi-agent situations, an agent’s actions affect other agents

Premise P3: If an agent’s principles cannot be reasonably accepted by other agents, conflict may result

Premise P4: Conflict is unfavorable to all agents

Premise P5: Principles that can be reasonably accepted by other agents are more likely to promote cooperation

Intermediate conclusion C1: Adopting principles that can be reasonably accepted by other agents is rational

Premise P6: Rational agents ought to adopt rational strategies

Conclusion C2: Rational agents ought to adopt principles that can be reasonably accepted by other rational agents

Proposition 5.8 (The core claim of the Intersubjectivity Principle)

The core claim of the Intersubjectivity Principle is: in multi-agent situations, the justificatory status of a principle depends not only on the agent’s own rationality but also on its acceptability to other rational agents. This is a requirement of “universalizability,” similar to Kant’s principle of universalization, but it places greater emphasis on actual intersubjective acceptability.

§5.3 The Justification of the Bridge Principles

§5.3.1 The Survival-Conditions Argument

I. The Structure of the Survival-Conditions Argument

The survival-conditions argument is the first path that provides justification for the bridge principles.

Argument 5.6 (The survival-conditions argument)

Premise P1: Rational agents have goals and preferences

Premise P2: The realization of any goal presupposes the survival of the agent

Premise P3: Survival requires specific conditions (survival conditions)

Premise P4: If X is a necessary condition for realizing Y, and the agent pursues Y, then the agent has reason to pursue X

Intermediate conclusion C1: The agent has reason to maintain its survival conditions

Premise P5: The bridge principles (BP-1, BP-2, BP-3) are necessary conditions for maintaining survival conditions

Conclusion C2: The agent has reason to accept the bridge principles

Proposition 5.10 (The core claim of the survival-conditions argument)

The core claim of the survival-conditions argument is: the bridge principles are necessary conditions for a rational agent to maintain its survival conditions. Therefore, accepting the bridge principles is rational.

II. A Detailed Development of the Survival-Conditions Argument

The survival-conditions argument for BP-1:

BP-1 (the Survival Rationality Principle) is directly about survival conditions. Accepting BP-1 is a direct requirement of maintaining survival conditions.

The survival-conditions argument for BP-2:

BP-2 (the Epistemic Humility Principle) is connected with survival conditions in the following ways:

  • Cognitive errors may lead to erroneous decisions
  • Erroneous decisions may threaten survival
  • Reversible decisions allow errors to be corrected and reduce threats to survival
  • Therefore, accepting BP-2 helps maintain survival conditions

The survival-conditions argument for BP-3:

BP-3 (the Intersubjectivity Principle) is connected with survival conditions in the following ways:

  • In multi-agent situations, conflict may threaten survival
  • Principles that can be accepted by other agents reduce conflict
  • Therefore, accepting BP-3 helps maintain survival conditions

III. The Boundaries of the Survival-Conditions Argument

Boundary Declaration 5.5 (The boundaries of the survival-conditions argument)

The survival-conditions argument has the following boundaries:

  • The survival-conditions argument is an argument from instrumental rationality, not an argument from intrinsic value
  • The survival-conditions argument assumes that agents have a will to survive
  • The survival-conditions argument does not exclude an agent sacrificing survival for a higher value
  • The survival-conditions argument needs to be combined with other argumentative paths

  • It acknowledges the connection between the normative and natural facts, but does not reduce the normative to natural facts
  • It connects description and norm through the bridge principles, rather than deriving norms directly from description
  • It adopts the methodology of multiple arguments, rather than a single foundationalism

§5.5.2 The Formalized Representation of the Derivation

I. The Derivation of the Recursive Freedom Principle

Derivation 5.1 (The derivation of the Recursive Freedom Principle)

Premise 1 (NC-1): Agents are nodes in a relational network

Premise 2 (NC-3): Agents participate in constructing a shared reality

Premise 3 (BP-3): Agents ought to adopt principles that can be reasonably accepted by other rational agents

Step 1: By NC-1, an agent’s existence depends on a relational network

Step 2: By NC-3, an agent’s actions affect other agents’ space of possibilities

Step 3: By BP-3, agents ought to adopt principles that can be accepted by other agents

Step 4: Actions that irreversibly compress others’ space of possibilities cannot be accepted by others

Step 5: Therefore, agents ought to avoid irreversibly compressing others’ space of possibilities

§5.5.3 Boundary Declarations for the Derivation

I. The Applicable Boundaries of the Derivation

Boundary Declaration 5.9 (The applicable boundaries of the derivation)

The derivation from the bridge principles to the normative principles has the following applicable boundaries:

  • (1) Agent boundary: the derivation applies to agents that have rationality, a will to survive, and cognitive capacity. For entities lacking these features, the derivation may not apply.
  • (2) Situational boundary: the derivation assumes multi-agent situations and the finiteness of resources. In single-agent situations or situations of infinite resources,

the derivation may need adjustment.

  • (3) Temporal boundary: the derivation focuses on long-range consequences but does not exclude short-term considerations. In emergencies, it may be necessary to weigh

long-range principles against short-term needs.

II. The Revisability of the Derivation

Boundary Declaration 5.10 (The revisability of the derivation)

The derivation from the bridge principles to the normative principles is revisable:

  • (1) Revisable premises: if the propositions of the Three New Views or the bridge principles are revised, the conclusions of the derivation must be revised accordingly.
  • (2) Revisable derivation: if a logical error in the derivation is discovered, the derivation must be corrected.
  • (3) Revisable conclusions: if the normative principles produce unacceptable consequences in application, the premises and the derivation must be checked retrospectively.

III. The Limitations of the Derivation

Boundary Declaration 5.11 (The limitations of the derivation)

The derivation from the bridge principles to the normative principles has the following limitations:

  • (1) Incompleteness: the derivation cannot resolve all normative problems; it provides only a basic normative framework.
  • (2) Abstractness: the conclusions of the derivation are abstract principles that require further specification before they can be applied.
  • (3) Possible conflicts: the three normative principles may conflict with one another, requiring further priority rules.

IV. The Relation between the Derivation and Experience

Proposition 5.22 (The relation between the derivation and experience)

The derivation from the bridge principles to the normative principles has the following relation to experience:

  • The descriptive premises of the derivation (the Three New Views) are compatible with the empirical sciences
  • The conclusions of the derivation (the normative principles) can be empirically tested as to their consequences
  • If the application of the normative principles produces unacceptable empirical consequences, the derivation must be checked retrospectively
