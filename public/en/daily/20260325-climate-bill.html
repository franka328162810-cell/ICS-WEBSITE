Climate Change

We Owe a Bill That No One Signed

Source: World Meteorological Organization (WMO) / Rapid Attribution Research, US Southwest Heat Wave | March 23–25, 2026

On March 23, 2026, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) released its State of the Global Climate 2025 report, confirming that global average temperatures in 2025 were approximately 1.43°C above the 1850–1900 pre-industrial baseline, breaking ocean heat records and making 2015–2025 the hottest 11 consecutive years in instrumental history. Concurrently, rapid attribution research on the March heat wave in the US Southwest found that human-driven climate change raised local temperatures by an extra 2.6–4°C, making such an event "virtually impossible" without anthropogenic influence. Two stories colliding in a single week. Every headline screamed "new record" or "hottest ever." But one question none of them asked: this is not an accident. It is a bill being systematically collected — and the signatories were never asked for consent.

Behind that number lies the time-lag effect of decades of carbon emissions: much of the CO₂ in today's atmosphere traces to decisions made generations ago, while today's emissions will drive climate impacts for generations to come. Those alive today are simultaneously paying down old debts and signing new ones for their descendants. 🔎 Dimension 2: Where Is the Reversibility in Our Commitments?

International climate commitment frameworks — the Paris Agreement, national NDC targets — exhibit fundamental structural weaknesses when viewed through the lens of CRV (Commitment Reversibility and Verifiability):

• Observability: Carbon emission data verification relies on national self-reporting; third-party independent verification coverage is inadequate.
• Enforceability: There are no binding penalty mechanisms; the logic of "voluntary reduction" presupposes that defection is cost-free.
• Reversibility: Near zero — no existing technology can remove already-emitted carbon on policy-relevant timescales.

The global climate governance architecture therefore has extremely low CRV, with the V_rev (reversibility) component approaching zero. When a commitment system's CRV is this low, its capacity to persuade actors that rules are real — and thus worth accepting short-term costs — will erode continuously.

📉 Dimension 3: Behind 11 Consecutive Records, the CDI Is Declining

The core question evaluated by the Civilization Development Index (CDI) is: is this civilization sustaining its negentropy conditions in a viable way? The CDI spans five dimensions: energy efficiency, ecological impact, knowledge accumulation, institutional resilience, and agent diversity.

From the WMO report: the energy dimension shows notable progress in renewables; but the ecological dimension — ocean heat, glacier mass, extreme weather frequency — is deteriorating across the board; and the institutional resilience dimension is most alarming, with international coordination capacity on climate emergencies declining rather than rising. Overall, the current global civilization's CDI is estimated at 0.35–0.5, with partial gains in energy being offset by declining ecology and institutions.
