We Dumped Garbage Into Space and No One Has to Clean It Up

March 16, 2026

There are roughly 1.4 million fragments larger than one centimeter orbiting Earth right now. Each one travels at about 27,000 kilometers per hour — fast enough that a fleck of paint can crack a window, and a bolt can destroy a satellite. Nobody owns them. Nobody is responsible for removing them. And the treaty that governs outer space was written in 1967, when the only actors in orbit were two superpowers and the phrase "space debris" had not yet been invented.

ICS Quick View: Outer space is the clearest existing case of a commons that was opened before anyone wrote the rules for closing it. Under the Negentropic Responsibility Principle (NRP), the entropy accumulating in low Earth orbit is a debt transferred to generations who had no part in contracting it.

Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty makes states responsible for the activities of their private companies. But it defines no enforcement mechanism, sets no deadline, and — critically — says nothing about cleanup. Article IX asks states to avoid "harmful contamination," a term left entirely undefined. Six decades later, the gap between the treaty's ambition and its mechanics is no longer theoretical. It is measured in collision-avoidance maneuvers and in the insurance premiums satellite operators now pay for risks they cannot control.

The Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP) asks what today's choices do to the space of possible futures. Every debris fragment crowds the orbital envelope that future launch capacity depends on. The Kessler cascade — a runaway chain of collisions that could render entire orbital bands unusable — is not a hypothetical. It is a threshold being approached at a pace nobody is measuring against a shared baseline.

The Reversibility Principle (REV) makes the diagnosis sharper. Debris removal is technically conceivable but economically and legally unresolved: who pays, who authorizes, who is liable when the wrong object is captured? Once orbital bands become unusable, no amount of later funding restores them on a human timescale.

Under the Negentropic Responsibility Principle (NRP), the question is not whether space is valuable — it plainly is. The question is who bears the entropy cost. Today's launch operators capture the benefit; tomorrow's generations inherit a contested, cluttered orbital environment. That asymmetry is the definition of intergenerational injustice.

For policymakers and the international legal community: the definition of "harmful contamination" cannot remain deferred, and a binding debris-mitigation and cleanup framework must be established before the first irrecoverable collision. The UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) has the mandate. What it lacks is a deadline.

Core recommendation: begin treating orbital capacity as a finite, auditable resource while it still is one. We have made the mistake of writing rules only after the technology proliferated — with the internet, with AI governance. On space debris, we still have time to avoid repeating it.
