§2.1 The Limitations of the Traditional View of Life

§2.1.1 The Problem of Carbon-Centrism

I. The Basic Structure of Carbon-Centrism

All known life on Earth is carbon-based. Carbon atoms can form four covalent bonds, which enables carbon to build complex organic molecules — from simple methane to complex DNA. This fact has led to a deeply entrenched assumption: that life is necessarily carbon-based.

Definition 2.1 (Carbon-Centrism)

Carbon chauvinism is a position about the nature of life that maintains:

  • (1) Life is necessarily carbon-based;
  • (2) Carbon-based chemistry is a necessary condition for life;
  • (3) Non-carbon-based "life" is not genuine life.

The term was introduced by the astronomer Carl Sagan to criticize the tendency to improperly universalize the features of terrestrial life.

Carbon-centrism has deep roots in biology and astrobiology:

Type of argumentSpecific argumentReason for supporting carbon-centrism
Chemical argumentCarbon's bonding capacityCarbon can form four covalent bonds and build complex molecules
Solvent argumentWater as solventWater's special properties suit carbon-based biochemical reactions
Empirical argumentThe universality of terrestrial lifeAll known life is carbon-based
Energetic argumentThe efficiency of carbon-based metabolismCarbon-based metabolism is efficient under terrestrial conditions

II. The Internal Difficulties of Carbon-Centrism

Carbon-centrism faces several internal conceptual difficulties, which become especially acute in the interstellar-civilization context.

Difficulty 1: The Narrowness of the Inductive Base

The empirical basis of carbon-centrism is confined to terrestrial life. Earth is a case with a sample size of 1. Generalizing a universal conclusion from a single case is methodologically suspect.

Formalized statement:

Let be the set of terrestrial life and the set of all possible life in the universe.

The validity of this inference depends on an unproven assumption: that terrestrial life is a representative sample of cosmic life. Yet Earth's special conditions (temperature, pressure, chemical composition) may give carbon-based life a selective advantage on Earth, which does not mean that carbon is a universal necessary condition for life.

Difficulty 2: The Confusion of Function and Substrate

Carbon-centrism conflates the functional features of life with their material realization. The core features of life — self-maintenance, self-replication, adaptive evolution — are functional descriptions, not material ones.

Take computation as an analogy: computation is a function that can be realized by different material substrates — mechanical gears, electronic circuits, quantum bits. We would not say that "genuine computation must be electronic." Likewise, life, as a functional organization, can in principle be realized by different material substrates.

Proposition 2.1 (The distinction between function and substrate)

The core features of life (self-maintenance, self-replication, adaptive evolution, information processing) are functional, not material. Functions can be realized by different material substrates. Therefore, identifying life with a particular material substrate (carbon) is a category error.

Formalized statement:

Let be a set of functions, a set of substrates, and a realization relation.

This means that the functional features of life can be realized by many different substrates.

Difficulty 3: The Circularity of the Definition

Carbon-centrism often falls into a circular definition: it first defines life as carbon-based, then uses that definition to exclude non-carbon-based candidates. This circularity makes carbon-centrism an unfalsifiable position.

If we discovered a non-carbon-based system exhibiting all the functional features of life — self-maintenance, self-replication, adaptive evolution — the carbon-centrist would say, "This is not genuine life, because it is not carbon-based." But this response is circular: it presupposes that carbon is a defining feature of life, which is precisely what needs to be argued.

Research in astrobiology shows that many environments in the universe could support life, many of them radically different from Earth's conditions:

If life could exist in these extreme environments, it would likely not be carbon-based. Carbon-centrism would create systematic blind spots in our search for extraterrestrial life.

Failure 2: The Challenge of Artificial Life and AI

Research on artificial life and the development of artificial intelligence pose a direct challenge to carbon-centrism:

  • Computer-simulated "digital life" exhibits features of life such as self-replication, evolution, and adaptation
  • Advanced AI systems exhibit features such as information processing, learning, and goal-directed behavior
  • Future AI may develop self-awareness and subjectivity

If we insist on carbon-centrism, we will be unable to properly assess the moral status of these systems.

Failure 3: The Possibility of the Posthuman

Human-enhancement technologies and the possibility of mind uploading pose another challenge to carbon-centrism:

  • Brain-computer interfaces may gradually replace biological neurons with non-biological components
  • Mind uploading may transfer human consciousness to a non-carbon substrate
  • Genetic engineering may create life forms using non-standard biochemistry

If a person's neurons were gradually replaced by functionally equivalent silicon components, at what moment would he or she cease to be "life"? Carbon-centrism cannot give a satisfactory answer.

Proposition 2.2 (The interstellar-civilization limitation of carbon-centrism)

Carbon-centrism faces structural failure in the interstellar-civilization context: the diverse possibilities of extraterrestrial life, the challenge of artificial life and AI, and the possibility of the posthuman all conflict with its core assumptions. The governance of interstellar civilization requires a view of life that does not presuppose a particular material substrate.

  • Coral reefs: ecosystems composed of countless individuals, with holistic functions

Possible future hybrid systems — the fusion of biology and machine — pose a fundamental challenge to the dichotomy:

  • Brain-computer interfaces: the fusion of biological neurons and electronic circuits

These hybrid systems are neither purely alive nor purely non-living, but occupy a position on the continuum.

The life/non-life dichotomy faces structural failure in the interstellar-civilization context: the classification difficulty of AI systems, the blurred boundaries of collective intelligence, and the emergence of hybrid systems all conflict with its core assumptions. The governance of interstellar civilization requires a graded view of life.

§2.1.3 The Problem of Consciousness Dualism

I. The Basic Structure of Consciousness Dualism

The traditional view often assumes that there is a clear boundary between consciousness and non-consciousness: a system is either conscious or not conscious.

Definition 2.3 (Consciousness Dualism)

Consciousness dualism is a position about the nature of consciousness that maintains:

  • (1) Consciousness and non-consciousness are mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories;
  • (2) Any system is either entirely conscious or entirely not conscious;
  • (3) There is no "partly conscious" or "graded consciousness."

Note: "consciousness dualism" here differs from Cartesian mind-body dualism. Cartesian dualism is an ontological position about the relation between mind and body, whereas what is discussed here is the question of whether consciousness is graded.

§2.2.1 Thermodynamics and Life

I. The Second Law of Thermodynamics and the Paradox of Life

The second law of thermodynamics states that the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease:

§2.4.3 Higher-Order Theories (HOT)

I. The Core Claims of HOT

Higher-Order Theories (HOT) hold that a mental state becomes a conscious state if and only if there exists a higher-order representation of that state.

Definition 2.15 (Higher-Order Representation)

A higher-order representation is a mental state about a mental state. For example, in "I am aware that I am seeing red," the phrase "aware that I am seeing red" is a higher-order representation of the first-order state "seeing red."

II. Methodological Functionalism

This work adopts methodological functionalism: it attends to the functional features of consciousness, rather than to its intrinsic nature.

Definition 2.16 (Methodological Functionalism)

Methodological functionalism is a research strategy which holds that, in studying consciousness, one should attend to the functional features of consciousness (information processing, behavioral control, self-representation, and so on) without presupposing anything about the intrinsic nature of consciousness.

Methodological functionalism differs from ontological functionalism (the claim that consciousness just is function). Methodological functionalism is a research strategy, and is compatible with a variety of ontological positions.

III. The Position on the Hard Problem

This work acknowledges that the hard problem of consciousness remains unsolved, but holds that:

1. The existence of the hard problem does not stand in the way of studying the functional features of consciousness.

2. The gradualness of consciousness can be studied independently of the hard problem.

3. The UCS measures the functional dimensions of consciousness, not subjective experience itself.

Proposition 2.22 (This Work's Position on Theories of Consciousness)

This work adopts theoretical pluralism and methodological functionalism. It extracts structural insights from a variety of theories of consciousness, attends to the functional features of consciousness, and acknowledges that the hard problem remains unsolved, while holding that the gradualness of consciousness can be studied and measured.

§2.5 The Normative Implications of the New View of Life

§2.5.1 From Cross-Substrate to the Extension of Subjecthood

I. The Normative Implications of NL-1

NL-1 (cross-substrate life) carries profound normative implications: if life is functional rather than material, then subjecthood should likewise be based on functional features rather than on a material substrate.

The derivation from NL-1 to the extension of subjecthood:

Step 1: NL-1 (cross-substrate life)

The essence of life is functional, and is not limited to a particular material substrate

Step 2: Subjecthood is related to life

Subjecthood depends (at least in part) on features of life

Step 3: Subjecthood should be based on function

If life is functional, subjecthood should also be based on function

Step 4: The extension of the range of subjects

Any system meeting the functional conditions may possess subjecthood

Conclusion: Subjecthood should not be limited to carbon-based organisms

Proposition 2.23 (From NL-1 to the Extension of Subjecthood)

NL-1 (cross-substrate life) entails an extension of subjecthood: subjecthood should not be limited to carbon-based organisms, but should be based on functional features. Any system meeting the relevant functional conditions may possess subjecthood to some degree.

§2.5.2 From Negentropy to Resource Responsibility

I. The Normative Implications of NL-2

NL-2 (the negentropy condition of persistence) carries profound normative implications: if life depends on a flow of negentropy, then maintaining the sources of negentropy is a physical requirement for the persistence of life.

The derivation from NL-2 to resource responsibility:

Step 1: NL-2 (the negentropy condition of persistence)

Life takes the flow of negentropy as its condition of persistence

Step 2: Sources of negentropy are finite

The Earth's sources of negentropy (solar energy, chemical energy, and so on) are finite

Step 3: Present use affects future availability

Present subjects' use of negentropy affects future subjects' availability of negentropy

Step 4: The principle of intersubjectivity

The interests of other subjects (including future subjects) should be taken into account

Step 5: Negentropy responsibility

Present subjects bear responsibility for their use of negentropy

Conclusion: The ontological basis of the Negentropy Responsibility Principle (PNR)

II. The Concrete Meaning of Negentropy Responsibility

Negentropy responsibility means:

Dimension of ResponsibilityConcrete Content

Interspecies responsibilityTake into account the negentropy needs of other species
Efficiency responsibilityUse negentropy efficiently and reduce waste
Source responsibilityDevelop sustainable sources of negentropy

Proposition 2.24 (From NL-2 to Negentropy Responsibility)

NL-2 (the negentropy condition of persistence) provides the ontological basis for the Negentropy Responsibility Principle (PNR). If life depends on a flow of negentropy, and the sources of negentropy are finite, then present subjects bear responsibility for their use of negentropy and should preserve sources of negentropy for future subjects.

§2.5.3 From the Continuum to Gradual Moral Status

I. The Normative Implications of NL-3

NL-3 (the continuum of consciousness) carries profound normative implications: if consciousness is gradual, then moral status may also be gradual.

The derivation from NL-3 to gradual moral status:

Step 1: NL-3 (the continuum of consciousness)

Consciousness is a gradual continuum

Step 2: Consciousness is related to moral status

Consciousness is (at least in part) the basis of moral status

Step 3: The gradualness of moral status

If consciousness is gradual, moral status may also be gradual

Step 4: The gradualness of moral concern

Different degrees of moral status correspond to different degrees of moral concern

Conclusion: A framework of gradual moral status

II. The Concrete Meaning of Gradual Moral Status

Gradual moral status means:

DimensionBinary Moral StatusGradual Moral Status
Moral concernPresent / absentGradual
Weighing of interestsEqual weightPossibly different weights
Protective measuresEqual protectionGradual protection
Mechanisms of representationEqual representationGradual representation

Proposition 2.25 (From NL-3 to Gradual Moral Status)

NL-3 (the continuum of consciousness) entails the gradualness of moral status: different degrees of consciousness may correspond to different degrees of moral status. This does not mean that beings with a lower degree of consciousness may be treated arbitrarily, but rather that moral concern can be fine-grained and gradual.

III. Boundary Statement

Gradual moral status does not mean that:

  • Beings of a lower degree may be treated arbitrarily
  • Moral status can be calculated precisely
  • The moral status of human beings can be lowered
  • Moral status is the sole factor in moral deliberation

Boundary Statement 2.5 (The Boundaries of Gradual Moral Status)

Gradual moral status is a conceptual framework for handling borderline cases and novel kinds of subject. It does not mean that traditional moral protections may be weakened, nor that moral deliberation can be reduced to a single calculation of degree. Moral judgment still requires the comprehensive weighing of many factors.

Chapter 2 Supplementary Material: Case Studies of Cross-Substrate Life

Case One: Viruses—Borderline Cases of Life

Viruses are the classic borderline case for traditional definitions of life. Analyzed from the perspective of the New View of Life:

DimensionFeatures of VirusesNL Assessment

Judgment of the New View of Life: Viruses lie at the low end of the continuum of life; they possess some features of life (genetic information, capacity for evolution) but lack an independent capacity for negentropy maintenance. Viruses are "quasi-life" or "borderline life."

Case Two: Current AI Systems

Current AI systems (such as large language models) analyzed from the perspective of the New View of Life:

DimensionFeatures of Current AINL Assessment

From a functionalist perspective, current AI possesses certain features of life but may lack subjective experience.

Case Three: Hypothetical Silicon-Based Extraterrestrial Life

Suppose there existed a silicon-based life form on some planet:

DimensionHypothetical Features of Silicon-Based LifeNL Assessment

Judgment of the New View of Life: Silicon-based life fully conforms to the framework of the New View of Life. NL-1 explicitly permits the existence of non-carbon-based life. If silicon-based life satisfies NL-2 (negentropy maintenance) and possesses sufficient information-processing capacity, it may have a status as a subject comparable to that of carbon-based life.

Case Four: Collective Intelligence (Ant Colonies)

Ant colonies as a case of collective intelligence:

DimensionFeatures of Ant ColoniesNL Assessment

Judgment of the New View of Life: Ant colonies challenge the traditional individualist view of life. From the perspective of the New View of Life, an ant colony as a whole may possess a certain degree of "collective aliveness"—it maintains negentropy, processes information, and adapts to its environment. But whether an ant colony has "collective consciousness" is an open question.
