Chapter 8: A Formal System for Cross-Substrate Life

Chapter Introduction

This chapter constructs the formal system MS (Multi-Substrate Life System), providing a rigorous formal foundation for the concept of "cross-substrate life" proposed in Chapter 2 of Volume 1. This is the first step in the formalization of the New View of Life, and also a key bridge connecting the New Cosmology (the NC system) with the normative framework.

The core tasks of this chapter:

1. To transform the philosophical claim that "life does not depend on a specific material substrate" into a provable mathematical theorem

2. To formalize the concept of "functional equivalence" so that it escapes circular definition

3. To prove the necessary and sufficient conditions for cross-substrate identity

4. To provide a theoretical foundation for the cross-substrate application of the UCS metric

The innovative contributions of this chapter:

  • Theorem 8.3 (Cross-Substrate Identity Theorem): for the first time rigorously proves under what conditions systems on two different substrates can be regarded as "the same life"
  • Theorem 8.5 (Multiple Realizability of Functional Realization): proves that any functional specification has infinitely many physical implementations
  • Theorem 8.7 (Undecidability of the Life-Detection Problem): proves that in the general case "deciding whether a system is alive" is an undecidable problem

Reading suggestions:

  • Prerequisites: Chapter 7 (the NC system), Chapter 2 of Volume 1
  • Technical difficulty: ★★★★☆ (requires foundations in category theory and topology)
  • Core theorems: 8.3, 8.5, 8.7
  • Skippable parts: the machine-verification details of §8.8 (on a first reading)

§8.1 Construction of the Formal System MS

§8.1.1 Motivation and Strategy of Formalization

The Philosophical Claim of Volume 1

Chapter 2 of Volume 1 proposed the first core claim of the New View of Life (NL-1):

NL-1 (Cross-Substrate Nature): The essence of life lies in functional organization, not in a specific material substrate. Carbon-based life, silicon-based life, and quantum-computer life are all possible in principle.

The philosophical challenges facing this claim:

1. Risk of circular definition: if "function" is defined by "life," and "life" is in turn defined by "function," then we fall into circularity

2. The precise meaning of multiple realizability: what is meant by a "different implementation" of "the same function"?

3. Criterion of identity: is a carbon-based human and its silicon-based "uploaded version" the "same individual"?

Formalization Strategy

Our strategy is:

1. Topologization of substrate space: construct all possible material substrates as a topological space

2. Category-theoretic formulation of function: understand function as a functor between categories

3. Fiber-bundle theory of identity: understand cross-substrate identity as a section of a fiber bundle

The advantages of this strategy:

  • Avoids circular definition (function is defined independently by mathematical structure)
  • Precisely characterizes multiple realizability (different natural transformations of the same functor)
  • Provides a computable criterion of identity

Unresolved Problems

Although MS has made important progress, the following problems remain open:

1. The ultimate definition of function

MS defines function by the input-output-behavior triple, but is this the most fundamental definition? Can the concept of function be derived from more basic physical or mathematical principles?

2. Determination of the life threshold

What is the concrete value of ? How can it be calibrated through empirical data?

3. The status of consciousness

Is consciousness a function? If so, how is it to be formalized in MS? If not, how must MS be extended?

4. The source of normativity

MS describes "what is" (functions, substrates, systems), but how does one derive "what ought to be" (normativity)? This requires the bridge principle of Chapter 17.

5. Quantum life

MS mainly deals with classical systems. How can it be extended to life forms that are essentially quantum (such as quantum-computer life)?

(c) Why is the life-detection problem undecidable? What implications does this have for the definition of life?

2. Applying the theorems

(a) Use Theorem 8.5 to analyze the following scenario: a person's brain is gradually replaced by nanorobots, 1% of the neurons at a time. Under what conditions is the person after replacement "the same individual" as the original person?

(b) Use Theorem 8.4 to argue: if extraterrestrial life exists, it is unlikely to be carbon-based.

(c) Use Theorem 8.8 to explain: why can the intelligence of a brain not be simply enhanced by increasing the number of neurons?

3. Critical thinking

(a) MS assumes that function can be defined independently of substrate. Is this assumption reasonable? Can a counterexample be constructed?

(b) MS uses Kolmogorov complexity to define functional complexity. What problems does this choice have? Are there better alternatives?

(c) MS claims that identity is the existence of a continuous path. But if there are multiple distinct paths connecting and , does identity still hold?

4. Extended research

(a) Design an experiment to test the prediction of MS: functionally equivalent systems are indistinguishable under all tests (Theorem 8.6).

(b) Try to extend MS to quantum systems. Which definitions and theorems need to be modified?

(c) Explore the relation between MS and information theory. Can function be defined using concepts from information theory (such as mutual information or channel capacity)?

5. Philosophical reflection

(a) Is MS reductionist or anti-reductionist? Argue for your position.

(b) What answer does MS give to the question "what is life"? Is this answer satisfactory?

(c) Does the formalization of MS really reveal new insights, or does it merely restate known philosophical views in mathematical language?
