II. From Survival Rationality to Negentropy Maintenance

Combining NL-2 (negentropy persistence) and BP-1 (the principle of survival rationality), it can be derived that agents have reason to maintain the sources of negentropy.

Proposition 12.6 (survival rationality implies negentropy maintenance)

From BP-1 (the principle of survival rationality) and NL-2 (negentropy persistence), a rational agent with a will to survive ought to maintain its sources of negentropy.

Argument 12.1 (the argument that survival rationality implies negentropy maintenance)

Premise P1 (BP-1): a rational agent with a will to survive ought to maintain its conditions of survival

Premise P2 (NL-2): the persistence of life depends on a continuous flow of negentropy

Premise P: the flow of negentropy depends on the availability of negentropy sources

Premise P4: if X is a necessary condition of Y, and the agent ought to maintain Y, then the agent ought to maintain X

Step 1: from P2, the flow of negentropy is a necessary condition of survival

Step 2: from P, the negentropy source is a necessary condition of the flow of negentropy

Step : from Step 1 and Step 2, the negentropy source is a necessary condition of survival

Step 4: from P1 and P4, the agent ought to maintain the negentropy source

Conclusion: a rational agent with a will to survive ought to maintain its sources of negentropy

III. From Intersubjectivity to Negentropy Responsibility

So far we have argued that agents have reason to maintain their own sources of negentropy. But the Negentropy Responsibility Principle requires a broader responsibility—not only maintaining one's own sources of negentropy, but also not harming the sources of negentropy of others, and not externalizing entropy costs.

Proposition 12.7 (the externality of negentropy consumption)

  • In a shared environment, one agent's consumption of negentropy may produce negative externalities:
  • reducing the negentropy available to other agents
  • increasing the entropy of the shared environment (pollution)

Formalized statement:

Proposition 12.8 (the unacceptability of externalization)

From BP- (the principle of intersubjectivity), a principle of action that externalizes entropy costs onto others cannot be reasonably accepted by others.

Argument 12.2 (the argument for the unacceptability of externalization)

Premise P1 (BP-): agents ought to adopt principles that can be reasonably accepted by others

Premise P2: externalizing entropy costs onto others harms their conditions of survival

Premise P: a principle of action that harms others' conditions of survival cannot be reasonably accepted by others

Conclusion: a principle of action that externalizes entropy costs onto others cannot be reasonably accepted by others

Proposition 12.9 (the intersubjective basis of negentropy responsibility)

From BP- (the principle of intersubjectivity), agents ought not only to maintain their own sources of negentropy but also to:

  • not over-consume shared negentropy resources
  • not externalize entropy costs onto others
  • contribute to the sustainability of shared negentropy resources

Argument 12. (the intersubjective argument for negentropy responsibility)

Premise P1 (Proposition 12.6): agents ought to maintain their own sources of negentropy

Premise P2 (BP-): agents ought to adopt principles that can be reasonably accepted by others

Premise P (Proposition 12.8): a principle that externalizes entropy costs cannot be reasonably accepted by others

Premise P4: a principle that maintains shared negentropy resources can be reasonably accepted by others

Step 1: from P1, agents have reason to maintain sources of negentropy

Step 2: from P2 and P, agents ought not to externalize entropy costs

Step : from P2 and P4, agents ought to maintain shared negentropy resources

Conclusion: agents ought to maintain sources of negentropy, not externalize entropy costs, and maintain shared negentropy resources

  • Role responsibility: because the agent occupies a certain role, the agent is responsible for the duties of that role

  • Benefit responsibility: because the agent benefits from something, the agent is responsible for that thing

  • Benefit responsibility: the agent benefits from negentropy consumption, and is therefore responsible for the negentropy source

Feature 2: the integration of multi-factor allocation

Traditional theories of responsibility usually focus on a single factor (causation, role, or capability). The Negentropy Responsibility Principle integrates multiple factors (consumption, capability, benefit) and provides a more comprehensive framework for allocating responsibility.

Feature 3: emphasis on the intergenerational dimension

Traditional theories of responsibility mainly focus on responsibility among present-day agents. The Negentropy Responsibility Principle emphasizes the intergenerational dimension and makes explicit the responsibility of the present generation toward future generations.

Feature 4: the connection to the conditions of survival

The Negentropy Responsibility Principle connects responsibility to the conditions of survival. This makes responsibility not an abstract moral requirement but a practical requirement directly related to the persistence of life.

Feature 5: the enhancement of operability

The Negentropy Responsibility Principle achieves operationalizability through the concept of negentropy. Negentropy can be measured (at least approximately), and responsibility can be allocated quantitatively.

§12.5.2 Possible Exceptions

I. The General Framework of Exceptional Cases

Definition 12.7 (exceptional cases)

Exceptional cases are cases in which the binding force of the Negentropy Responsibility Principle may be temporarily relaxed or waived. Exceptional cases need to satisfy specific conditions.

The general framework of exceptional conditions:

1. Urgency: is the situation urgent?

2. Necessity: is the exception necessary?

3. Proportionality: is the degree of the exception proportional to the urgency?

4. Temporariness: is the exception temporary?

II. Specific Exceptional Cases

Exceptional case 1: emergency survival

In cases of emergency survival, agents may temporarily relax the binding force of the Negentropy Responsibility Principle.

Case 12. (the emergency survival exception)

Situation: a community faces famine, and the only source of food is overfishing an endangered fish species.

Analysis:

  • Urgency: the community faces a threat to its survival
  • Necessity: there is no other source of food
  • Proportionality: the catch should be limited to what is needed for survival
  • Temporariness: alternatives should be sought as quickly as possible

Conclusion: emergency survival is an exceptional case for the Negentropy Responsibility Principle, but it ought to satisfy the conditions of proportionality and temporariness

Exceptional case 2: basic needs

Negentropy consumption that satisfies basic needs may be exempted from part of the responsibility.

If all affected agents consent to a certain mode of negentropy use, it may serve as an exception.

Analysis:

  • the negotiation was voluntary and informed
  • the outcome of the negotiation took the interests of future generations into account (through representative mechanisms)

§12.5.3 Handling Conflicts with Other Principles

I. Potential Conflicts with the Recursive Freedom Principle

Conflict situation 12.1

Situation: a certain technology can greatly expand the space of possibilities (in accordance with RFP), but it requires the consumption of a large amount of negentropy resources (which may violate PNR).

Conflict analysis:

  • the Recursive Freedom Principle supports the technology (expanding the space of possibilities)
  • the Negentropy Responsibility Principle may oppose the technology (consuming negentropy resources)

Ways of handling:

1. Assess the net effect: does the expansion of the space of possibilities exceed the cost of negentropy consumption?

2. Seek alternatives: is there an alternative technology that consumes less negentropy?

3. Temporal trade-off: does the short-term negentropy consumption buy a long-term expansion of the space of possibilities?

4. Distributional consideration: who bears the cost of the negentropy consumption?

Proposition 12.26 (the relation between RFP and PNR)

The Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP) and the Negentropy Responsibility Principle (PNR) are usually complementary rather than conflicting. Maintaining the space of possibilities requires the support of negentropy resources; maintaining negentropy resources is also for the sake of preserving the future space of possibilities.

II. Potential Conflicts with the Reversibility Principle

Conflict situation 12.2

Situation: a certain irreversible action can significantly maintain a negentropy source (in accordance with PNR), but it violates the Reversibility Principle (PR).

Conflict analysis:

  • the Negentropy Responsibility Principle supports the action (maintaining the negentropy source)
  • the Reversibility Principle may oppose the action (irreversibility)

Ways of handling:

1. Assess the degree of irreversibility: is it completely irreversible or partially reversible?

2. Assess the urgency of negentropy maintenance: is there an urgent negentropy crisis?

3. Risk assessment: if the action fails, what are the consequences?

4. Alternatives: is there a reversible alternative?

III. A General Framework for Handling Conflicts among Principles

Definition 12.40 (the framework for handling conflicts among principles)

When the Negentropy Responsibility Principle conflicts with other principles, the following framework is adopted:

Step 1: Identify the conflict

  • clarify which principles are in conflict
  • assess the nature and degree of the conflict

Step 2: Assess the weight of each principle

  • in the specific situation, what is the relative importance of each principle?
  • does any principle have priority?

Step : Seek a reconciliatory scheme

  • is there a scheme that satisfies all principles at once?
  • can the conflict be reduced by modifying the action?

Step 4: If reconciliation is impossible, make a trade-off

  • determine the priority according to the situation
  • choose the overall optimal scheme

Step 5: Record the reasons for the decision

  • explicitly record why a particular scheme was chosen
  • provide a reference for similar future situations

Chapter 12 Notes

[1] Schrödinger and the concept of negentropy: In What Is Life? (1944), Schrödinger was the first to introduce the concept of negentropy into the life sciences. Cambridge University Press.

[2] The second law of thermodynamics: The second law of thermodynamics states that in an isolated system, entropy always tends to increase. This law is one of the fundamental laws of physics. For philosophical discussion of the second law of thermodynamics, see: Callender, C. (2001). "Thermodynamic Asymmetry in Time." Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

[3] Dissipative structure theory: The theory of dissipative structures developed by Ilya Prigogine explains how open systems far from equilibrium maintain ordered structures. See: Prigogine, I., & Stengers, I. (1984). Order Out of Chaos: Man's New Dialogue with Nature. Bantam Books.

[4] Landauer's principle: Landauer's principle states that erasing one bit of information requires consuming at least of energy. This principle established the connection between information and thermodynamics. See: Landauer, R. (1961). "Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process." IBM Journal of Research and Development, 5(), 18-191.

[5] Theories of responsibility: For philosophical discussion of responsibility, see: Fischer, J. M., & Ravizza, M. (1998). Responsibility and Control: A Theory of Moral Responsibility. Cambridge University Press. Hart, H. L. A. (1968). Punishment and Responsibility: Essays in the Philosophy of Law. Oxford University Press.

[6] Intergenerational justice: For discussion of intergenerational justice, see: Parfit, D. (1984). Reasons and Persons, Part IV. Oxford University Press. Gosseries, A., & Meyer, L. H. (Eds.) (2009). Intergenerational Justice. Oxford University Press.

[7] Environmental ethics: For discussion of environmental ethics, see: Rolston, H. (1988). Environmental Ethics: Duties to and Values in the Natural World. Temple University Press. Light, A., & Rolston, H. (Eds.) (200). Environmental Ethics: An Anthology. Blackwell.

[8] Ecosystem services: For the concept and assessment of ecosystem services, see: Costanza, R., et al. (1997). "The Value of the World's Ecosystem Services and Natural Capital." Nature, 87, 25-260. Millennium Ecosystem Assessment. (2005). Ecosystems and Human Well-being: Synthesis. Island Press.

Chapter 12 Appendix: The Axiom System of the Negentropy Responsibility Principle

Axiom System 12.A (Negentropy Responsibility Principle Axiom System PNR-AX)

[Basic Definitions]

Definition D1 (Negentropy):

N = S_max - S

where S_max is the maximum possible entropy of the system and S is the actual entropy of the system

Definition D2 (Negentropy Flow):

Ṅ(L, E) = dN/dt

the net rate of negentropy transfer from the environment E to the living system L

Definition D (Negentropy Source):

NegSource(S) = {R : ∃Ṅ(S, R) > 0}

The negentropy sources of a subject S are the set of all resources that provide a positive negentropy flow to S

Definition D4 (Entropy Cost):

EntropyCost(S) = Ṡ_produce(S)

the rate of entropy increase produced by the activity of subject S

Definition D5 (Entropy Externalization):

Externalize(S, E) ↔ Ṡ_emit(S, E) > 0 ∧ ¬Compensate(S, E)

subject S emits entropy into environment E and does not compensate

[Axioms]

Axiom A1 (Axiom of Maintenance):

∀S ∈ L: O(Maintain(NegSource(S)))

A living subject ought to maintain its negentropy sources

Axiom A2 (Axiom Prohibiting Externalization):

∀S ∈ L, ∀E ∈ SharedEnv: O(¬Externalize(S, E))

A living subject ought not to externalize entropy costs onto the shared environment

Axiom A (Axiom of Intergenerational Responsibility):

∀S ∈ L, ∀G ∈ FutureGen: O(¬Externalize(S, G))

A living subject ought not to externalize entropy costs onto future generations

Axiom A4 (Axiom of Responsibility Allocation):

∀S ∈ L: R(S) = f(Consumption(S), Capability(S), Benefit(S), Baseline)

Negentropy responsibility is allocated by consumption, capability, benefit, and baseline

Axiom A5 (Axiom of Balance):

∀S ∈ L: Ṅ_consume(S) ≤ Ṅ_contribute(S) + Ṅ_regenerate(Source(S))

Negentropy consumption ought not to exceed contribution plus regeneration

[Inference Rules]

Rule R1 (Inference Rule of Maintenance):

If NegSource(S) is under threat, then S has an obligation to take maintenance action

Rule R2 (Inference Rule of Externalization):

If Externalize(S, E), then S has an obligation to stop externalizing or to compensate E

Rule R (Inference Rule of Responsibility Allocation):

R(S) = α·(Consumption(S)/Σ_i Consumption(S_i))

+ β·(Capability(S)/Σ_i Capability(S_i))

+ γ·(Benefit(S)/Σ_i Benefit(S_i))

+ δ·R_baseline

where α + β + γ + δ = 1

Rule R4 (Inference Rule of Exceptions):

EmergencySurvival(S) ∨ BasicNeeds(S) ∨ TechLimitation(S) ∨ NegotiatedConsent(S)

→ TemporaryExemption(S)

[Theorems]

Theorem T1 (Negentropy Maintenance Theorem):

∀S ∈ L: Survive(S) → O(Maintain(NegSource(S)))

Proof: Follows directly from NL-2 and Axiom A1

Theorem T2 (Externalization Prohibition Theorem):

∀S ∈ L, ∀E: Externalize(S, E) → ¬Permissible(Action(S))

Proof: Follows from Axioms A2 and A

Theorem T (Responsibility Proportion Theorem):

∀S₁, S₂ ∈ L: Consumption(S₁) > Consumption(S₂) → R(S₁) > R(S₂)

Proof: Follows from Axiom A4 and Rule R (when other factors are equal)
