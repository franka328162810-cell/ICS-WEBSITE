Chapter 27　New Insights from Formalization

Chapter Introduction

This chapter is a summary of the theoretical gains of Volume II. The preceding twenty-six chapters constructed 27 formal systems, proved about 180 theorems, and completed a full formalization from the Three New Views to the indicator system. But the value of formalization lies not only in “writing down what is already known in symbols,” but even more in “discovering the unknown through formalization.”

The core task of this chapter is: to display the new insights produced by the process of formalization itself—those discoveries that did not appear in the natural-language arguments of Volume I and that can be revealed only through rigorous formalization.

The Core Thesis of This Chapter

Formalization is not decoration, but a tool of discovery. The formalization work of Volume II has produced at least 15 substantive new insights, and these insights:

  • cannot be read off directly from the arguments of Volume I
  • require the rigor of formalization in order to be discovered
  • have an important corrective or deepening effect on the theoretical framework
  • provide new guidance for the applications of Volume III

Significance One: The Pluralistic Formulations of Ontology

Significance Two: The Possibility of Translation

Topological isomorphism guarantees the complete translatability among the Three Views. Any proposition expressible in one view can be translated losslessly into the other two. This provides a solid foundation for interdisciplinary dialogue.

Significance Three: The Identification of Invariants

Topological isomorphism reveals the invariants shared by the Three Views—those structural features that remain unchanged under all formulations. These invariants are the core of the ontology of the universe.

  • connectivity
  • emergence
  • the arrow of time

Guidance for Volume III

The guiding role of this insight for Volume III:

2. Cross-civilizational communication: different civilizations may prefer different formulations, but translation can be achieved through the isomorphism mappings

3. Theoretical simplification: certain problems are simpler under one formulation, and one can solve them under that formulation first and then translate back

§27.1.3　Insight Three: The Triple Origin of the Arrow of Time

Statement of the Insight

The directedness of time (the arrow of time) has three independent origins in the Three Views, but these three origins are formally equivalent:

  • Relational Ontology: the irreversible accumulation of relations
  • Process Ontology: the directedness of entropy increase
  • Participatory Universe: the irreversibility of observation

Basis in Formalization

This insight comes from Theorem 7.5.3 (the Triple Equivalence of the Arrow of Time) of Chapter 7 of Volume II:

Theorem 7.5.3 (the Triple Equivalence of the Arrow of Time)

The following three propositions are equivalent:

1. The topological complexity of the relational network increases monotonically

2. The entropy of the process manifold increases monotonically

3. The information of the observer space increases monotonically

Key points of the proof:

1. Use the topological isomorphism of Theorem 7.5.1

2. Prove that the three kinds of “monotonicity” are transformed into one another under the isomorphism mappings

3. Verify that the three kinds of monotonicity all define the same direction of time

What Was Missing in Volume I

Volume I discussed the relations between the Three Views and time separately, but did not reveal the equivalence among the three. In particular, Volume I did not realize that the arrow of time can be derived from three independent angles.

Philosophical Significance

Significance One: The Emergence of Time

Time is not fundamental, but emerges from more fundamental structures (relations, processes, observation). This supports the “timeless” picture of certain quantum gravity theories (such as the Wheeler-DeWitt equation).

Significance Two: The Ontological Basis of the Second Law of Thermodynamics

The increase of entropy in Process Ontology is not an independent physical law, but a necessary consequence of the accumulation of relations and the irreversibility of observation. This provides a deeper ontological explanation for the second law of thermodynamics.

Significance Three: The Objectivity of the Direction of Time

The triple equivalence shows that the arrow of time does not depend on any particular formulation, and is therefore objective. This answers the doubts of certain philosophers (such as McTaggart) about the objectivity of time.

Guidance for Volume III

1. Multiple formulations of irreversibility: the Reversibility Principle (REV) can be understood and applied from three angles

2. The unification of time scales: the Three Views provide a unified definition of the time scale

3. The formalization of historical dependence: path dependence can be consistently formalized within the Three Views

Residual Problems

Problem One: Does the emergence of time mean that time is “illusory”?

Response: No. Emergent properties are still real. Temperature is also emergent, but temperature is not illusory.

Problem Two: Does the triple equivalence rule out the possibility of time travel?

Response: At the classical level, yes. But at the quantum level there may be exceptions (such as closed timelike curves). The formalization of Volume II is confined to the classical case.

Classical utilitarianism permits trade-offs among different value dimensions. The lexicographic priority relation shows that, at least at the level of RFP, such trade-offs are not permitted.

The lexicographic priority relation greatly simplifies actual decision making:

1. First check RFP, and rule out all options that violate RFP

2. Among the options satisfying RFP, check NRP, and rule out the options that violate NRP

3. Among the options satisfying RFP and NRP, choose according to REV

This avoids complex multi-objective optimization problems.

Guidance for Volume III

1. The priority of indicators: UCS and RFD (corresponding to RFP and NRP) have absolute priority

2. The constraints of institutional design: any institutional design must first satisfy RFP and NRP

3. The process of ethical review: ethical review should proceed in lexicographic order

But this is an epistemological compromise, not a normative trade-off.

Problem Two: Does the lexicographic order depend on a particular formalization?

Response: §21.4.5 of Chapter 21 of Volume II proves that the lexicographic order holds under a variety of formalizations, and so it is robust.

What Was Missing in Volume I

The definition of reversibility in Chapter 9 of Volume I was intuitive: “the ability to return to the original state.” But what is “returning”? What is the “original state”? These concepts are vague in natural language. The topological characterization provides a precise mathematical definition.

Philosophical Significance

Significance One: The Essence of Irreversibility

Theorem 20.4.2 reveals the essence of irreversibility: an irreversible action either has no inverse mapping, or destroys the topological structure (its inverse mapping is not continuous).

The second case is particularly illuminating: even if an action can in principle be “undone,” if the undoing process destroys the topological structure of the space of possibilities (for example, causing some possibilities to become discontinuously reachable), the action is still irreversible.

Significance Two: A Connection with Thermodynamics

The topological characterization has a deep connection with the second law of thermodynamics. Thermodynamically irreversible processes correspond to non-homeomorphic mappings in phase space. This shows that the Reversibility Principle is consistent with the fundamental laws of physics.

Significance Three: A Critique of “Technological Fixes”

Certain technological optimists believe that any problem can be “fixed” by future technology. The topological characterization shows that this view is mistaken: if an action destroys the topological structure of the space of possibilities, then no matter how advanced the technology, it cannot truly be “fixed.”

For example: species extinction may be topologically irreversible; even if the species could be “revived” in the future through genetic engineering, the revived species would not occupy the same position in the space of possibilities as the original species.

Guidance for Volume III

1. The determination of irreversibility: whether an action is reversible can be determined by topological analysis

2. A topological method of risk assessment: when assessing the risk of an action, one should analyze its effect on the topology of the space of possibilities

3. The limits of technological fixes: one should not rely excessively on the promise of “future technological fixes”

Examples of Topological Irreversibility

1. Species extinction: it deletes a connected component of the space of possibilities

2. Nuclear war: it destroys the continuity of the paths of civilizational development

3. AGI going out of control: it may lead to a topological collapse of the space of possibilities

4. Climate tipping points: after crossing a tipping point, the topology of the space of possibilities undergoes an irreversible change

Response: This is a difficult problem. §20.3 of Chapter 20 of Volume II provides several methods of construction, but there is no unique “correct” topology. Different choices of topology may lead to different determinations of reversibility.

§27.2.5　Insight Nine: The Self-consistency Conditions of the Three Principles

Statement of the Insight

The joint application of the three major principles (RFP, NRP, REV) is not always self-consistent. There exist certain extreme situations in which the three principles produce irreconcilable conflicts. But these conflict situations can be precisely characterized, and in “normal” situations the three principles are self-consistent.

Basis in Formalization

This insight comes from Theorem 21.3.1 (the Conditional Consistency Theorem) of Chapter 21 of Volume II:

Theorem 21.3.1 (the Conditional Consistency Theorem)

If a situation satisfies the following conditions:

1. There exists at least one feasible action satisfying RFP

2. There exists at least one feasible action satisfying NRP

3. There exists at least one feasible action satisfying REV

4. The set of feasible actions is compact

then the joint application of the three principles is consistent, that is, there exists at least one action that simultaneously satisfies RFP, NRP, and REV.

Key points of the proof:

1. Use the lexicographic priority relation (Theorem 21.4.4)

2. Construct a sequence of actions satisfying the three principles

3. Use compactness to prove that the sequence converges to a feasible action

4. Verify that this action satisfies all three principles

  • Giving the food to B: satisfies B's NRP, but violates A's NRP
  • Splitting the food: neither can maintain its negentropic organization, violating both their NRPs

In this situation, there is no action that simultaneously satisfies all the principles.

Counterexample 2: The dilemma of an irreversible choice

  • Suppose one must choose between two irreversible actions
  • Action 1: highly compresses RFD, but maintains the negentropic organization
  • Action 2: maintains RFD, but causes the collapse of the negentropic organization

In this situation, the lexicographic priority relation requires choosing Action 2 (RFP takes priority over NRP), but this causes the collapse of the negentropic organization, violating the core requirement of NRP.

Significance One: The Formalization of Moral Dilemmas

Significance Two: A Challenge to “Ought Implies Can”

This shows that “ought implies can” is not a universally valid metaethical principle, but a heuristic rule that holds in normal situations.

8. Apply the system as a whole (Insight 15): apply the integrated system as a whole, and exploit its emergent properties

The Limits of Formalization

Although formalization has produced important insights, we must also acknowledge its limits:

1. Abstractness: formalization necessarily involves abstraction and simplification, and may omit important concrete details

2. Intelligibility: the results of formalization require specialized training to understand, which may limit their dissemination

3. Computational complexity: the computational complexity of certain formalization results (such as topological analysis) is very high, making practical application difficult

4. Cultural particularity: the mathematical language used in formalization may be less accessible to certain cultures

Volume V will discuss how to improve intelligibility and applicability while maintaining the rigor of formalization.

Future Formalization Work

The 15 insights identified in this chapter are not the end point of the formalization work, but a new starting point. Future formalization work can proceed in the following directions:

1. Deepening the existing insights: conduct deeper analysis and extension of the 15 insights

2. Discovering new insights: continue the formalization work, which may discover more insights

3. Interdisciplinary integration: integrate the formalization of this work with the formalizations of other disciplines (physics, biology, computer science)

4. The improvement of machine verification: improve the Coq/Isabelle/Lean implementations and increase the verification coverage

5. Education and dissemination: develop teaching materials to lower the threshold for understanding formalization

Conclusion

Through the formalization work of 27 chapters, Volume II has not only provided a rigorous foundation for the arguments of Volume I, but, more importantly, has produced 15 substantive new insights. These insights deepen our understanding of the universe, life, cognition, and normativity, and provide solid guidance for the applications of Volume III.

Formalization has proved its value: it is not decoration, but a tool of discovery.

Volume III will show how to transform these theoretical insights into practical guidance, and how to apply the abstract formal systems to concrete ethical problems and institutional design.

Appendix 27E: Counterexamples and Boundary Conditions of the Insights

This appendix discusses the boundaries of applicability and the known counterexamples of the 15 insights.

Boundaries of Insight 1:

Topological isomorphism may fail in the following cases:

  • Quantum scale: quantum effects may destroy the classical topological structure
  • Near singularities: the topology near a spacetime singularity may be unstable
  • Non-standard logic: if non-classical logic is adopted (such as intuitionistic logic), the isomorphism may not hold

Boundaries of Insight 2:

The quantization of participation may need revision in the following cases:

  • Macroscopic scale: when the degree of participation is far greater than , the quantization effects can be neglected
  • Continuum limit: under certain theoretical limits, a continuous approximation may be needed

Boundaries of Insight 5:

The lexicographic priority relation may need to be relaxed in the following cases:

  • Measurement error: when the difference between two options on a high-priority principle is smaller than the measurement error
  • Extreme situations: in the counterexample situations of Theorem 21.3.1, the lexicographic order may lead to no solution

Boundaries of Insight 7:

Non-local responsibility may need to be limited in the following cases:

  • Causal isolation: if two events are separated by an event horizon, the responsibility may be zero
  • Practical workability: a decay function needs to be introduced to make the computation of responsibility feasible

Boundaries of Insight 8:

The topological characterization may be insufficient in the following cases:

  • Choice of topology: different choices of topology may lead to different determinations of reversibility
  • Measurement problem: it is difficult in practice to measure the topology of the space of possibilities

Boundaries of Insight 10:

The concrete form of the UCS-RFD coupling may depend on:

  • Physical substrate: beings with different substrates may have different coupling functions
  • Cognitive architecture: different cognitive architectures may have different optimal intervals

Boundaries of Insight 14:

The modal characterization of deep uncertainty may need extension in the following cases:

  • Quantum uncertainty: quantum uncertainty may require a different modal structure
  • Subjective uncertainty: an individual's subjective uncertainty may differ from objective deep uncertainty

Appendix 27G: Reader Feedback and Frequently Asked Questions

This appendix collects the feedback and frequently asked questions of first-edition readers concerning the formalization part.

FAQ 1: Is formalization too abstract, divorced from reality?

Response: The abstractness of formalization is necessary; it guarantees the rigor and universality of the theory. But we also recognize that an abstract theory needs to be “grounded” through the applications of Volume III. Volume IV provides computational tools that lower the threshold for application.

FAQ 2: Why is machine verification needed? Are human proofs not enough?

Response: Machine verification provides an additional safeguard. Complex proofs are prone to errors that are difficult for humans to detect. Machine verification is not a distrust of human proofs, but provides an independent check.

FAQ 3: Do the 15 insights exhaust all the discoveries of formalization?

Response: No. The 15 insights are the most important discoveries, but there are many smaller discoveries in the process of formalization. We chose 15 in order to keep the chapter readable.

FAQ 4: Has formalization changed the core conclusions of Volume I?

Response: No. Formalization has deepened and made precise the arguments of Volume I, but the core conclusions remain consistent. This proves that the intuitions of Volume I were correct.

FAQ 5: How can readers who do not understand mathematics understand these insights?

Response: We provide non-technical explanations in the “Philosophical Significance” part of each insight. Readers may skip the formalization details and read the Philosophical Significance part directly. Chapter 3 of Volume V also provides popularized explanations.

FAQ 6: Are there contradictions among the insights?

Response: No. In Appendix 27B we analyzed the dependencies among the insights and proved that they are mutually consistent.

FAQ 7: Why are some insights only partially verified?

Response: Mainly for technical reasons. Certain mathematical libraries are not yet complete in certain provers. We plan to complete all the verifications in the third edition.

FAQ 8: Does formalization make the theory “rigid”?

Response: Quite the contrary. Formalization makes the theory more flexible, because it clarifies which parts are core (and cannot be changed) and which parts can be adjusted. This provides a clear path for the evolution of the theory.

FAQ 9: Do other philosophers accept these insights?

Response: The reaction of the academic community has been positive, but there are also criticisms. The second part of Volume V discusses the academic dialogue in detail. Overall, the rigor of the formalization has been recognized, but certain philosophical interpretations remain controversial.

FAQ 10: Is formalization applicable to all philosophical problems?

Response: No. Formalization is applicable to problems that can be precisely defined. Certain philosophical problems (such as aesthetic and existential questions) may not be suited to formalization. We do not claim that formalization is the only method of philosophy, but that it is a valuable complementary method.
