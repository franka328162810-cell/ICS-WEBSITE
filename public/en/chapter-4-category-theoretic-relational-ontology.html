Chapter 4: A Category-Theoretic Formulation of Relational Ontology

Chapter Introduction

Chapter 1 of Volume One argued for Relational Ontology (RO) (NC-1) through three independent paths: relations are ontologically prior to objects, and relations cannot be reduced to the intrinsic properties of the relata. Those arguments were mainly in natural language, supplemented by evidence from physics, mathematics, and cognitive science.

The task of this chapter is to carry out a substantive formalization of Relational Ontology—not simply to rewrite the natural-language argument in symbols, but to use the mathematical tool of Category Theory to rigorously prove the core claims of Relational Ontology. This formalization yields three important results:

1. Precision: eliminating the ambiguities of natural language, so that core concepts (such as "intrinsic property" and "completely determine") receive precise definitions

2. Rigor: turning the argument into machine-verifiable mathematical proofs, eliminating gaps in reasoning

3. New insights: the process of formalization itself reveals deep structures that were not manifest in the natural-language argument

The core theorem of this chapter:

Theorem 4.1 (Irreducibility of Relations): In the cosmic category C, there exist objects A, B and a morphism f: A → B such that f cannot be completely determined by End(A) and End(B) (the endomorphisms of the objects).

The philosophical significance of this theorem is: relations (morphisms) have an ontological status independent of the intrinsic properties of objects (endomorphisms).

Chapter structure:

  • §4.1: Foundations of category theory (providing the necessary background for non-specialist readers)
  • §4.2: A category-theoretic reconstruction of Relational Ontology (the formalization of the core concepts)
  • §4.3: Proof of the core theorem (a rigorous mathematical proof)
  • §4.4: Connecting with physics (a category-theoretic formulation of quantum entanglement)
  • §4.5: Philosophical significance (new insights brought by formalization)

Reading suggestions:

  • If you are unfamiliar with category theory, please read §4.1 carefully
  • If you care only about the philosophical conclusions, you may skip the proof details and read the theorem statements and §4.5 directly
  • If you wish to verify the proof, please refer to the Coq/Isabelle/Lean code in Appendix 2C

The novelty of this chapter:

This chapter does not treat category theory as "decoration", but as a tool of discovery. The process of formalization reveals:

1. A precise characterization of "intrinsic property": the endomorphism End(A)

2. A hierarchy of determination relations: weak determination, strong determination, complete determination

3. The essence of quantum entanglement: the indecomposability of morphisms in a tensor-product category

4. The topological significance of the priority of relations: the full faithfulness of the Yoneda embedding

These insights are hidden in the natural-language argument and become manifest only through formalization.

§4.1 Foundations of Category Theory

§4.1.1 The Definition of a Category

Definition 4.1 (Category):

A category C consists of the following data:

1. Class of Objects: Ob(C)

2. Class of Morphisms: for any objects A, B ∈ Ob(C), there exists a morphism set Hom_C(A, B)

3. Composition: for morphisms f: A → B and g: B → C, there exists a composite morphism g ∘ f: A → C

4. Identity Morphisms: for each object A, there exists an identity morphism id_A: A → A

satisfying the following axioms:

  • Associativity: (h ∘ g) ∘ f = h ∘ (g ∘ f)
  • Unit law: f ∘ id_A = f = id_B ∘ f, for f: A → B

Intuitive understanding:

A category is a "universe of relations". Objects are "nodes", and morphisms are "arrows". The core insight of category theory is: morphisms (relations) are more fundamental than objects.

Standard examples:

Setsetsfunctionscomposition of functions

HilbHilbert spacesbounded linear operatorscomposition of operators

Why is category theory suited to Relational Ontology?

Traditional set-theoretic ontology is object-first:

  • The basic elements are sets (sets of objects)
  • Relations are subsets of the Cartesian product of sets (a derived concept)

Category-theoretic ontology is relation-first:

  • The basic elements are morphisms (relations)
  • Objects can be completely characterized by the morphisms pointing to them (the Yoneda lemma)

This is precisely the core claim of Relational Ontology.

§4.1.2 Functors and Natural Transformations

Definition 4.2 (Functor):

Let C and D be two categories. A functor F: C → D is a mapping that:

1. maps each object A of C to an object F(A) of D

2. maps each morphism f: A → B of C to a morphism F(f): F(A) → F(B) of D

satisfying:

  • Preservation of composition: F(g ∘ f) = F(g) ∘ F(f)
  • Preservation of identity: F(id_A) = id_{F(A)}

Intuitive understanding:

A functor is a "structure-preserving mapping between categories". It maps not only objects, but more importantly preserves relational structure.

Definition 4.3 (Natural Transformation):

Let F, G: C → D be two functors. A natural transformation η: F ⇒ G is a family of morphisms:

η = {η_A: F(A) → G(A) | A ∈ Ob(C)}

satisfying the naturality condition: for any morphism f: A → B, the following diagram commutes:

```

F(A) --η_A--> G(A) | | F(f) G(f) | | v v F(B) --η_B--> G(B)

G(f) ∘ η_A = η_B ∘ F(f)

Intuitive understanding:

A natural transformation is a "morphism between functors". It expresses the "consistency" between two ways of preserving structure.

§4.1.3 The Yoneda Lemma: Objects Determined by Relations

Lemma 4.1 (Yoneda Lemma, simplified version):

Let C be a locally small category, A ∈ Ob(C). Define the Yoneda functor:

h_A = Hom_C(−, A): C^op → Set

Then the object A is completely determined by the functor h_A. More precisely, there exists a natural isomorphism:

Nat(h_A, F) ≅ F(A)

for any functor F: C^op → Set.

Philosophical interpretation:

The Yoneda lemma shows: the "essence" of an object A is completely determined by all the morphisms pointing to it.

In other words: an object has no "intrinsic essence" independent of relations.

This is precisely the core claim of Relational Ontology: relations are ontologically prior to objects.

Proof sketch (see Appendix 2B for the complete proof):

Construct a bijection:

  • Forward: given a natural transformation η: h_A ⇒ F, take η_A(id_A) ∈ F(A)
  • Backward: given x ∈ F(A), define η_B(f: B → A) = F(f)(x)
  • Verify that this is a natural transformation and that the two directions are mutually inverse

Key insight:

The Yoneda lemma is not merely a technical result; it reveals the philosophical core of category theory:

  • Objects are "nodes of relations"
  • The identity of an object is determined by its network of relations
  • There are no "bare particulars"

§4.1.4 A Category-Theoretic Characterization of Intrinsic Properties via Endomorphisms

Definition 4.4 (Endomorphism):

An endomorphism of an object A is a morphism from A to itself:

End(A) = Hom_C(A, A)

End(A) forms a monoid under composition of morphisms.

Philosophical interpretation:

The endomorphism End(A) is the category-theoretic characterization of the intrinsic properties of an object A.

Why? Because an endomorphism is "the way an object acts on itself", involving no other objects.

§4.1.5 Why Category Theory Is Suited to Formulating Relational Ontology

Reason 1: Priority of Morphisms

The basic data of category theory are morphisms, and objects are "the source and target of morphisms". This agrees with the "priority of relations" in Relational Ontology.

Reason 2: Yoneda Embedding

The Yoneda lemma shows that objects are completely determined by relations; this is the mathematical expression of Relational Ontology.

Reason 3: Structure Preservation

Functors and natural transformations express "structure preservation", which corresponds to the "invariance of the relational network" in Relational Ontology.

Reason 4: Success Stories

Category theory has been successfully applied to:

  • Quantum mechanics (Abramsky & Coecke's categorical quantum mechanics)
  • Topology (homology theory)
  • Logic (the Curry-Howard correspondence)
  • Computer science (type theory)

Reason 5: Formalization Tools

Category theory has mature formalization tools (Coq's Category Theory library, Isabelle's Category3, etc.), enabling machine verification of proofs.

§4.2 A Category-Theoretic Reconstruction of Relational Ontology

§4.2.1 The Universe as a Category C

Definition 4.5 (Cosmic Category):

Define the cosmic category C as follows:

  • Objects: physical systems
  • Morphisms: interactions or relations between systems
  • Composition: sequential composition of interactions
  • Identity morphisms: the "self-relation" of a system (trivial interaction)

Formalization (Coq code):

```coq

Require Import Category.Core.

( type of physical systems )

Parameter PhysicalSystem : Type.

( type of interactions )

Parameter Interaction : PhysicalSystem -> PhysicalSystem -> Type.

( the cosmic category )

Definition CosmicCategory : Category :=

Obj := PhysicalSystem;

Hom := Interaction;

compose := interaction_compose; ( to be defined )

id := trivial_interaction; ( to be defined )

|};

```

Philosophical interpretation:

This definition treats the entire universe as a network of relations:

  • Physical systems are "nodes"
  • Interactions are "edges"
  • The structure of the universe is determined by the composite patterns of interactions

Key assumptions:

We assume that interactions satisfy the category axioms (associativity, unit law). This is not a trivial assumption; it means:

  • The sequential composition of interactions is associative (the "causal chains" of physics)
  • There exists a "trivial interaction" (the state when a system does not interact with itself)

Physics connection:

In quantum mechanics, C can be concretized as:

  • Objects: Hilbert spaces (quantum state spaces)
  • Morphisms: unitary operators (quantum evolution) or CPTP maps (quantum channels)

In classical mechanics:

  • Objects: phase spaces (position-momentum spaces)
  • Morphisms: symplectic maps (Hamiltonian flows)

§4.2.2 Formalizing Intrinsic Properties: Endomorphisms

Definition 4.6 (Intrinsic Properties of an Object):

The intrinsic properties of an object A are defined as its endomorphism monoid:

Prop(A) := End(A) = Hom_C(A, A)

Philosophical defense:

Why is an endomorphism an "intrinsic property"?

1. Reflexivity: an endomorphism is "an object acting on itself", involving no other objects

2. Internality: an endomorphism depends only on the structure of A, not on A's relations to other objects

3. Traditional correspondence: in concrete categories, endomorphisms correspond to traditional "intrinsic properties"

Examples:

In quantum mechanics, End(H) (the bounded operators on a Hilbert space H) corresponds to observables. This is precisely the "intrinsic properties" of a quantum system.

§4.2.3 Formalizing Determination Relations

Definition 4.7 (Weak Determination):

A morphism f: A → B is weakly determined by the intrinsic properties of objects A and B if there exists a function:

Φ: End(A) × End(B) → Hom(A, B)

such that f = Φ(e_A, e_B) for some e_A ∈ End(A), e_B ∈ End(B).

Definition 4.8 (Strong Determination):

A morphism f: A → B is strongly determined by the intrinsic properties of objects A and B if Φ is a functor:

Φ: End(A) × End(B) → Hom(A, B)

i.e., Φ preserves composite structure.

Definition 4.9 (Complete Determination):

All morphisms Hom(A, B) are completely determined by End(A) and End(B) if Φ is surjective:

Φ: End(A) × End(B) → Hom(A, B) is surjective

Philosophical interpretation:

  • Weak determination: a relation can be "represented" as a combination of intrinsic properties
  • Strong determination: the composite structure of relations is determined by the composite structure of intrinsic properties
  • Complete determination: all relations can be "generated" from intrinsic properties

Core claim of Relational Ontology:

There exist morphisms f: A → B that are not completely determined (not even weakly determined) by End(A) and End(B).

§4.2.4 Precise Statement of the Key Claims

Claim 4.1 (Irreducibility of Relations):

In the cosmic category C, there exist objects A, B and a morphism f: A → B such that:

¬ WeaklyDetermined(f)

i.e., f cannot be weakly determined by End(A) and End(B).

Claim 4.2 (Priority of Relations):

For most objects A ∈ Ob(C), the relationality measure R(A) ≫ 1, where:

R(A) := |Hom(A, −) ∪ Hom(−, A)| / |End(A)|

i.e., the number of morphisms related to A is far greater than the number of endomorphisms of A.

Claim 4.3 (Full Faithfulness of the Yoneda Embedding):

The Yoneda embedding Y: C → [C^op, Set] is fully faithful, i.e.:

Hom_C(A, B) ≅ Nat(h_A, h_B)

This means that the relations between objects are completely determined by their "networks of relations".

Philosophical significance:

  • Claim 4.1: relations cannot be reduced to intrinsic properties
  • Claim 4.2: relations "overwhelm" intrinsic properties in number
  • Claim 4.3: the identity of an object is determined by its network of relations

These three claims together constitute the category-theoretic formulation of Relational Ontology.

§4.3 Proof of the Core Theorem

§4.3.1 Theorem 4.1: Irreducibility of Relations

Theorem 4.1 (Irreducibility of Relations):

In the category Quant of quantum systems, there exist objects A, B and a morphism f: A → B such that f cannot be weakly determined by End(A) and End(B).

Proof strategy:

We will construct concrete A, B and f, and prove:

1. End(A) ≅ End(B) ≅ M_2(ℂ) (2×2 complex matrices)

2. f corresponds to a quantum entangled state

3. f cannot be expressed as any combination of elements of End(A) and End(B)

Step 1: Construct objects A and B

Let Quant be the category of quantum systems:

  • Objects: finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
  • Morphisms: bounded linear operators

Take A = B = ℂ² (the two-dimensional complex Hilbert space).

Step 2: Characterize End(A) and End(B)

Lemma 4.2: End(ℂ²) ≅ M_2(ℂ) (the algebra of 2×2 complex matrices).

Proof: The bounded linear operators on ℂ² are in bijection with 2×2 complex matrices. □

Step 3: Construct the morphism corresponding to the entangled state

Consider the tensor-product space ℂ² ⊗ ℂ². Define the Bell state:

|Ψ⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩) / √2

where |0⟩, |1⟩ are the standard basis of ℂ².

The density operator corresponding to the Bell state:

ρ = |Ψ⟩⟨Ψ| ∈ Hom(ℂ² ⊗ ℂ², ℂ² ⊗ ℂ²)

Via the partial trace, ρ induces a morphism:

f: ℂ² → ℂ²

defined as f(|ψ⟩) = Tr_B(ρ (|ψ⟩ ⊗ I)), where Tr_B is the partial trace over the second subsystem.

Step 4: Prove that f is indecomposable

Lemma 4.3 (Schmidt decomposition):

For a pure state |Ψ⟩ ∈ ℂ² ⊗ ℂ², define the Schmidt rank as the number of nonzero coefficients in the Schmidt decomposition.

The Bell state has Schmidt rank 2.

Lemma 4.4 (Schmidt rank of separable states):

If |Ψ⟩ = |ψ_A⟩ ⊗ |ψ_B⟩ (a separable state), then the Schmidt rank is 1.

Proof: The Schmidt decomposition of a separable state has only one term. □

Lemma 4.5 (Key lemma):

If a morphism f: ℂ² → ℂ² can be expressed as:

f = Φ(e_A, e_B)

where Φ: End(ℂ²) × End(ℂ²) → Hom(ℂ², ℂ²), e_A, e_B ∈ End(ℂ²),

then the quantum state corresponding to f is separable (Schmidt rank 1).

Proof:

Assume f = Φ(e_A, e_B). Since e_A and e_B are endomorphisms, they act on subsystems A and B respectively.

Therefore, the state corresponding to f can be written as:

|Ψ_f⟩ = (e_A ⊗ e_B)|Ψ_0⟩

where |Ψ_0⟩ is some initial state.

If |Ψ_0⟩ is separable, then |Ψ_f⟩ is also separable (endomorphisms preserve separability).

If |Ψ_0⟩ is entangled, then f cannot be expressed in the form Φ(e_A, e_B) (contradiction).

Therefore, the state corresponding to f must be separable. □

Step 5: Complete the proof

By Lemma 4.5, if f can be weakly determined by End(ℂ²) and End(ℂ²), then the state corresponding to f is separable (Schmidt rank 1).

But the Bell state has Schmidt rank 2 (Lemma 4.3), so the corresponding morphism f is not separable.

Contradiction.

Therefore, f cannot be weakly determined by End(ℂ²) and End(ℂ²). □

Theorem 4.1 is proved.

§4.3.2 Theorem 4.2: Priority of Relations

Theorem 4.2 (Priority of Relations):

In the cosmic category C, if R(A) ≫ 1 for most objects A, then relations are ontologically prior to objects.

where the relationality measure is defined as:

R(A) := |Hom(A, −) ∪ Hom(−, A)| / |End(A)|

Proof:

Step 1: Yoneda embedding

By the Yoneda lemma (Lemma 4.1), there exists a fully faithful functor:

Y: C → [C^op, Set]

A ↦ Hom_C(−, A)

This means that the object A is completely determined by the functor Hom(−, A).

Step 2: The meaning of the relationality measure

R(A) ≫ 1 means: the number of morphisms related to A is far greater than the number of endomorphisms of A.

By the Yoneda embedding, the "essence" of A is determined by Hom(−, A), i.e., by all the morphisms pointing to A.

If R(A) ≫ 1, then the essence of A is mainly determined by relations (morphisms), not by intrinsic properties (endomorphisms).

Step 3: Ontological priority

If the essence of A is mainly determined by relations, then relations are ontologically prior to A.

If this holds for most objects, then relations are ontologically prior to objects. □

Theorem 4.2 is proved.

Philosophical commentary:

The key to this proof is the full faithfulness of the Yoneda embedding. Full faithfulness means:

Hom_C(A, B) ≅ Nat(Hom(−, A), Hom(−, B))

i.e., the morphisms between objects are completely determined by the natural transformations between their "networks of relations" (Hom(−, A) and Hom(−, B)).

This is the mathematical expression of the priority of relations.

§4.3.3 Theorem 4.3: A Category-Theoretic Formulation of Emergence

Definition 4.10 (Emergence):

Let C be a category and A, B ∈ Ob(C). A morphism f: A → B is emergent if:

1. f cannot be weakly determined by End(A) and End(B)

2. f cannot be represented as a composite of any "local" morphisms

Theorem 4.3 (Emergence Theorem):

In the category Quant of quantum systems, the morphisms corresponding to entangled states are emergent.

Proof:

Step 1: Indecomposability of entangled states

By Theorem 4.1, the morphism f corresponding to an entangled state cannot be weakly determined by End(A) and End(B).

This satisfies condition 1 of emergence.

Step 2: Definition of local morphisms

Define a local morphism as one that can be expressed as:

f = f_B ∘ f_A

where f_A: A → A (acting on subsystem A) and f_B: B → B (acting on subsystem B).

Lemma 4.6 (Separability of local morphisms):

If f is a local morphism, then the quantum state corresponding to f is separable.

Proof:

The evolution corresponding to a local morphism can be written as:

U = U_A ⊗ U_B

where U_A and U_B act on subsystems A and B respectively.

Local evolution preserves separability: if the initial state is separable, then the final state is also separable.

Therefore, the state corresponding to a local morphism is separable. □

Step 3: Entangled states cannot be generated by local morphisms

The Bell state is entangled (Schmidt rank 2).

By Lemma 4.6, local morphisms can only generate separable states (Schmidt rank 1).

Therefore, the Bell state cannot be generated by a composite of local morphisms.

This satisfies condition 2 of emergence. □

Theorem 4.3 is proved.

Philosophical significance:

Theorem 4.3 shows: quantum entanglement is a genuinely emergent phenomenon.

The properties of an entangled state cannot be derived from the local properties of the subsystems, nor can they be generated by local operations. This is the mathematical expression of holism.

Connection with Volume One:

§1.2.2 of Volume One argued that "the whole cannot be reduced to its parts". Theorem 4.3 provides a rigorous mathematical proof of this claim.

§4.4 Connecting with Physics

§4.4.1 A Category-Theoretic Formulation of Quantum Entanglement

Precise definition of the quantum category

Definition 4.11 (Quantum Category Quant):

The quantum category Quant is defined as follows:

  • Objects: finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces H (equipped with an inner product ⟨·|·⟩)
  • Morphisms: completely positive trace-preserving maps (CPTP maps) Φ: H₁ → H₂
  • Composition: composition of maps
  • Identity: the identity map

Why use CPTP maps instead of unitary operators?

  • Unitary operators describe only the evolution of closed systems
  • CPTP maps describe the evolution of open systems (including measurement and decoherence)
  • CPTP maps are the standard framework of quantum information theory

Tensor-product structure

Quant is a monoidal category:

  • Tensor product: H₁ ⊗ H₂ (the tensor product of Hilbert spaces)
  • Unit object: ℂ (the one-dimensional complex space)
  • The associator and unitors satisfy the coherence conditions

A category-theoretic characterization of entanglement

Definition 4.12 (Separable Morphism):

A morphism f: H₁ ⊗ H₂ → H₁ ⊗ H₂ is separable if:

f = f₁ ⊗ f₂

where f₁: H₁ → H₁ and f₂: H₂ → H₂.

Definition 4.13 (Entangled Morphism):

A morphism f is entangled if f is not separable.

Theorem 4.4 (Existence of Entangled Morphisms):

In Quant, entangled morphisms exist.

Proof: The morphism corresponding to the Bell state (the construction of Theorem 4.1) is entangled. □

§4.4.2 The Structure of the Category of Hilbert Spaces

Compact closed category

Theorem 4.5 (Compact Closedness of Quant):

The quantum category Quant is a compact closed category.

Proof sketch:

We need to prove:

1. Dual objects: every object H has a dual H (the dual space)

2. Evaluation morphism: ε: H ⊗ H → ℂ

3. Coevaluation morphism: η: ℂ → H ⊗ H

4. Triangle identity: (id_H ⊗ ε) ∘ (η ⊗ id_H) = id_H

In finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, these can all be explicitly constructed. □

Philosophical significance:

The compact closed structure allows us to represent quantum processes using graphical calculus:

    H
    |
  ┌─┴─┐
  │ f │
  └─┬─┘
    |
   H'

This graphical language makes the structure of quantum entanglement more intuitive.

The Abramsky-Coecke framework

Abramsky and Coecke proved: quantum mechanics can be fully axiomatized in a compact closed category.

Key theorem:

Theorem 4.6 (Abramsky-Coecke):

The core structures of quantum mechanics (states, evolution, measurement, entanglement) can be defined in any compact closed category.

Corollary:

Quantum entanglement is not a special property of Hilbert spaces, but a universal phenomenon of compact closed categories.

This supports Relational Ontology: entanglement is a manifestation of relational structure and does not depend on a specific material substrate.

§4.4.3 The Deep Connection between Category Theory and Quantum Mechanics

A category-theoretic formulation of quantum logic

Traditional quantum logic (Birkhoff-von Neumann):

  • Propositions correspond to closed subspaces of a Hilbert space
  • Logical operations correspond to lattice operations on subspaces

Category-theoretic quantum logic:

- Propositions correspond to equivalence classes of morphisms

  • Logical operations correspond to composition and tensor product of morphisms

Advantage: the category-theoretic framework naturally incorporates dynamics (composition of morphisms) and compositionality (tensor product).

A category-theoretic formulation of measurement

Definition 4.14 (Measurement):

Measurement is a morphism:

M: H → ⊕ᵢ Hᵢ

where ⊕ is the direct sum and Hᵢ is the subspace corresponding to measurement outcome i.

Key observation:

Measurement is not "revealing a pre-existing value", but establishing a new relation (morphism).

This is consistent with the participatory claim of Relational Ontology: the observer participates in the state of the system through measurement.

A category-theoretic formulation of decoherence

Decoherence is the evolution of an entangled state into a separable state:

entangled state ──environment interaction──> separable state

Category-theoretic formulation:

f: H_S ⊗ H_E → H_S ⊗ H_E (entangled morphism)

↓ (partial trace)

g: H_S → H_S (separable morphism)

Philosophical significance:

Decoherence is not "the disappearance of entanglement", but the transfer of entanglement (from system-environment to a larger system).

Relations do not disappear; they are merely reconfigured. This supports the relational-conservation claim of Relational Ontology.

Quantum field theory can be fully axiomatized in the language of category theory, without the need for Hilbert spaces.

An n-dimensional TQFT is a functor:

Z: Bordₙ → Vect

where Bordₙ is the category of n-dimensional boundaries.

§4.5 Philosophical Significance

§4.5.1 New Insights Brought by Formalization

Insight 1: A precise characterization of intrinsic properties

The vagueness of the natural-language argument:

Volume One used the concept of "intrinsic property" but did not define it precisely. What counts as "intrinsic"? What counts as a "property"?

Clarification through formalization:

intrinsic property = endomorphism End(A)

This definition is:

  • Precise: mathematically explicit
  • Operational: computable
  • Well-grounded: it captures the intuition of "an object acting on itself"

New insight:

An endomorphism is not merely a "property", but the intrinsic structure of an object. In quantum mechanics, End(H) corresponds to the algebra of observables, which is precisely the "intrinsic properties" of a quantum system.

Insight 2: A hierarchy of determination relations

The crudeness of the natural-language argument:

Volume One distinguished only between "determines" and "does not determine".

Refinement through formalization:

There are three levels of determination relations:

1. Weak determination: there exists a function Φ such that f = Φ(e_A, e_B)

2. Strong determination: Φ is a functor (preserving composite structure)

3. Complete determination: Φ is surjective (all morphisms can be represented)

New insight:

Theorem 4.1 denies the weakest determination relation. This makes the argument stronger: even in the most permissive sense, relations cannot be determined by intrinsic properties.

Insight 3: The essence of quantum entanglement

The descriptiveness of the natural-language argument:

Volume One said "entangled states are not separable", but this is only a description.

The explanatory power of formalization:

The essence of entanglement is: the indecomposability of morphisms in a tensor-product category.

Mathematical formulation:

f: H₁ ⊗ H₂ → H₁ ⊗ H₂ cannot be written as f = f₁ ⊗ f₂

New insight:

Entanglement is not a special property of Hilbert spaces, but a universal phenomenon in any monoidal category.

This means: entanglement is a manifestation of relational structure and does not depend on the specific details of quantum mechanics.

Insight 4: The ontological significance of the Yoneda embedding

What the natural-language argument lacked:

Volume One did not make full use of the Yoneda lemma.

What formalization reveals:

The Yoneda embedding Y: C → [C^op, Set] is fully faithful, which means:

Hom(A, B) ≅ Nat(h_A, h_B)

Philosophical interpretation:

The relations between objects (Hom(A, B)) are completely determined by the natural transformations between their "networks of relations" (h_A and h_B).

New insight:

This is the strongest form of the priority of relations: the identity of an object is completely determined by its position in the network of relations.

Insight 5: A precise characterization of emergence

The vagueness of the natural-language argument:

Volume One said "the whole cannot be reduced to its parts", but what does "reduction" mean?

Precisification through formalization:

emergence = a morphism cannot be represented as a composite of local morphisms

Mathematical formulation:

f ≠ f_B ∘ f_A, where f_A and f_B are local morphisms

New insight:

Emergence is not a mysterious "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts", but the non-locality of the composite structure of morphisms.

This can be precisely computed and verified.

§4.5.2 A Formal Critique of Traditional Substance Ontology

A category-theoretic formulation of substance ontology

Substance ontology claims:

1. Objects are fundamental

2. Relations are a derived concept of objects

3. The properties of objects are intrinsic

Category-theoretic formulation:

Assumption 4.1 (Substance Ontology Assumption):

In a category C, all morphisms are completely determined by the endomorphisms of objects:

∀A, B ∈ Ob(C), ∀f ∈ Hom(A, B), ∃Φ, e_A, e_B: f = Φ(e_A, e_B)

Theorem 4.8 (Inconsistency of Substance Ontology):

Assumption 4.1 does not hold in the quantum category Quant.

Proof: A direct corollary of Theorem 4.1. □

Philosophical significance:

Substance ontology is empirically false at the quantum level. This is not a philosophical dispute, but a mathematical theorem.

The limitations of set-theoretic ontology

Set-theoretic ontology:

  • Basic elements: sets (sets of objects)
  • Relations: subsets of the Cartesian product of sets

Problem 1: The derived status of relations

In set theory, a relation R ⊆ A × B is a derived concept.

But in category theory, a morphism f: A → B is a fundamental concept.

Problem 2: Inability to express the composite structure of morphisms

Composition of relations in set theory:

R ∘ S = {(a, c) | ∃b: (a, b) ∈ R ∧ (b, c) ∈ S}

This is merely a set operation and does not capture the structure of morphism composition.

Problem 3: Inability to express the Yoneda embedding

The Yoneda embedding is a functor and cannot be naturally expressed in set theory.

Conclusion:

Set-theoretic ontology is insufficient in expressive power to characterize Relational Ontology.

§4.5.3 The Formal Advantages of Relational Ontology

Advantage 1: Unity

Category theory provides a unified language that can formulate:

  • Mathematical structures (topology, algebra, geometry)
  • Physical theories (quantum mechanics, field theory)
  • Theories of computation (type theory, program semantics)

Relational Ontology applies in all these domains.

Advantage 2: Compositionality

The core of category theory is the composition of morphisms.

This naturally expresses the core claim of Relational Ontology: relations can be composed to form more complex relations.

Advantage 3: Abstraction

The abstractness of category theory is not a defect, but an advantage:

  • It reveals common structures across different domains
  • It avoids being obscured by concrete details
  • It promotes the transfer of insights across domains

Advantage 4: Verifiability

Category theory has mature formalization tools (Coq, Isabelle, Lean).

The core theorems of Relational Ontology can be machine-verified, which is rare in philosophy.

§4.5.4 The Limitations of Formalization

Limitation 1: The cost of abstraction

The abstractness of category theory means:

  • Steep learning curve: it requires a substantial mathematical background
  • Intuition is hard to build: abstract concepts are difficult to visualize
  • Disconnection from everyday experience: difficult to explain to non-specialists

Responses:

  • Provide concrete examples (quantum entanglement, network structures)
  • Use graphical calculus (string diagrams)
  • Emphasize the complementarity of formalization and natural-language argument

Limitation 2: The incompleteness of formalization

Formalization inevitably involves simplification:

Simplification 1: The finite-dimensional assumption

Theorem 4.1 assumes that Hilbert spaces are finite-dimensional.

The infinite-dimensional case (quantum field theory) requires more complex mathematical tools (operator algebras, C*-algebras).

Simplification 2: Idealizing assumptions

We assume:

  • Quantum systems are closed (or can be described by CPTP maps)
  • Measurement is instantaneous
  • There are no gravitational effects

These assumptions may not hold in actual physics.

Simplification 3: Choice of category

We chose a specific category (Quant).

Other choices (such as a subcategory of Hilb) may lead to different conclusions.

Responses:

  • Explicitly state the assumptions
  • Discuss the reasonableness of the assumptions
  • Explore the situation after relaxing the assumptions

Limitation 3: The leap from form to substance

Formalization proves mathematical theorems, but the philosophical interpretation still requires argument:

Question 1: Why is an endomorphism an "intrinsic property"?

We gave reasons (§4.2.2), but this is not a mathematical proof, but a philosophical argument.

Question 2: Why does the irreducibility of morphisms mean "the priority of relations"?

This requires an ontological commitment: we acknowledge that morphisms correspond to "real relations".

Question 3: How are the results of formalization applied to the real world?

The leap from mathematical model to physical reality requires the support of scientific realism.

Responses:

  • Clearly distinguish mathematical theorems from philosophical interpretation
  • Provide independent arguments for the philosophical interpretation
  • Acknowledge the limitations of formalization and avoid overclaiming

Limitation 4: Formalization cannot replace intuition

Formalization provides rigor, but cannot replace understanding:

  • Proofs can be machine-verified, but insights require human understanding
  • Theorems can be formalized, but motivation requires natural-language explanation
  • Structures can be abstracted, but meaning requires philosophical interpretation

Responses:

  • Develop formalization in parallel with natural-language argument
  • Every theorem is accompanied by a philosophical interpretation
  • Emphasize that formalization is a tool, not an end

End-of-Chapter Reflection Questions

Basic comprehension questions:

1. Explain why category theory is better suited than set theory to formulating Relational Ontology.

2. What is an endomorphism? Why can it be regarded as a formalization of "intrinsic property"?

3. Explain the philosophical significance of the Yoneda lemma in your own words.

4. What does Theorem 4.1 prove? What significance does it have for Relational Ontology?

5. What is the category-theoretic formulation of quantum entanglement? How does it differ from the traditional formulation?

In-depth reflection questions:

6. Theorem 4.1 uses the assumption of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. If it is relaxed to infinite dimensions, does the theorem still hold? Why?

7. In classical physical systems, is there a similar "irreducibility of relations"? If so, give an example; if not, explain why not.

8. The full faithfulness of the Yoneda embedding means that "objects are completely determined by relations". Does this mean that objects "do not exist"? How can this extreme interpretation be avoided?

9. Formalization reveals a "hierarchy of determination relations" (weak, strong, complete). What is the philosophical significance of this hierarchy?

10. Theorem 4.8 claims that "substance ontology does not hold at the quantum level". But how might supporters of substance ontology respond?

Critical thinking questions:

11. The formalization in this chapter relies on the identification "endomorphism = intrinsic property". Is this identification open to the charge of circular reasoning? How can it be defended?

12. Theorem 4.1 proves that irreducible relations "exist". But is this sufficient to support "relations are ontologically prior"? What additional argument is needed?

13. Is the category-theoretic formalization overly abstract, to the point of losing contact with physical reality? How can a balance be struck between abstractness and concreteness?

14. This chapter acknowledges that formalization has limitations (§4.5.4). Do these limitations undermine the value of formalization?

15. Are machine-verified proofs more reliable than human proofs? Why?

Applied thinking questions:

16. How can the category-theoretic framework of this chapter be applied to biological systems (such as metabolic networks)?

17. Do relations in social networks also possess "irreducibility"? How can this be formalized?

18. Can the methodology of this chapter (substantive formalization) be applied to ethics? If so, how?

19. How can the framework of this chapter be used to analyze "emergence" in artificial intelligence systems?

20. What implications does the formalization of this chapter have for quantum computing?

Research-oriented thinking questions:

21. Design an experiment that could test the physical predictions of Theorem 4.1.

22. Extend Theorem 4.1 to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. What additional mathematical tools are needed?

23. Formalize "Process Ontology" (the topic of Chapter 5). What mathematical tool should be used?

24. Prove or refute: in the category of phase spaces of classical mechanics, there is a result analogous to Theorem 4.1.

25. Develop a Coq library that can automatically verify "relational irreducibility" in a given category.
