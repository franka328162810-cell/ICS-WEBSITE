II. Types of Logical Relations among the Three Principles

There exist multiple logical relations among the Three Principles, which must be analyzed one by one.

Definition 14.1 (Types of logical relations among principles)

The logical relations among principles can be divided into the following types:

  • Entailment relation: one principle entails another principle
  • Complementary relation: two principles supplement each other and jointly cover a broader domain
  • Independent relation: two principles are mutually independent and each applies to a different domain
  • Conflict relation: two principles come into conflict in certain situations

Proposition 14.1 (The principal relation among the Three Principles is complementarity)

The principal relation among the Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP), the Negentropy Responsibility Principle (PNR), and the Reversibility Principle (PR) is a complementary relation, not an entailment relation or a conflict relation. The Three Principles regulate action from different angles and jointly constitute a complete normative framework.

Argument 14.1 (Argument for the complementary relation among the Three Principles)

Premise P1: RFP concerns the maintenance and expansion of the space of possibilities

Premise P2: PNR concerns the maintenance of negentropy sources and the distribution of entropy costs

Premise P: PR concerns the reversibility of actions and the capacity for error correction

Premise P4: The space of possibilities, negentropy sources, and reversibility are distinct but related concepts

Premise P5: The Three Principles regulate action from different angles respectively

Step 2: From P4, these dimensions are related but not identical

Step : From P5 and Steps 1–2, the Three Principles supplement each other

Step 4: Together the Three Principles cover multiple dimensions of the normativity of action

Conclusion: The principal relation among the Three Principles is a complementary relation

  • The maintenance of negentropy sources provides the material basis for the reversibility of actions

The Three Principles share a single unifying basis: maintaining the conditions for the survival of complex organization. This basis derives from the Three New Views, especially NL-2 (negentropy dependence) and NC-2 (processuality).

Premise P (BP-1): Survival rationality requires maintaining the conditions of survival

Premise P4: RFP maintains the space of possibilities, which is one of the conditions of survival

Premise P5: PNR maintains negentropy sources, which is the material basis of survival

§14.1.2 The Priority of the Three Principles

I. Raising the Question of Priority

Although the Three Principles are principally in a complementary relation, in certain situations it may be necessary to determine their priority.

Definition 14.2 (Priority of principles)

The priority of principles refers to the rule for determining which principle ought to be considered first when multiple principles apply to the same situation.

Proposition 14.6 (The situation-dependence of priority)

The priority of the Three Principles is not absolutely fixed but depends on the concrete situation. In different situations, different principles may hold a priority position.

II. A General Framework for Priority

Definition 14. (Framework for determining priority)

The priority of the Three Principles is determined by the following factors:

  • Urgency: which principle’s protected value faces a more urgent threat?
  • Severity: violation of which principle leads to more serious consequences?
  • Reversibility: violation of which principle has more irreversible consequences?
  • Scope: which principle involves a broader range of subjects?

§14.1.3 Conflict Resolution among the Three Principles

I. Types of Conflict

Definition 14.7 (Conflict among principles)

A conflict among principles refers to a situation in which complying with one principle leads to violating another principle.

Proposition 14.11 (The possibility of conflict among the Three Principles)

Although the Three Principles are principally in a complementary relation, in certain situations conflicts may arise.

Table 14. Types of conflict among the Three Principles

II. General Principles for Conflict Resolution

Definition 14.8 (Principles of conflict resolution)

When the Three Principles come into conflict, the following principles of resolution should be followed:

  • Priority principle: determine priority according to the situation; the priority principle takes precedence
  • Minimal-violation principle: choose the option that violates each principle to the least degree
  • Reconciliation principle: seek a reconciled option that can simultaneously satisfy each principle
  • Compensation principle: if a principle must be violated, compensation should be provided

Proposition 14.12 (Reconciliation takes precedence over trade-off)

In handling conflicts among principles, one should first seek a reconciled option, and engage in trade-off only when reconciliation is impossible.

III. Procedure for Conflict Resolution

Procedure 14.2 (Conflict-resolution procedure)

Step 1: Identify the conflict

  • Clarify which principles are in conflict
  • Assess the nature and degree of the conflict

Step 2: Seek a reconciled option

  • Is there an option that simultaneously satisfies each principle?
  • Can the conflict be reduced by modifying the action?
  • Can it be reconciled through phased implementation?

Step : If reconciliation is impossible, determine priority

  • Determine priority according to the situation
  • Use Procedure 14.1

Step 4: Choose the minimal-violation option

  • On the premise of satisfying the priority principle
  • Choose the option that violates the other principles to the least degree

Step 5: Design compensatory measures

  • If a principle must be violated
  • Design compensatory measures to mitigate the impact of the violation

Step 6: Record the reasons for the decision

  • Explicitly record the process and reasons of conflict resolution
  • Provide reference for similar situations in the future

IV. Case Analysis of Conflict Resolution

Case 14.1 (RFP-PNR conflict: technological development and resource consumption)

Situation: A new technology can greatly expand humanity’s space of possibilities, but its development and deployment require consuming large amounts of negentropy resources.

Conflict analysis:

  • RFP supports: the technology expands the space of possibilities
  • PNR opposes: the technology consumes negentropy resources

Step 1: Identify the conflict

  • Conflict type: RFP-PNR conflict
  • Degree of conflict: medium

Step 2: Seek a reconciled option

  • Option A: Develop a more efficient version of the technology to reduce resource consumption
  • Option B: Deploy in phases, optimizing as deployment proceeds
  • Option C: Develop an alternative technology that uses renewable resources

Step : If reconciliation is impossible, determine priority

  • Situation type: normal situation
  • Priority: RFP ≈ PNR (balance required)

Step 4: Choose the minimal-violation option

  • Choose Option B: phased deployment
  • Reason: it obtains the technological benefits while controlling resource consumption

Step 5: Design compensatory measures

  • Invest in the restoration and development of negentropy sources
  • Set an upper limit on resource consumption
  • Establish monitoring mechanisms

Conclusion: Through phased deployment and compensatory measures, the conflict between RFP and PNR can be reconciled

Case 14.2 (RFP-PR conflict: irreversible expansion of possibilities)

Situation: A gene-editing technology can greatly expand humanity’s biological possibilities, but the application of this technology is irreversible.

Conflict analysis:

  • RFP supports: the technology expands the space of possibilities
  • PR opposes: the application of the technology is irreversible

Step 1: Identify the conflict

  • Conflict type: RFP-PR conflict
  • Degree of conflict: high

Step 2: Seek a reconciled option

  • Option A: Develop a reversible version of the technology
  • Option B: First test on non-human systems
  • Option C: Limit the scope of application and preserve unmodified populations

Step : If reconciliation is impossible, determine priority

  • Situation type: deep-uncertainty situation
  • Priority: PR > RFP

Step 4: Choose the minimal-violation option

  • Choose Option C: limit the scope of application
  • Reason: obtain partial benefits while preserving reversibility

Step 5: Design compensatory measures

  • Establish long-term monitoring mechanisms
  • Preserve unmodified genetic resources
  • Design emergency rollback procedures (if technically feasible)

Conclusion: Under deep uncertainty, PR takes precedence over RFP, and the scope of application of irreversible technologies should be limited

Case 14. (PNR-PR conflict: irreversible environmental protection)

Situation: To protect an endangered ecosystem, irreversible measures must be taken (such as permanently prohibiting human activity in a certain area).

Conflict analysis:

  • PNR supports: protect the negentropy source (the ecosystem)
  • PR opposes: the measures are irreversible

Step 1: Identify the conflict

  • Conflict type: PNR-PR conflict
  • Degree of conflict: medium

Step 2: Seek a reconciled option

  • Option A: Set time-limited protection measures with periodic review
  • Option B: Design adjustable protection levels
  • Option C: Protect the core area while keeping surrounding areas flexible

Step : If reconciliation is impossible, determine priority

  • Situation type: negentropy-source-exhaustion situation
  • Priority: PNR > PR

Step 4: Choose the minimal-violation option

  • Choose Option C: zoned protection
  • Reason: protect the core negentropy source while preserving partial reversibility

Step 5: Design compensatory measures

  • Establish periodic review mechanisms
  • Design procedures for adjusting protection levels
  • Provide alternatives for affected populations

Conclusion: When negentropy sources face exhaustion, PNR takes precedence over PR, but partial reversibility should be preserved as far as possible

The axiom system of cosmic responsibility ethics, CRE-AX, includes the following axioms:

The space of possibilities is the precondition for the realization of value.

PossibilitySpace → Precondition(ValueRealization)

The survival of complex organization depends on the continuous acquisition of negentropy flow.

Survival(ComplexOrganization) → Require(NegEntropyFlow)

All cognitive results are revisable, and the cognitive subject may make mistakes.

∀p: Know(S, p) → Revisable(p)

A rational agent ought to maintain its conditions of survival.

RationalAgent(S) → O(Maintain(SurvivalConditions(S)))

Axiom A5 (Axiom of intersubjectivity):

Principles ought to be adopted that can be reasonably accepted by others.

O(Adopt(Principles) → ReasonablyAcceptable(Principles, Others))

[Normative Axioms]

Axiom N1 (Axiom of recursive freedom):

The space of possibilities ought to be maintained or expanded.

O(Maintain(PossibilitySpace) ∨ Expand(PossibilitySpace))

Negentropy sources ought to be maintained, and entropy costs should not be externalized to innocent agents.

O(Maintain(NegSource) ∧ ¬Externalize(EntropyCost, InnocentAgents))

Axiom N (Axiom of reversibility):

Under deep uncertainty, reversible actions ought to be given priority.

DeepUncertainty → O(Prefer(Reversible))

Axiom N4 (Axiom of forbidden red lines):

There exist action boundaries that are absolutely not to be crossed.

∃FRL: ∀a ∈ FRL: ¬Permissible(a)

The forbidden red lines take precedence over all other principles.

∀P: Priority(FRL) > Priority(P)

∀P ∉ FRL: Revisable(P)

Theorem 14.1 (The independence of the Three Principles)

The Three Principles are mutually independent, that is, each principle can hold or fail to hold independently of the other principles.

Proof:

Construct counterexamples to show that each principle can hold or fail to hold independently of the other principles.

  • Counterexample 1: action satisfies RFP but not PNR (expands the space of possibilities but consumes negentropy)
  • Counterexample 2: action satisfies PNR but not RFP (maintains negentropy but compresses the space of possibilities)
  • Counterexample : action satisfies PR but not RFP (is reversible but compresses the space of possibilities)

Therefore, the Three Principles are mutually independent. □

Theorem 14.2 (The compatibility of the Three Principles)

The Three Principles are mutually compatible, that is, there exist actions that simultaneously satisfy the Three Principles.

Proof:

Construct an action that satisfies the Three Principles.

Let action be “in a reversible manner, use renewable negentropy sources to expand the space of possibilities.”

  • satisfies RFP: it expands the space of possibilities
  • satisfies PNR: it uses renewable negentropy sources
  • satisfies PR: it proceeds in a reversible manner

Therefore, the Three Principles are mutually compatible.

Theorem 14. (The sufficiency of the Three Principles)

Together the Three Principles constitute a sufficient framework for the evaluation of action, that is, any action can be evaluated by the Three Principles.

Proof:

Let be an arbitrary action.

  • has an impact on the space of possibilities (expansion, maintenance, or compression), and can therefore be evaluated by RFP
  • has an impact on negentropy sources (maintenance, neutrality, or damage), and can therefore be evaluated by PNR
  • has a degree of reversibility (high, medium, or low), and can therefore be evaluated by PR

Therefore, any action can be evaluated by the Three Principles.

Premise P2: Completely irreversible catastrophic consequences cannot be made up for by any compensatory measures

Premise P4: Absolute action boundaries are needed to prevent the worst case

Proposition 14.19 (Forbidden red lines in extreme situations)

Even in extreme situations, the forbidden red lines remain absolute. This is because:

  • The forbidden red lines protect the most fundamental values
  • Allowing exceptions would lead to a slippery slope
  • The extreme situation itself may be the result of erroneous judgment

Argument 14.5 (The absoluteness of the forbidden red lines in extreme situations)

Premise P1: The forbidden red lines protect the most fundamental values

Premise P2: Allowing exceptions would lead to a slippery slope

Premise P: Judgment of extreme situations may be erroneous

Premise P4: Epistemic humility requires maintaining doubt about judgments of extreme situations

Step 2: From P2, allowing exceptions would weaken the protective function of the forbidden red lines

Step : From P and P4, judgment of extreme situations is unreliable

Step 4: From Steps 1–, even in extreme situations, the forbidden red lines remain absolute

Conclusion: Extreme situations do not constitute an exception to the forbidden red lines

Proposition 14.20 (Handling conflicts among forbidden red lines)

When two forbidden red lines conflict, one should:

  • If it cannot be avoided, choose the option with the least degree of violation
  • Record the conflict and the reasons for the decision, to provide reference for future revision

Conflicts among forbidden red lines are extremely rare. Their design has already taken mutual compatibility into account. If systematic conflicts among forbidden red lines are discovered in practice, this indicates that the design of the forbidden red lines needs revision.

Definition 14.22 (The deep-time perspective)

The deep-time perspective means that CRE extends ethical concern to the deep-time scale (hundreds of years, thousands of years, or even longer), rather than attending only to the present or the near future.

§14.4.3 Coping with Deep Uncertainty

I. A Review of the Concept of Deep Uncertainty

Definition 14.25 (Deep uncertainty)

Deep uncertainty refers to a situation in which probabilities cannot be assigned to possible outcomes. Under deep uncertainty, the traditional method of expected-utility maximization fails.

II. CRE’s Response to Deep Uncertainty

Proposition 14.27 (CRE’s response to deep uncertainty)

CRE copes with deep uncertainty in the following ways:

  • The Reversibility Principle (PR): give priority to reversible actions and preserve the capacity for error correction
  • The forbidden red lines (FRL): set absolute boundaries to prevent the worst case
  • Incremental decision-making: make decisions in stages, deciding the next step only after evaluating each stage
  • Pluralistic deliberation: reduce cognitive bias through pluralistic deliberation

2. Reversibility-priority principle: give priority to actions with higher reversibility

3. Forbidden-red-line principle: absolutely avoid transgressing the forbidden red lines

The principles of CRE need to be operationalized before they can be applied, but the process of operationalization may introduce errors and biases.

  • The six-dimensional assessment of CSIA requires a large amount of information, which may be incomplete

The application of CRE requires a large amount of information, but in practice information is often incomplete.

The application procedures of CRE require time, but in certain circumstances there may be time pressure, making it impossible to complete a full assessment.

I. Acknowledgment of Validation Limitations

Boundary Declaration 14.6 (Acknowledgment of validation limitations)

CRE acknowledges that it has limitations at the level of validation. As a normative theory, the validation of CRE faces special challenges.

II. Specific Validation Limitations

Limitation One: The difficulty of validating a normative theory

Proposition 14.8 (The difficulty of validating a normative theory)

A normative theory cannot be directly validated through empirical observation the way a descriptive theory can. The validation of CRE needs to rely on the validity, coherence, and practical effects of arguments.

Limitation Two: The unobservability of long-term effects

Proposition 14.9 (The unobservability of long-term effects)

CRE attends to long-term effects, but long-term effects are currently unobservable. They can only be assessed through simulation, reasoning, and historical analogy.

Limitation Three: The unverifiability of counterfactuals

Proposition 14.40 (The unverifiability of counterfactuals)

The assessment of the effects of CRE involves counterfactuals (what would happen if CRE were not adopted), but counterfactuals cannot be directly verified.

Limitation Four: The difficulty of cross-situational validation

Proposition 14.41 (The difficulty of cross-situational validation)

CRE claims to apply to multiple situations, but cross-situational validation is difficult. Certain situations (such as contact with extraterrestrial civilizations) currently cannot be validated.

Axiom System 14.A (Full Version of the Cosmic Responsibility Ethics Axiom System CRE-AX)

[Part One: Basic Axioms]

A1 (Axiom of Possibility Space):

Possibility space is the precondition for the realization of value.

∀v ∈ Values: Realize(v) → Require(PossibilitySpace)

A2 (Axiom of Negentropy Dependence):

The persistence of complex organizations depends on the continuous acquisition of negentropy flows.

∀c ∈ ComplexOrganizations: Survive(c) → Require(NegEntropyFlow(c))

A3 (Axiom of Cognitive Fallibility):

All cognitive results are revisable, and cognitive agents may make mistakes.

∀S ∈ CognitiveAgents, ∀p: Know(S, p) → Revisable(p) ∧ Fallible(S)

A4 (Axiom of Survival Rationality):

Rational agents ought to maintain their conditions of survival.

∀S ∈ RationalAgents: O(Maintain(SurvivalConditions(S)))

A5 (Axiom of Intersubjectivity):

Principles that can be reasonably accepted by others ought to be adopted.

∀P ∈ Principles: Adopt(P) → ReasonablyAcceptable(P, Others)

[Part Two: Normative Axioms]

N1 (Axiom of Recursive Freedom):

Possibility space ought to be maintained or expanded.

O(Maintain(PossibilitySpace) ∨ Expand(PossibilitySpace))

N2 (Axiom of Negentropy Responsibility):

Negentropy sources ought to be maintained, and entropy costs ought not to be externalized onto innocent agents.

O(Maintain(NegSource) ∧ ¬Externalize(EntropyCost, InnocentAgents))

N3 (Axiom of Reversibility):

Under deep uncertainty, reversible actions ought to be preferred.

DeepUncertainty(Sit) → O(Prefer(Reversible(a)))

N4 (Axiom of Forbidden Red Lines):

There exist action boundaries that absolutely must not be crossed.

∃FRL ⊂ A: ∀a ∈ FRL, ∀Sit, ∀Consequence: ¬Permissible(a)

Procedure 14.A (Detailed Version of the CRE Application Procedure)

[Stage One: Situation Analysis]

Step 1.1: Identify the decision situation

Input: description of the decision problem

Output: situation-type label

Checklist:

□ Is there a survival crisis?

□ Is there deep uncertainty?

□ Is there a sharp compression of possibility space?

□ Is there depletion of negentropy sources?

□ Is it a normal situation?

Step 1.2: Identify the relevant agents

Input: description of the decision problem

Output: list of agents

Checklist:

□ Directly affected agents

□ Indirectly affected agents

□ Future generations

□ Other species

□ Possible AI systems

Step 1.3: Assess the degree of uncertainty

Input: description of the decision problem

Output: uncertainty level (risk/uncertainty/deep uncertainty)

Criteria of judgment:

  • Risk: the probability distribution is known
  • Uncertainty: the state space is known, the probabilities are unknown
  • Deep uncertainty: the state space is unknown

Step 1.4: Identify the time scale and the scope of impact

Input: description of the decision problem

Output: time scale (short-term/medium-term/long-term/deep time)

scope of impact (local/regional/global/interstellar)

[Stage Two: Forbidden Red Line Check]

Step 2.1: Check the Forbidden Red Lines one by one

FRL-1 check:

□ Could the action lead to the extinction of any subject type?

□ If so, is it intentional?

FRL-2 check:

□ Could the action lead to large-scale environmental destruction?

□ If so, is it irreversible?

FRL-3 check:

□ Does the action involve cognitive modification?

□ If so, is it irreversible?

□ If so, has consent been obtained?

FRL-4 check:

□ Does the action externalize entropy costs onto future generations?

□ If so, does it reach an extreme degree?

FRL-5 check:

□ Does the action involve deploying a system?

□ If so, is the system shutdownable?

□ If so, is the system rollbackable?

Step 2.2: Forbidden Red Line determination

If any Forbidden Red Line is violated:

Output: the action is unacceptable

Terminate the procedure

If no Forbidden Red Line is violated:

Proceed to the next stage

[Stage Three: Evaluation of the Three Principles]

Step 3.1: RFP evaluation

Dimensions of evaluation:

  • Impact on possibility space (expand/maintain/compress)
  • Degree of impact (slight/moderate/severe)
  • Reversibility of impact (reversible/partially reversible/irreversible)

Indicator used: RFD

Output: RFP score (-1 to +1)

Step 3.2: PNR evaluation

Dimensions of evaluation:

  • Impact on negentropy sources (maintain/neutral/damage)
  • Degree of entropy-cost externalization (none/slight/moderate/severe)
  • Affected agents (present generation/future/other species)

Indicator used: CDI

Output: PNR score (-1 to +1)

Step 3.3: PR evaluation

Dimensions of evaluation:

  • Degree of reversibility of the action (high/medium/low)
  • Cost of rollback (low/medium/high)
  • Time of rollback (immediate/short-term/long-term/impossible)

Indicator used: CRV

Output: PR score (-1 to +1)

[Stage Four: Determination of Priority]

Step 4.1: Determine the weights according to the situation type

If situation type = survival crisis:

w(RFP) = 0.3, w(PNR) = 0.5, w(PR) = 0.2

If situation type = deep uncertainty:

w(RFP) = 0.3, w(PNR) = 0.2, w(PR) = 0.5

If situation type = possibility space compression:

w(RFP) = 0.5, w(PNR) = 0.2, w(PR) = 0.3

If situation type = depletion of negentropy sources:

w(RFP) = 0.3, w(PNR) = 0.5, w(PR) = 0.2

If situation type = normal:

w(RFP) = 0.3, w(PNR) = 0.3, w(PR) = 0.4

Step 4.2: Compute the composite score

CRE(a) = w(RFP) × RFP(a) + w(PNR) × PNR(a) + w(PR) × PR(a)

[Stage Five: Conflict Resolution (if any)]

Step 5.1: Identify conflicts

If the scores of the principles point in opposite directions:

Mark as a conflict

Step 5.2: Search for a reconciliation solution

Checklist:

□ Can the action be modified to reduce the conflict?

□ Can it be implemented in stages?

□ Can compensatory measures be added?

□ Do alternative options exist?

Step 5.3: If reconciliation is impossible, carry out a tradeoff

Carry out the tradeoff according to the priority weights

Choose the option with the highest composite score

[Stage Six: Decision Formation]

Step 6.1: Compare the CRE scores of the options

Step 6.2: Choose the option with the highest CRE score

Step 6.3: Record the reasons for the decision

[Stage Seven: Design of the Rollback Mechanism]

Step 7.1: Identify the trigger conditions for rollback

Checklist:

□ Under what circumstances is rollback needed?

□ What is the threshold for rollback?

□ Who has the authority to decide on rollback?

Step 7.2: Design the rollback procedure

Checklist:

□ What are the specific steps of rollback?

□ How long does rollback take?

□ What resources does rollback require?

Step 7.3: Assign rollback responsibility

Checklist:

□ Who is responsible for monitoring?

□ Who is responsible for deciding?

□ Who is responsible for executing?

Step 7.4: Test the rollback mechanism

Checklist:

□ Has the rollback mechanism been tested?

□ What were the test results?

□ Does it need improvement?

[Stage Eight: Monitoring and Revision]

Step 8.1: Establish a monitoring mechanism

Content of monitoring:

  • The actual consequences of the action
  • Deviation from expectations
  • Newly emerging risks

Step 8.2: Periodic evaluation

Frequency of evaluation: determined by the situation

Content of evaluation:

  • Have the scores of the Three Principles changed?
  • Has a new Forbidden Red Line risk appeared?
  • Does the decision need adjustment?

Step 8.3: Initiate rollback when necessary

Trigger conditions:

  • Serious negative consequences appear
  • Approaching a Forbidden Red Line
  • The scores of the Three Principles deteriorate significantly

Step 8.4: Record and learn

Content to be recorded:

  • The decision process
  • The actual consequences
  • Lessons learned

Uses:

  • Improve future decisions
  • Revise the CRE framework
