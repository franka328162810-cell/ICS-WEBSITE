This is a key chapter of Part Five of Volume Two (the formalization of normativity) and also the core bridge connecting Part Four (the formalization of the New View of Cognition) and Chapters 18 to 20 (the formalization of the three normative principles).

II. The Core Tasks of This Chapter

This chapter answers a fundamental question: how can the derivation from "is" (descriptive) to "ought" (normative) be rigorously characterized within a formal system?

This question is known in the history of philosophy as the "Hume problem" or the "fact–value gap." Hume pointed out in A Treatise of Human Nature that from purely factual statements ("is") one cannot logically derive value judgments ("ought"). This observation has been called by later generations "Hume's Law" or "Hume's Guillotine."

Chapter 6 of Volume One responded to the Hume problem through a three-tier cumulative defense:

1. The transcendental defense: survival is a precondition of value judgment

2. The practical-reason defense: survival is a constitutive commitment of rational agents

3. The thermodynamic defense: survival is a physical necessity of negentropic organization

But the expression of these three tiers of defense at the level of natural language still suffers from the following problems:

  • The formal structure is unclear: what is the logical relation among the three tiers of defense? Are they independent or interdependent?
  • The derivational steps are unclear: how does each tier of defense derive a normative conclusion from descriptive premises? What are the intermediate steps?
  • Consistency is unverified: are the three tiers of defense mutually consistent? Are there hidden contradictions?

The task of this chapter is to resolve these problems through formalization:

Task 1: Construct the formal system BP of bridge principles

  • Define a formal language for descriptive propositions and normative propositions
  • Introduce bridge principles as inference rules connecting the two
  • Establish the axiom system of BP

Task 2: Formalize the three-tier cumulative defense

  • Transform the transcendental defense into a formal theorem
  • Transform the practical-reason defense into a formal theorem
  • Transform the thermodynamic defense into a formal theorem

Task 3: Prove the consistency of the three-tier defense

  • Prove that the three tiers of defense contain no logical contradiction

§17.1 The Philosophical Background of Bridge Principles

§17.1.1 The Classical Formulation of the Hume Problem

Hume writes in A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–1740), Book III, Part I, Section I:

"In every system of morality which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, it is necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it."

This passage points to a fundamental problem: the derivation from purely factual statements ("is") to value judgments ("ought") requires an additional argument, and this argument is missing in most systems of morality.

The Hume problem can be formalized as follows:

Hume's Law:

That is: from purely descriptive premises one cannot logically derive a normative conclusion.

Example:

  • Descriptive premise: "Human beings need food in order to survive"
  • Normative conclusion: "Human beings ought to obtain food"

The derivation from the premise to the conclusion requires an additional bridge principle, such as "Human beings ought to survive." But this bridge principle is itself normative, and so the problem is pushed back one step: how do we justify "Human beings ought to survive"?

§17.1.2 A Survey of Existing Solutions

Responses to the Hume problem in the history of philosophy can be divided into four major categories:

Option One: Deny the fact–value gap (anti-Humeanism)

Representative figures: John Searle, Philippa Foot, Michael Smith

Core claim: certain special factual statements themselves entail normativity.

Searle's example (1964):

1. Jones says: "I promise to pay you 5 dollars" (fact)

2. Jones promises to pay Smith 5 dollars (fact)

3. Jones is obligated to pay Smith 5 dollars (norm)

Searle holds that the derivation from (1) to (3) is valid, because the very concept of "promise" already contains normativity.

Criticism: this option merely hides normativity inside the definitions of certain concepts (such as "promise") and does not genuinely solve the problem.

Option Two: Accept the gap but introduce synthetic bridge principles (moderate naturalism)

Representative figures: Peter Railton, Richard Boyd, David Brink

Core claim: acknowledge the fact–value gap, but hold that the two can be connected by synthetic bridge principles.

For example:

  • Bridge principle: "If X promotes human well-being, then X is good"
  • Factual premise: "Action A promotes human well-being"
  • Normative conclusion: "Action A is good"

Criticism: how is the bridge principle itself justified? If it is analytic, then it is merely a definition; if it is synthetic, then it requires further justification.

Option Three: Reject the objectivity of normativity (non-cognitivism)

Representative figures: A.J. Ayer, Charles Stevenson, Simon Blackburn

Core claim: normative statements are not genuine propositions but expressions of emotion or prescriptions for action, and therefore there is no problem of deriving value from fact.

For example:

  • "You ought to help others" does not state a fact but expresses the emotion or command "Help others!"

Criticism: this option abandons the objectivity of normativity and leads to moral relativism.

Option Four: The transcendental argument (Kantianism)

Representative figures: Immanuel Kant, Christine Korsgaard, Thomas Nagel

Core claim: certain normative principles are preconditions of reason itself, and therefore need not be derived from external facts.

Kant's example:

  • The moral law (such as the categorical imperative) is a constitutive principle of rational agents
  • To reject the moral law is to reject reason itself
  • Therefore, rational agents necessarily acknowledge the moral law

Criticism: the persuasiveness of the transcendental argument depends on a particular understanding of "reason," and this understanding may itself be controversial.

§17.1.3 The Position and Contribution of This Chapter

This chapter adopts a position of plural bridge principles, integrating the merits of the above options:

Position 1: Acknowledge the existence of the fact–value gap

We agree with Hume: from purely descriptive premises one cannot logically derive a normative conclusion. Any such derivation requires additional bridge principles.

Position 2: Introduce multilayer bridge principles

We do not rely on a single bridge principle but construct three tiers of bridge principles, corresponding to three different strategies of justification:

  • BP₁ (the transcendental bridge): draws on Kantianism, deriving normativity from a precondition of reason
  • BP₂ (the practical-reason bridge): draws on the theory of practical reason, deriving normativity from the constitutive commitments of agents
  • BP₃ (the thermodynamic bridge): draws on naturalism, deriving normativity from physical necessity

Position 3: Prove the cumulative effect of the three bridges

We prove that the conjunction of the three-tier bridge principles is stronger than any single-tier bridge principle. Even if one tier of bridge is rejected, the other two tiers still provide support.

Position 4: Preserve openness and revisability

We acknowledge that bridge principles are themselves philosophical commitments rather than logical necessities. Different philosophical positions may reject certain bridge principles. But we provide a clear formal structure such that the cost of such rejection is transparent.

The contributions of this chapter:

1. The first systematic formalization of multilayer bridge principles: the existing literature usually discusses only a single bridge principle, whereas this chapter constructs a complete formal system of three-tier bridge principles.

2. The first proof of the formal consistency of cumulative defense: Chapter 6 of Volume One proposed a three-tier cumulative defense at the level of natural language, and this chapter proves their consistency and cumulative effect for the first time at the formal level.

3. The first integration of bridge principles with deep uncertainty: this chapter proves that under deep uncertainty bridge principles remain valid but require the introduction of a reversibility constraint.

4. The provision of machine-verifiable proofs: all the core theorems have Coq-verified proofs, ensuring formal rigor.

The three-tier bridge principles constructed in this chapter are not value-neutral formal tools but contain substantive philosophical commitments. This section makes these commitments explicit and discusses their defensibility.

Commitment 1: The dichotomy between the descriptive and the normative

Axioms 17.1 and 17.2 establish a strict dichotomy between the descriptive and the normative:

Philosophical implications:

  • This rules out the possibility of "thick concepts"
  • Thick concepts (such as "cruel" and "brave") contain both descriptive and normative components
  • Certain philosophers (such as Hilary Putnam and John McDowell) hold that thick concepts are irreducible

Defense:

We acknowledge the existence of thick concepts but hold that they can be decomposed into descriptive and normative components. For example:

  • "Cruel" = "causing unnecessary suffering" (descriptive) + "this is wrong" (normative)

This decomposition may be imperfect, but it is necessary for formalization.

Commitment 2: The derivation from necessity to normativity

All three tiers of bridge principles involve a derivation from some kind of necessity (precondition, constitutive commitment, physical necessity) to normativity.

Philosophical implications:

  • This presupposes that "necessity has normative force"
  • But why is what is necessary also what ought to be?

Defense:

Our defense relies on the type of necessity:

1. The necessity of a precondition (BP₁): if X is a precondition of Y, and we have an obligation to realize Y, then we have an obligation to maintain X. This is an application of instrumental reason.

2. Constitutive necessity (BP₂): if X is a constitutive commitment of rational agents, then rational agents have an obligation to realize X. This is a requirement of rationality's self-consistency.

3. Physical necessity (BP₃): if X is a physical necessity of negentropic organizations, then negentropic organizations have an obligation to realize X. This is a teleological requirement of functional organization.

Each kind of necessity has its own specific normative basis, and is not simply "necessary, therefore ought."

Commitment 3: The normativity of rationality

BP₂ relies on "rational agents necessarily acknowledge the normativity of their constitutive commitments" (Axiom 17.7).

Philosophical implications:

  • This presupposes that rationality itself has normativity
  • But why is rationality normative?

Defense:

This is the core claim of Kantianism. Our defense has two layers:

1. Conceptual analysis: the concept of rationality already includes normativity. A "rational agent" that does not acknowledge reasons is self-contradictory.

2. Practical unavoidability: even if one questions the normativity of rationality in theory, in practice one cannot avoid relying on rationality. This practical unavoidability is itself a kind of normativity.

Commitment 4: The normativity of function

BP₃ relies on "the purpose of a functional organization generates normativity" (the teleological bridge BP₃′).

Philosophical implications:

  • This presupposes that function has normativity
  • But why does a functional description generate normativity?

Defense:

We adopt a moderate teleological naturalism:

1. Functional definition: a negentropic organization is defined as "a system that maintains a negentropic state"

2. Normative emergence: from the functional definition, the normativity of "ought to maintain the negentropic state" emerges

3. Irreducibility: this normativity cannot be reduced to purely descriptive facts, but it is also not a mysterious metaphysical entity

This position lies between reductive naturalism and non-naturalism.

§17.7.2 Relations to Major Metaethical Positions

Relations to moral realism

Moral realism holds that moral facts exist objectively, independently of human beliefs and attitudes.

Our position:

  • Partially in agreement: we hold that certain normative facts (such as "rational agents ought to survive") are objective
  • Partially different: we do not hold that these normative facts are "independent of" rational agents but rather "constitutively dependent on" rational agents

This makes our position close to constructivist realism.

Relations to constructivism

Constructivism holds that normativity is produced by the constitutive activities of rational agents.

Our position:

  • Highly in agreement: BP₂ explicitly adopts a constructivist position
  • Extension: we extend constructivism to non-rational negentropic organizations (BP₃)

This makes our position a generalized constructivism.

Relations to naturalism

Naturalism holds that normativity can be derived from or reduced to natural facts.

Our position:

  • Partially in agreement: BP₃ adopts a naturalist position
  • Partially different: we do not hold that normativity can be fully reduced to natural facts but rather requires bridge principles

This makes our position a non-reductive naturalism.

Relations to non-cognitivism

Non-cognitivism holds that normative statements are not genuine propositions but expressions of emotion or prescriptions for action.

Our position:

  • Explicit rejection: we hold that normative statements are genuine propositions that can be true or false
  • But we acknowledge: the truth value of a normative statement depends on a particular bridge principle

Summary:

Our metaethical position is a pluralism that integrates:

  • Constructivism (BP₂)
  • Teleological naturalism (BP₃)
  • The transcendental argument (BP₁)

This pluralism is not eclecticism but a recognition that different types of normativity have different sources.

§17.7.3 The Limits of Bridge Principles

Although the three-tier bridge principles provide a powerful argument, they have clear limits.

Limit 1: They cannot persuade the radical skeptic

If someone rejects all three tiers of bridge principles, we cannot "prove" that they are wrong.

Example:

  • The value nihilist rejects BP₁ (denying the normativity of value judgment)
  • The irrationalist rejects BP₂ (denying the normativity of rationality)
  • The anti-teleologist rejects BP₃ (denying the normativity of function)

Our response:

We do not try to "prove" the bridge principles but display the cost of rejecting them:

  • Rejecting BP₁: giving up value judgment
  • Rejecting BP₂: giving up rational agency
  • Rejecting BP₃: denying the laws of physics or functional organization

These costs are so high that in practice it is almost impossible to consistently reject all three tiers of bridges.

Limit 2: Dependence on a particular conceptual understanding

The three-tier bridge principles depend on a particular understanding of concepts such as "rationality," "agency," and "function."

Example:

  • BP₂ relies on "rational agents necessarily acknowledge their constitutive commitments"
  • But if someone has a different understanding of "rationality," they may reject this claim

Our response:

We make explicit the conceptual definitions we use (such as Definition 17.11) and provide a defense of these definitions. Different conceptual understandings may lead to different bridge principles, but our conceptual understanding is well-grounded.

Limit 3: Cultural and historical relativity

The three-tier bridge principles may reflect a particular cultural and historical background (such as the Western philosophical tradition).

Example:

  • Certain Eastern philosophical traditions (such as Buddhism) may question "survival is valuable"
  • Certain postmodern currents of thought may question "the normativity of rationality"

Our response:

We acknowledge cultural pluralism but hold that the three-tier bridge principles have cross-cultural validity:

  • BP₁: value judgment requires the existence of the judge (this is a conceptual truth and does not depend on culture)
  • BP₂: rational agents acknowledge their constitutive commitments (this is a definitional feature of rationality)
  • BP₃: negentropic organizations need to maintain their negentropic state (this is a physical fact)

Even in different cultures, these basic structures hold.

§17.7.4 Open Problems

The formalization of this chapter leaves several open problems:

Open problem 1: The completeness of BP

Conjecture 17.1 claims that BP is complete, but we have not been able to prove it. The completeness proof is an important open problem.

Open problem 2: The minimal set of bridge principles

Are the three-tier bridge principles minimal? Are there fewer bridge principles that could derive the same conclusions?

Open problem 3: The possibility of other bridge principles

Do there exist a fourth or fifth tier of bridge principles? For example:

  • From social contract to normativity (the social-contract bridge)
  • From evolutionary adaptation to normativity (the evolutionary bridge)

Open problem 4: The priority of bridge principles

When different bridge principles produce conflicting normative conclusions (although this does not happen for survival), how is priority determined?

These problems are left for future research.
