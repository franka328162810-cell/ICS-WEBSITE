§3.1 The Limits of the Traditional View of Cognition

§3.1.1 The Problem of Spectator Epistemology

I. The Basic Structure of Spectator Epistemology

The mainstream tradition of Western epistemology understands cognition as a passive reflection of the external world. On this view, the cognizer is like a mirror, whose task is to reflect external reality as accurately as possible. This epistemological position may be called "spectator epistemology".

Definition 3.1 (Spectator Epistemology)

Spectator epistemology is a position about the nature of cognition which holds that:

  • (1) cognition is a passive reflection of the external world;
  • (2) the ideal cognizer is a neutral spectator who does not intervene in the object of cognition;
  • (3) the goal of cognition is to obtain representations that correspond to external reality;
  • (4) there is a clear boundary between the cognizer and the object of cognition.

II. The Internal Difficulties of Spectator Epistemology

Difficulty One: The Theory-Ladenness of Observation

Research in the philosophy of science shows that observation is not a purely passive reception, but is influenced by a theoretical framework. The work of Thomas Kuhn and Norwood Hanson revealed the "theory-ladenness" of observation ...

Proposition 3.1 (The Theory-Ladenness of Observation)

Observation is not a purely passive reception of the external world, but is influenced by the observer's theoretical framework, conceptual scheme, expectations, and background knowledge. There is no "pure" observation unaffected by theory.

Difficulty Two: The Intervention of Measurement

Quantum mechanics reveals the intervention of measurement: at the microscopic level, the act of measurement itself changes the system being measured. This finding challenges the core assumption of spectator epistemology — that the cognizer can observe the world without intervening.

Difficulty Three: The Embodiment of Cognition

Research in contemporary cognitive science shows that cognition does not occur in a "mind" isolated from the world, but is embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive — the so-called "4E cognition".

Proposition 3.2 (The Embodiment of Cognition)

Cognition is not pure information processing occurring in a mind isolated from the world, but a process that is embodied, embedded in an environment, and interacting with the world. The cognizer's body, environment, and action are constitutive parts of cognition.

III. The Failure of Spectator Epistemology under Interstellar Conditions

Failure One: The Impossibility of Cross-Civilizational Cognition

If cognition were passive reflection, then different civilizations should be able to obtain the same knowledge — so long as they observe the same world. Yet different civilizations may have fundamentally different conceptual frameworks, perceptual modes, and cognitive structures. Spectator epistemology cannot handle such fundamental cognitive difference.

Failure Two: The Difficulty of Evaluating AI Cognition

Spectator epistemology assumes a clear boundary between the cognizer and the object of cognition. Yet AI systems blur this boundary: AI is both a cognizer (processing information and making judgments) and an object of cognition (studied and evaluated by humans). More complex still, AI may participate in constructing the human cognitive environment.

Failure Three: The Cognitive Predicament under Deep Uncertainty

Spectator epistemology assumes that there exists a determinate external world waiting to be known. Yet under conditions of deep uncertainty, the "external world" is itself indeterminate — future states depend on present decisions, and present decisions in turn depend on cognition of the future. Proposition 3.3 (The Interstellar Limits of Spectator Epistemology)

Spectator epistemology faces structural failure under interstellar conditions: the fundamental difference of cross-civilizational cognition, the blurred boundaries of AI cognition, and the cognition-action loop under deep uncertainty. These features conflict with the core assumptions of spectator epistemology. The governance of interstellar civilizations needs a participatory view of cognition.

§3.1.2 The Problem of Individualist Epistemology

I. The Basic Structure of Individualist Epistemology

Traditional epistemology focuses mainly on the knowledge states of individual cognizers. Knowledge is defined as an individual's "justified true belief," and the core question of epistemology is how an individual acquires knowledge and how beliefs are justified.

Definition 3.2 (Individualist Epistemology)

Individualist epistemology is a position about the nature of knowledge which holds that:

  • (1) the basic unit of knowledge is the belief state of an individual;
  • (2) the justification of knowledge ultimately depends on the cognitive capacities of the individual;
  • (3) the core question of epistemology is how an individual acquires and justifies knowledge;
  • (4) social factors are secondary or merely instrumental in the constitution of knowledge.

II. The Internal Difficulties of Individualist Epistemology

Difficulty One: The Sociality of Knowledge

Research in contemporary social epistemology shows that knowledge is essentially social:

  • Most knowledge comes from testimony, rather than from an individual's direct experience.
  • Scientific knowledge is the collective achievement of a scientific community, rather than the discovery of an individual scientist.
  • The standards for justifying knowledge are social, depending on the norms of a cognitive community.

Proposition 3.4 (The Sociality of Knowledge)

Knowledge is not merely an individual's belief state, but a social achievement. The acquisition, justification, and transmission of knowledge all depend on social structures, institutions, and norms. Individual knowledge is a node in a social knowledge system, not an independent unit.

Some cognitive achievements are collective, and cannot be reduced to a simple aggregation of individual cognition:

Proposition 3.5 (The Irreducibility of Collective Cognition)

Some cognitive achievements are collective, and cannot be reduced to a simple aggregation of individual cognition. Collective cognition has emergent properties — the cognitive capacity of the whole exceeds the sum of its parts.

Difficulty Three: The Division of Cognitive Labor

Cognition in modern society is highly divided. No individual can master all relevant knowledge; we rely on experts, institutions, and technical systems to extend our cognitive capacities. Individualist epistemology cannot properly handle this division of cognitive labor.

III. The Failure of Individualist Epistemology under Interstellar Conditions

Failure One: The Problem of the Ownership of Cross-Civilizational Knowledge

If human beings establish contact with an extraterrestrial civilization, to whom should the knowledge exchanged by the two sides belong? Individualist epistemology assumes that knowledge belongs to individuals, but cross-civilizational knowledge may be genuinely "shared knowledge" — belonging to no individual or single civilization.

Failure Two: The Problem of the Ownership of AI Knowledge

To whom should the knowledge produced by AI systems belong — to the AI itself, to the AI's designers, to the AI's users, or to the whole human-machine system? Individualist epistemology cannot handle this distributed ownership of knowledge.

Failure Three: The Problem of the Transmission of Intergenerational Knowledge

The timescales of interstellar civilization require considering the transmission of knowledge across many generations. Knowledge exists not only in the minds of present individuals, but also in cultural traditions, institutional structures, and technical systems. Individualist epistemology cannot properly handle this intergenerational existence of knowledge.

Proposition 3.6 (The Interstellar Limits of Individualist Epistemology)

Individualist epistemology faces structural failure under interstellar conditions: the problem of the ownership of cross-civilizational knowledge, the distributedness of AI knowledge, and the transmission of intergenerational knowledge. These features conflict with the core assumptions of individualist epistemology. The governance of interstellar civilizations needs a social view of knowledge.

§3.1.3 The Problem of Value-Neutral Epistemology

I. The Basic Structure of Value-Neutral Epistemology

Traditional epistemology assumes that cognition and value are separate: the goal of cognition is to obtain truth, and truth is value-neutral. The cognizer should exclude value bias and pursue objective knowledge.

Definition 3.3 (Value-Neutral Epistemology)

Value-neutral epistemology is a position about the relation between cognition and value which holds that:

  • (1) the goal of cognition is to obtain truth, and truth is value-neutral;
  • (2) value judgments should not influence factual judgments;
  • (3) the ideal cognizer should exclude value bias;
  • (4) science should be value-neutral.

This position receives its clearest formulation in logical positivism: fact and value are sharply separate; science deals only with facts, and value belongs to a non-cognitive domain.

II. The Internal Difficulties of Value-Neutral Epistemology

Difficulty One: The Value Character of Cognitive Goals

Cognition itself has goals — to obtain truth, understand the world, solve problems. These goals are themselves value-laden. To pursue truth rather than falsehood, and understanding rather than confusion, is a value choice.

Proposition 3.7 (The Value Character of Cognitive Goals)

Cognitive activity itself presupposes values: truth is better than falsehood, understanding is better than confusion, knowledge is better than ignorance. Cognition is not a value-neutral activity, but a value-oriented one.

Difficulty Two: The Value-Ladenness of Research Agendas

The choice of a scientific research agenda is value-laden: what problem we choose to study, what methods we use, and how we allocate resources all involve value judgments.

Type of DecisionValues Involved
Choice of problemWhat problem is worth studying?
Choice of methodWhat method is acceptable?
Allocation of resourcesHow should limited research resources be allocated?

Difficulty Three: The Value Character of Cognitive Norms

Cognitive activity is constrained by norms: we should believe propositions supported by evidence, avoid logical contradiction, and remain open to refutation. These cognitive norms are themselves normative — they tell us how we ought to cognize.

Proposition 3.8 (The Value Character of Cognitive Norms)

Cognitive activity is constrained by norms that tell us how we ought to cognize. Cognitive norms are normative, not purely descriptive. Cognition therefore has a normative dimension.

Formalized statement:

Let be a cognitive activity and a set of cognitive norms.

Good cognition is cognition that conforms to cognitive norms. Cognitive norms are normative.

III. The Failure of Value-Neutral Epistemology under Interstellar Conditions

Failure One: Differences in the Norms of Cross-Civilizational Cognition

Different civilizations may have different cognitive norms. If cognition were value-neutral, there should exist universal cognitive standards. Yet cognitive norms may be culturally relative. Value-neutral epistemology cannot handle this normative pluralism.

Failure Two: The Ethical Dimension of Cognition

Under interstellar conditions, cognitive activity has a profound ethical dimension:

  • Cognition of extraterrestrial life may affect how we treat it.
  • Cognition of AI may affect the rights and status of AI.
  • Cognition of the future may affect our responsibility toward future generations.

Cognition is not value-neutral, but has ethical consequences.

Failure Three: Cognition as Intervention

In a participatory universe (NC-3), cognition is itself a form of intervention. Our cognitive frameworks participate in shaping the world in which we are able to act. Cognition is not a passive reflection of an independent world, but an active construction of the world.

Proposition 3.9 (The Interstellar Limits of Value-Neutral Epistemology)

Value-neutral epistemology faces structural failure under interstellar conditions: differences in the norms of cross-civilizational cognition, the ethical dimension of cognition, and cognition as intervention. These features conflict with the core assumptions of value-neutral epistemology. The governance of interstellar civilizations needs a view of cognition that acknowledges its normativity.

§3.2 The Scientific Basis of the New View of Cognition

§3.2.1 Research on Embodied Cognition

I. The Core Claims of Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition is an important research paradigm in contemporary cognitive science, which challenges the traditional computationalist view of cognition. Its core claim is that cognition is not abstract information processing occurring in a "mind" isolated from the world, but a process that depends on the body, is embedded in an environment, and interacts with the world.

Definition 3.4 (Embodied Cognition)

Embodied cognition is a research paradigm about the nature of cognition which holds that:

  • (1) cognition depends on the body — the body's structure and capacities shape cognition;
  • (2) cognition is embedded in an environment — the environment is a constitutive part of cognition;
  • (3) cognition is action-oriented — cognition serves action, not pure representation;
  • (4) cognition is situated — cognition depends on the concrete situation.

The main evidence from research on embodied cognition:

III. Support from Embodied Cognition for the New View of Cognition

Research on embodied cognition provides an important scientific basis for the New View of Cognition:

Support One: The Participatory Character of Cognition

Embodied cognition shows that cognition is not passive information reception, but an active process of interacting with the world. The cognizer participates in the world through action, rather than merely spectating it. This supports NK-1 (participatory cognition).

Support Two: The Situatedness of Cognition

Embodied cognition shows that cognition depends on the concrete situation. There is no "pure cognition" detached from a situation. This means that cognitive standards may be situation-relative, supporting NK-4 (cognitive humility and negotiability).

Support Three: The Distributedness of Cognition

The extended-mind argument shows that cognition can be distributed across the brain, body, and environment. This provides a basis for understanding social knowledge, supporting NK-2 (social knowledge).

Proposition 3.11 (Support from Embodied Cognition for the New View of Cognition)

Research on embodied cognition provides a scientific basis for the New View of Cognition: the participatory character of cognition supports NK-1, the situatedness of cognition supports NK-4, and the distributedness of cognition supports NK-2. Embodied cognition challenges the core assumptions of spectator epistemology.

§3.2.2 Research on Social Cognition

I. The Core Findings of Social Cognition

Research on social cognition reveals the social dimension of cognition: human cognition is essentially social, depending on social interaction, social structures, and social norms.

Definition 3.5 (Social Cognition)

Social cognition is the field of research on how human beings understand and interact with the social world, including:

  • (1) theory of mind — the capacity to understand others' mental states;
  • (2) social learning — the capacity to learn by observing others;

§3.2.3 Predictive Processing Theory

I. The Core Claims of Predictive Processing

Predictive processing theory is an important theoretical framework in contemporary cognitive science and neuroscience, developed by Karl Friston, Andy Clark, Jakob Hohwy, and others.

Definition 3.7 (Predictive Processing)

Predictive processing is a theory of brain function which holds that:

  • (1) the brain is a prediction machine — it continuously generates predictions about sensory input;
  • (2) perception is prediction-error minimization — the goal of perception is to minimize the error between prediction and actual input;
  • (3) cognition is active inference — cognition is not passive reception, but active inference;
  • (4) action is the realization of prediction — action is the way of making the world conform to prediction.

§3.3.1 NK-1: Participatory Cognition

I. The Statement of the Proposition

Proposition NK-1 (Participatory Cognition)

Cognition is not the passive reflection of a pre-existing reality, but a participatory construction in which the cognizer and the environment interact. Cognitive frameworks participate in shaping the knowable reality.

Natural-language statement:

The proposition of participatory cognition holds that, when we ask "what is cognition," the answer should not be "a mirror reflection of the external world," but "a constructive process in which the cognizer and the world interact." The cognizer is not a spectator standing outside the world, but a participant embedded in the world and interacting with it. Cognitive frameworks — the ones we use to understand ...

Formalized statement:

Let be the cognizer, the environment, the content of cognition, and the cognitive framework.

Spectator epistemology holds:

that is, the content of cognition is determined solely by the environment.

NK-1 holds:

that is, the content of cognition is determined jointly by the cognizer, the environment, and the cognitive framework.

More strongly:

that is, the cognitive framework participates in shaping the knowable environment .

II. The Argument for the Proposition

Argument One: From NC-3 to NK-1

NC-3 (participation), established in Chapter 1, holds that the universe is participatory — the observer participates in constructing reality. NK-1 is the epistemological expression of NC-3: if the universe is participatory, then cognition, as a form of observation, is participatory too.

Argument Two: From Embodied Cognition to NK-1

Research on embodied cognition shows that cognition depends on the body and the environment, and that the cognizer participates in the world through action. This supports NK-1: cognition is not passive reflection, but active participation.

Argument Three: From Predictive Processing to NK-1

Predictive processing theory shows that perception is a process in which the brain actively generates predictions and matches them against sensory input. The world we "see" is partly determined by the brain's predictions. This supports NK-1: the content of cognition is partly determined by the cognizer.

Argument Four: From Theory-Ladenness to NK-1

Research in the philosophy of science shows that observation is theory-laden. What we see depends in part on what we believe. This supports NK-1: cognitive frameworks participate in shaping the content of cognition.

Argument Five: From Quantum Measurement to NK-1

The measurement problem in quantum mechanics shows that, at the microscopic level, the act of measurement changes the system being measured. This is the physical-level manifestation of participatory cognition.

III. The Implications of the Proposition

Implication One: Cognitive Responsibility

If cognition is participatory, then the cognizer bears responsibility for their cognitive activity. Cognition is not something that happens to the cognizer, but something the cognizer does. The cognizer can choose different cognitive frameworks, and is therefore responsible for their choice.

Implication Two: The Constructive Character of Cognition

If cognitive frameworks participate in shaping knowable reality, then different cognitive frameworks may lead to different "realities." This is not to say that reality is arbitrary, but that the way reality presents itself depends on the cognitive framework.

Implication Three: The Practical Character of Cognition

If cognition is participatory, then cognition and action are unified. Cognition is not a purely theoretical activity, but part of practical activity.

Argument Four: From Scientific Knowledge to NK-2

If knowledge is social, then knowledge is essentially public, not private. Knowledge is not the private property of individual minds, but the public resource of a cognitive community.

Implication Two: The Importance of Cognitive Institutions

If knowledge depends on social validation, then cognitive institutions (such as peer review, academic publishing, and educational systems) are crucial to the production of knowledge. Good cognitive institutions promote the production of knowledge; bad ones hinder it.

Implication Three: Epistemic Justice

If knowledge is social, then there are questions of justice in cognitive practice. Some groups may be systematically excluded from the production of knowledge, or their testimony improperly devalued. Epistemic justice requires correcting these injustices.

§3.3.3 NK-3: Normative Cognition

I. The Statement of the Proposition

Proposition NK-3 (Normative Cognition)

Cognition is not only descriptive (how we do cognize), but also normative (how we ought to cognize). Cognitive activity is constrained by norms, and the cognizer bears responsibility for their cognitive activity.

Natural-language statement:

The proposition of normative cognition holds that cognition is not a value-neutral activity, but a normative one. Cognition admits of better and worse: some ways of cognizing are good (such as following evidence, remaining consistent, and being open to refutation), while others are bad (such as ignoring evidence, contradicting oneself, and being stubborn). The cognizer ought to pursue good ways of cognizing and avoid ...

Formalized statement:

Let be a cognitive activity, a set of cognitive norms, and cognitive responsibility.

Value-neutral epistemology holds:

NK-3 holds:

that is, cognitive activity has a normative character.

More concretely:

that is, good cognition is cognition that conforms to cognitive norms, and conformity to cognitive norms entails cognitive responsibility.

II. The Argument for the Proposition

Argument One: From Cognitive Goals to NK-3

Cognitive activity has goals — to obtain truth, understand the world, solve problems. These goals are themselves value-laden. To pursue truth rather than falsehood is a value choice. Therefore cognitive activity is value-oriented and has a normative character.

Argument Two: From Cognitive Norms to NK-3

Cognitive activity is constrained by norms: we should believe propositions supported by evidence, avoid logical contradiction, and remain open to refutation. These norms tell us how we ought to cognize, and are therefore normative.

Argument Three: From Cognitive Evaluation to NK-3

We routinely evaluate cognitive activity: someone's reasoning is good or bad, someone's belief is reasonable or unreasonable, someone's judgment is wise or foolish. Such evaluation presupposes the normativity of cognition.

Argument Four: From Cognitive Responsibility to NK-3

We hold cognizers responsible for their cognitive activity: if someone believes a proposition unsupported by evidence, we criticize them; if someone ignores an obvious refutation, we blame them. This attribution of responsibility presupposes the normativity of cognition.

The New View of Cognition and social epistemology share the following core claims:

Common Ground One: The Sociality of Knowledge

Both hold that knowledge is essentially social, rather than a purely individual achievement. The acquisition, validation, and transmission of knowledge all depend on social processes.

Common Ground Two: The Importance of Cognitive Institutions

Both emphasize the importance of cognitive institutions (such as peer review, academic publishing, and educational systems) to the production of knowledge.

Common Ground Three: The Sociality of Cognitive Norms

Both acknowledge that cognitive norms are social, depending on the practices and standards of a cognitive community.

Difference One: Ontological Basis

Social epistemology usually does not concern itself with ontological questions, whereas the New View of Cognition is built on the ontological basis of the New Cosmology (NC). Difference Two: The Participatory Dimension

The New View of Cognition emphasizes the participatory character of cognition (NK-1), a dimension that is not a central concern of social epistemology. The New View of Cognition holds that cognitive frameworks participate in shaping knowable reality, which goes beyond the usual scope of social epistemology.

Difference Three: The Interstellar Context

The New View of Cognition is explicitly directed at the interstellar context, considering cross-civilizational cognition, AI cognition, and deep uncertainty. Social epistemology mainly concerns knowledge questions within human society.

Proposition 3.18 (The Relation between the New View of Cognition and Social Epistemology)

The New View of Cognition and social epistemology share the core claim of the sociality of knowledge, but the New View of Cognition has a deeper ontological basis (NC), a stronger participatory dimension (NK-1), and a broader scope of application (the interstellar context). The New View of Cognition can be seen as an extension and deepening of social epistemology under interstellar conditions.

§3.4.2 The Relation to Virtue Epistemology

I. The Core Claims of Virtue Epistemology

Virtue epistemology is another important branch of contemporary epistemology, shifting the focus of epistemology from belief to the character of the cognizer. Its main representatives include Ernest Sosa, Linda Zagzebski, and others.

Definition 3.8 (Virtue Epistemology)

Virtue epistemology is an epistemological position which holds that:

  • (1) the central object of cognitive evaluation is the character of the cognizer, not merely belief;
  • (2) knowledge is the manifestation of cognitive virtue;
  • (3) there exist cognitive virtues (such as openness, caution, and honesty) and cognitive vices (such as stubbornness, credulity, and self-deception);
  • (4) the cognizer should cultivate cognitive virtues.

The two main branches of virtue epistemology:

Responsibilist virtue epistemologyZagzebski, CodeCognitive virtue is a trait of character

II. What the New View of Cognition and Virtue Epistemology Share

Common Ground One: The Normativity of Cognition

Both hold that cognition is normative, and that there are good and bad ways of cognizing. NK-3 (normative cognition) agrees with the core claim of virtue epistemology.

Common Ground Two: Cognitive Responsibility

Both hold that the cognizer bears responsibility for their cognitive activity. The cognizer can be praised or criticized for their cognitive activity.

Common Ground Three: The Importance of Cognitive Character

Both emphasize the importance of cognitive character (cognitive virtue). A good cognizer not only holds correct beliefs, but also has a good cognitive character.

III. Where the New View of Cognition and Virtue Epistemology Differ

Difference One: The Social Dimension

Virtue epistemology mainly concerns the character of individual cognizers, whereas the New View of Cognition emphasizes the sociality of knowledge (NK-2). The New View of Cognition holds that cognitive virtue is not only an individual trait of character, but also a social practical capacity.

Difference Two: The Participatory Dimension

The New View of Cognition emphasizes the participatory character of cognition (NK-1): the cognizer participates in constructing knowable reality. This dimension is not a central concern of virtue epistemology.

Difference Three: The Negotiability Dimension

The New View of Cognition emphasizes the negotiability of cognitive standards (NK-4), whereas virtue epistemology usually assumes that there exist universal standards of cognitive virtue.

Proposition 3.19 (The Relation between the New View of Cognition and Virtue Epistemology)

The New View of Cognition and virtue epistemology share the core claim of the normativity of cognition, but the New View of Cognition adds the social dimension (NK-2), the participatory dimension (NK-1), and the negotiability dimension (NK-4). The New View of Cognition can be seen as a socialized and situated extension of virtue epistemology.

§3.4.3 The Relation to Pragmatist Epistemology

I. The Core Claims of Pragmatist Epistemology

Pragmatist epistemology stems from the American pragmatist tradition; its main representatives include Charles Peirce, William James, and John Dewey.

Definition 3.9 (Pragmatist Epistemology)

Pragmatist epistemology is an epistemological position which holds that:

  • (1) knowledge is a tool for action, not a mirror reflection of reality;
  • (2) truth is "what works" — a belief that works in practice is true;
  • (3) cognition is a process of problem-solving, not a purely theoretical activity;
  • (4) cognition and action are unified.

Dewey's critique of the "spectator theory of knowledge" is a central contribution of pragmatist epistemology. Dewey holds that cognition is not a passive reflection of a pre-existing reality, but an active inquiry interacting with the environment.

II. What the New View of Cognition and Pragmatist Epistemology Share

Common Ground One: Anti-Spectator Epistemology

Both oppose spectator epistemology, holding that cognition is participatory and practical. NK-1 (participatory cognition) agrees with Dewey's critique.

Common Ground Two: The Unity of Cognition and Action

Both hold that cognition and action are unified, and that cognition serves practical ends.

Common Ground Three: The Situatedness of Cognition

Both emphasize the situatedness of cognition: cognition depends on the concrete problem situation.

III. Where the New View of Cognition and Pragmatist Epistemology Differ

Difference One: Ontological Basis

The New View of Cognition is built on the ontological basis of the New Cosmology (NC), whereas pragmatist epistemology usually avoids ontological questions or takes an anti-ontological stance.

Difference Two: The Degree of Emphasis on Sociality

Although Dewey emphasizes the sociality of cognition, the New View of Cognition's emphasis on the sociality of knowledge is more systematic (NK-2).

Difference Three: The Interstellar Context

The New View of Cognition is explicitly directed at the interstellar context, considering cross-civilizational cognition, AI cognition, and so on. Pragmatist epistemology mainly concerns the context of human practice.

Proposition 3.20 (The Relation between the New View of Cognition and Pragmatist Epistemology)

The New View of Cognition and pragmatist epistemology share the core position of anti-spectator epistemology, but the New View of Cognition has a clearer ontological basis (NC), a more systematic emphasis on sociality (NK-2), and a broader scope of application (the interstellar context). The New View of Cognition can be seen as a development of pragmatist epistemology under interstellar conditions.
