§22.1　The Philosophical Foundations of Measurement

§22.1.1　What Is Measurement?

Definition 22.1 (intuitive definition of measurement)

Measurement is the process of mapping properties of the empirical world onto mathematical structures.

This intuitive definition contains three elements:

1. Properties of the empirical world: such as length, mass, temperature, degree of consciousness, size of the space of possibilities

2. Mathematical structure: usually the set of real numbers and its operations

3. Mapping process: a systematic correspondence from properties to numbers

But this intuitive definition immediately raises profound philosophical questions:

Question Q22.1: Are numbers really “in” empirical objects? Or do we “assign” numbers to objects?

Question Q22.2: What kind of mapping counts as “measurement”? Does any arbitrary numerical assignment count?

Question Q22.3: How is the objectivity of measurement guaranteed? How are the measurements of different observers coordinated?

These questions constitute the philosophical foundations of measurement theory.

§22.1.2　The Debate between Measurement Realism and Constructivism

With regard to the ontological status of measurement, there are two opposing positions:

Position One: Measurement Realism

Measurement realism holds: the property being measured exists independently of the measurement process, and measurement is “discovery” rather than “invention.”

Argument 22.1 (the argument for measurement realism)

Premise P1: Physical objects have objective properties (such as mass, length)

Premise P2: These properties exist prior to measurement

Premise P3: Measurement is an approximate representation of these properties

Conclusion C: Measurement is objective and does not depend on the observer

Position Two: Measurement Constructivism

Measurement constructivism holds: measurement is a conceptual framework constructed by humans, and numbers are a structure we impose on the world.

Argument 22.2 (the argument for measurement constructivism)

Premise P1: Different cultures have different measurement systems (such as imperial vs. metric)

Premise P2: The choice of measurement units is arbitrary

Premise P3: The measurability of certain properties (such as “intelligence”) is disputed

Conclusion C: Measurement is a social construction and depends on human practice

The Position of This Work: Critical Realism

We adopt an intermediate position:

Proposition 22.1 (critical realism about measurement)

Measurement is neither pure discovery nor pure invention, but construction under empirical constraints.

Formalized formulation:

Concretely:

1. Empirical constraints: the property being measured must have a stable manifestation in the empirical world

2. Conceptual construction: the measurement framework (units, zero point, scale type) is constructed by humans

3. Intersubjectivity: the validity of measurement depends on intersubjective repeatability

This position is consistent with the participatory cognition of the New View of Cognition (NK): measurement is the participatory interaction of a cognitive subject with the world.

§22.3　The Formalization of Scale Types

§22.3.1　Stevens's Classification of Scales

The psychologist S.S. Stevens (1946) proposed the classic fourfold classification of scales. We formalize and extend it on this basis.

Definition 22.11 (nominal scale)

A nominal scale is used only for classification and does not involve relations of magnitude.

Formalization: relational system , containing only an equivalence relation.

Admissible transformations: any one-to-one mapping (bijection)

Example 22.4: Player numbers, gender encoding (1=male, 2=female)

Permissible operations: frequency counts, mode

Impermissible operations: addition, average

Definition 22.12 (ordinal scale)

An ordinal scale represents the order of magnitude, but not the size of differences.

Formalization: relational system , containing a total order relation.

Admissible transformations: strictly monotone increasing functions

Example 22.5: Hardness grades (Mohs hardness), educational level (primary < secondary < university)

Permissible operations: median, percentiles, rank correlation

Impermissible operations: addition, average (arithmetic mean)

Definition 22.13 (interval scale)

An interval scale represents the size of differences, but the zero point is arbitrary.

Formalization: relational system , where is a difference operation.

Admissible transformations: positive affine transformations

Example 22.6: Temperature (Celsius, Fahrenheit), calendar years

Permissible operations: addition, subtraction, arithmetic mean, standard deviation

Impermissible operations: multiplication, ratios (“today is twice as hot as yesterday” is meaningless)

Key feature: the zero point is conventional and does not mean “none”

Definition 22.14 (ratio scale)

A ratio scale has an absolute zero point, representing the “absence” of the property.

Formalization: relational system , satisfying the axioms of Theorem 22.2.

Admissible transformations: similarity transformations

Example 22.7: Length, mass, time interval, energy

Permissible operations: all arithmetic operations, geometric mean, coefficient of variation

Key feature: the zero point is absolute, and ratios are meaningful (“A is twice as long as B”)

§22.3.2　Extended Classification of Scale Types

Besides Stevens's four categories, modern measurement theory identifies other types:

Definition 22.15 (absolute scale)

An absolute scale has no arbitrariness; there is only one way of representing it.

Admissible transformations: the identity transformation

Example 22.8: Counting (number of objects), probability

Definition 22.16 (log-interval scale)

A log-interval scale is an interval scale on a logarithmic scale.

Admissible transformations: power transformations

Example 22.9: Loudness (decibels), earthquake intensity (Richter magnitude)

§22.3.4　Determining the Scale Type

Question Q22.5: Given a measurement system, how is its scale type to be determined?

Determination procedure:

Step 1: Check whether a meaningful zero point exists

  • Yes → possibly a ratio scale or stronger
  • No → at most an interval scale

Step 2: Check whether a meaningful addition operation exists

  • Yes → possibly a ratio scale
  • No → at most an interval scale

Step 3: Check whether differences are meaningful

  • Yes → at least an interval scale
  • No → at most an ordinal scale

Step 4: Check whether only order relations exist

  • Yes → ordinal scale
  • No → check whether only classification exists
  • Yes → nominal scale

Theorem 22.7 (necessary and sufficient conditions for scale types)

Let be a relational system and a representation function. Then:

1. is a ratio scale meaningful zero point and addition operation exist

2. is an interval scale a meaningful difference operation exists but the zero point is arbitrary

3. is an ordinal scale only order relations are meaningful

4. is a nominal scale only equivalence relations are meaningful

Proof: follows directly from the properties of admissible transformations ∎

§22.5　Philosophical Reflections on Measurement Theory

§22.5.1　The Ontological Commitments of Measurement

What ontological commitments does measurement theory implicitly make?

Commitment O1: The reality of properties

Measurement presupposes that the property being measured is, in some sense, “real.” But what is the nature of this reality?

  • Strong realism: properties exist independently of the observer (such as mass, length)
  • Weak realism: properties depend on the observer but have intersubjectivity (such as color, temperature)
  • Constructivism: properties are social constructions (such as intelligence, beauty)

The position of this work (critical realism): the reality of properties is a matter of degree, depending on the strength of empirical constraints.

Commitment O2: The applicability of mathematical structures

Measurement presupposes that mathematical structures (such as the real numbers, order relations) can represent the empirical world. But why is mathematics “unreasonably effective” (Wigner)?

The response of this work: mathematical structures are neither “discovered” nor “invented,” but the product of the interaction between human cognition and the world (see the New View of Cognition NK-2).

Commitment O3: Discrete and continuous

The real numbers are continuous, but the empirical world may be discrete (quantized). How does measurement handle this tension?

The response of this work: the use of continuous mathematics in measurement is an effective approximation that holds at the macroscopic scale.

§22.5.2　The Epistemological Limitations of Measurement

What inherent epistemological limitations does measurement theory have?

Limitation E1: Measurement changes the object measured

In quantum mechanics, measurement inevitably disturbs the system (Heisenberg's uncertainty principle). In the social sciences, measurement may also change the object measured (the Hawthorne effect).

Formalization: let be the system state and the measurement operation, then:

The state after measurement differs from that before measurement.

Impact on this work: the measurement of UCS may affect the consciousness state of the subject measured; the assessment of MBCL may affect the spread of memes.

Limitation E2: Immeasurability

Certain properties are in principle immeasurable:

  • Logical immeasurability: self-referential properties (such as “the truth value of this sentence”)
  • Physical immeasurability: events beyond the observational horizon
  • Practical immeasurability: excessively high computational complexity

Impact on this work: certain normative properties (such as “genuine free will”) may be immeasurable and require proxy indicators.

Limitation E3: The theory-ladenness of measurement

Measurement is not theory-neutral, but depends on a background theory (the Duhem-Quine thesis).

Example 22.18: The measurement of temperature depends on thermodynamic theory; the measurement of UCS depends on a theory of consciousness.

Impact on this work: the validity of the indicators depends on the correctness of the Three New Views. If the Three New Views are refuted, the indicator system needs to be reconstructed.

§22.5.3　The Ethical Dimension of Measurement

Measurement is not only an epistemological issue, but also an ethical one.

Ethical Issue Eth1: Power relations in measurement

Who has the right to define measurement standards? How does measurement reinforce or challenge power structures?

Example 22.19: IQ tests were once used for racial discrimination; GDP as a development indicator ignores the environment and well-being.

Impact on this work: the design of the indicator system must take power relations into account and avoid reinforcing injustice.

Ethical Issue Eth2: The normative impact of measurement

Measurement not only describes the world, but also shapes it (Goodhart's law).

```python
not measurement_system.has_difference):

# Verify the order relation

if verify_order_properties(measurement_system):

return "ordinal"

# Default to a nominal scale

if measurement_system.has_equality:

return "nominal"

raise ValueError("Cannot determine the scale type")

def verify_addition_axioms(system):

"""Verify the addition axioms (associativity, monotonicity, the Archimedean property)"""

# Associativity

associativity = check_associativity(system)

# Monotonicity

monotonicity = check_monotonicity(system)

# Archimedean property

archimedean = check_archimedean_property(system)

return associativity and monotonicity and archimedean

def verify_difference_properties(system):

"""Verify the properties of the difference"""

# Symmetry of the difference: d(a,b) = -d(b,a)

symmetry = check_difference_symmetry(system)

# Transitivity of the difference: d(a,c) = d(a,b) + d(b,c)

transitivity = check_difference_transitivity(system)

return symmetry and transitivity

def verify_order_properties(system):

"""Verify the properties of the order relation"""

# Completeness

completeness = check_completeness(system)

# Transitivity

transitivity = check_transitivity(system)

return completeness and transitivity

```

End-of-Chapter Notes

Note 1: The historical development of measurement theory

The development of measurement theory as an independent discipline can be traced back to:

  • 1946: Stevens proposed the classification of scales (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio)
  • 1951: Campbell's Measurement in Physics laid the philosophical foundations of physical measurement
  • 1971: The three-volume Foundations of Measurement by Krantz et al., establishing the representational theory
  • 1993: ISO issued the GUM (Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement)
  • 2008: The rise of Bayesian measurement theory

This chapter synthesizes these developments and extends them to ethical measurement.

Note 2: Measurement and quantum mechanics

Quantum measurement theory reveals the profound nature of measurement:

  • Measurement disturbance: measurement inevitably changes the state of the system
  • Complementarity: certain properties cannot be measured simultaneously with precision (the uncertainty principle)
  • Context dependence: measurement results depend on the measuring apparatus

These insights are consistent with the Participatory Universe (PU) of the New Cosmology.

Note 3: The social construction of measurement

The sociology of science (such as Latour, Woolgar) emphasizes the social constructedness of measurement:

  • Measurement standards are the result of negotiation within the scientific community
  • Measurement practice is embedded in social power relations
  • The design of measurement tools reflects particular values

The critical-realist position of this chapter acknowledges these social dimensions without going to extreme relativism.

Note 4: Cross-cultural measurement

Research in cross-cultural psychology shows that certain measurement concepts are culturally specific:

  • The concept of intelligence is understood differently in different cultures
  • The classification of emotions differs across languages
  • The dimensions of moral judgment may vary across cultures

The indicator system of this work needs to remain vigilant in cross-cultural application.

Note 5: AI and measurement

The impact of AI technology on measurement theory:

  • Automated measurement: AI can automate complex measurement processes
  • New objects of measurement: AI systems themselves become objects of measurement (such as applying UCS to AI)
  • The democratization of measurement: AI lowers the technical threshold of measurement
  • New challenges: the black-box nature of AI brings problems of interpretability

Volume Four will discuss in detail the application of AI in measurement.
