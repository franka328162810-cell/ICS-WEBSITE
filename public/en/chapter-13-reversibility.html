§13.1.1 From the New View of Cognition to Epistemic Humility

I. Review of the Core Propositions of the New View of Cognition

Chapter 3 established the four core propositions of the New View of Cognition. Before deriving the Reversibility Principle, it is necessary to review the core content of these propositions, especially NK-1 (participatory construction) and NK-4 (negotiability).

Proposition NK-1 (participatory construction):

Cognition is not a passive reflection of an external world independent of the cognizer, but a product of the interaction between the cognizer and the world. The cognitive process participates in constructing its object of cognition.

Formal statement:

Proposition NK-2 (sociality):

Cognition is a social achievement. The production, validation, and dissemination of knowledge depend on the social practices of an epistemic community.

Formal statement:

Proposition NK-3 (normativity):

Cognitive activity is intrinsically normative. Cognition concerns not only “what is” but also “how one ought to cognize.”

Formal statement:

Proposition NK-4 (negotiability):

Cognitive results are negotiable and revisable. No cognitive result is absolutely certain or unrevisable.

II. The Concept of Epistemic Humility

Definition 13.1 (epistemic humility)

Epistemic Humility is the attitude of recognizing the limits of one’s own cognitive capacities and acknowledging that one’s beliefs may be mistaken, incomplete, or in need of revision.

Formal statement:

Proposition 13.1 (the New View of Cognition entails epistemic humility)

From the core propositions of the New View of Cognition, especially NK-1 (participatory construction) and NK-4 (negotiability), it can be derived that epistemic humility is an attitude that a rational agent ought to have.

Argument 13.1 (the argument that the New View of Cognition entails epistemic humility)

Premise P1 (NK-1): cognition is participatory construction, not a passive reflection of an external world

Premise P2 (NK-4): all cognitive results are revisable

Premise P3: if cognition is participatory construction, then cognitive results depend on the cognizer’s perspective and method

Premise P4: if cognitive results depend on perspective and method, then different perspectives and methods may produce different results

Premise P5: if all cognitive results are revisable, then no cognitive result is absolutely certain

Step 1: from P1 and P3, cognitive results depend on the cognizer’s perspective and method

Step 2: from Step 1 and P4, different perspectives and methods may produce different results

Step 3: from P2 and P5, no cognitive result is absolutely certain

Step 4: from Step 2 and Step 3, the cognizer ought to acknowledge the limits of their own cognition

Step 5: from Step 4, epistemic humility is an attitude that a rational agent ought to have

Conclusion: the New View of Cognition entails epistemic humility

III. Deep Uncertainty and Epistemic Humility

Definition 13.2 (deep uncertainty)

Deep Uncertainty is uncertainty that cannot be described by a probability distribution. Under deep uncertainty, we not only do not know what the outcomes are, but we do not even know what the space of possible outcomes is, nor can we assign probabilities to the possible outcomes.

Formal statement:

Proposition 13.2 (the ubiquity of deep uncertainty)

In situations involving complex systems, long time scales, and multi-agent interaction, deep uncertainty is ubiquitous.

Sources of deep uncertainty:

  • System complexity: the behavior of complex systems is hard to predict
  • Time scale: prediction over long time scales faces cumulative error
  • Multi-agent interaction: the decisions of multiple agents influence one another, producing unpredictable emergent behavior
  • Cognitive limits: inherent limits of human cognitive capacity
  • Model uncertainty: the models we use to understand the world may themselves be wrong

Proposition 13.3 (deep uncertainty reinforces epistemic humility)

The existence of deep uncertainty further reinforces the necessity of epistemic humility. Under deep uncertainty, we ought to acknowledge not only that we may be wrong, but also that we do not even know where we may be wrong.

Argument 13.2 (the argument that deep uncertainty reinforces epistemic humility)

Premise P1: under deep uncertainty, we cannot assign probabilities to possible outcomes

Premise P2: if we cannot assign probabilities, then we cannot use the standard method of maximizing expected utility

Premise P3: if we cannot use the standard decision method, then an alternative decision principle is needed

Premise P4: epistemic humility requires acknowledging the limits of one’s own cognition

Premise P5: deep uncertainty is an extreme manifestation of cognitive limitation

Step 1: from P1 and P5, deep uncertainty indicates the severity of cognitive limitation

Step 2: from P4 and Step 1, deep uncertainty reinforces the necessity of epistemic humility

Step 3: from P2 and P3, deep uncertainty requires an alternative decision principle

Conclusion: deep uncertainty reinforces epistemic humility and requires an alternative decision principle

Bridge principle BP-2 (the epistemic humility principle):

Under deep uncertainty, a rational agent ought to act cautiously and avoid irreversible catastrophic consequences.

By BP-3 (the intersubjectivity principle), actions that irrevocably affect others cannot be reasonably accepted by those others. Reversibility preserves the others’ space of choice.

Proposition 13.9 (the relation between reversibility and the possibility space)

Reversibility is closely related to the possibility space. Reversible actions keep the possibility space open; irreversible actions compress the possibility space.

The Reversibility Principle and the Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP) support each other. RFP requires maintaining the possibility space; the Reversibility Principle provides procedural guidance for maintaining the possibility space.

§13.4.1 Review of the Precautionary Principle

I. The Definition of the Precautionary Principle

Definition 13.23 (the precautionary principle)

The Precautionary Principle means that in the face of potential serious or irreversible harm, even in the absence of full scientific certainty, this shall not be used as a reason to postpone cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation.

The classic statement of the precautionary principle (Article 15 of the 1992 Rio Declaration):

“In order to protect the environment, the precautionary approach shall be widely applied by States according to their capabilities. Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation.”

II. The Core Elements of the Precautionary Principle

Proposition 13.19 (the core elements of the precautionary principle)

The precautionary principle contains the following core elements:

  • Threat condition: there is a threat of serious or irreversible harm
  • Uncertainty condition: there is a lack of full scientific certainty
  • Action requirement: precautionary measures ought to be taken
  • Cost consideration: the measures ought to be cost-effective

III. Controversies over the Precautionary Principle

In practice the precautionary principle faces the following controversies:

1. Vague triggering conditions: what degree of threat counts as “serious”?

  • Necessity: yes (delaying action would lead to more infections)
  • Proportionality: the irreversibility of the action should be proportional to the urgency
  • Temporariness: emergency measures should be reassessed once the emergency is over

Conclusion: an emergency is an exceptional case of the Reversibility Principle, but it must satisfy the conditions of proportionality and temporariness

Exceptional case two: unavoidable irreversibility

Some actions are inherently irreversible, and no reversible alternative can be chosen.

Definition 13.26 (the unavoidable-irreversibility exception)

The unavoidable-irreversibility exception: when all available actions are irreversible, or inaction itself is also irreversible, the constraints of the Reversibility Principle need to be adjusted.

Formal statement:

Case 13.6 (the unavoidable-irreversibility exception)

Situation: in the face of climate change, both inaction and action may produce irreversible consequences.

Analysis:

  • Inaction: climate change continues, producing irreversible environmental damage
  • Action (e.g., geoengineering): may produce irreversible side effects
  • Both are irreversible

Adjusted application:

  • Compare the degrees of irreversibility of different actions
  • Choose the action with a lower degree of irreversibility
  • Or choose the action with lighter irreversible consequences

Conclusion: when irreversibility is unavoidable, one should compare the degrees of irreversibility of different actions and choose the relatively better one

Exceptional case three: negotiated consent

If all affected agents consent to a certain irreversible action, it may serve as an exception.

Definition 13.27 (the negotiated-consent exception)

The negotiated-consent exception: if all affected agents, after being fully informed, consent to a certain irreversible action, the constraints of the Reversibility Principle may be relaxed.

Formal statement:

Restricting conditions:

  • Consent must be informed
  • Consent must be voluntary
  • It must include all affected agents (including representatives of future generations)

Exceptional case four: the requirement of a higher principle

When the Reversibility Principle conflicts with a principle at a higher tier (such as a taboo red line), the higher principle takes priority.

Definition 13.28 (the higher-principle exception)

The higher-principle exception: when complying with the Reversibility Principle would violate a principle at a higher tier, the higher principle takes priority.

§13.5.3 Handling Conflicts with Other Principles

I. Potential Conflict with the Recursive Freedom Principle

Conflict situation 13.1

Situation: a certain technology can greatly expand the possibility space (consistent with RFP), but the deployment of that technology is irreversible (possibly violating PR).

Conflict analysis:

  • The Recursive Freedom Principle supports the technology (expanding the possibility space)
  • The Reversibility Principle may oppose the technology (irreversible)

Way of handling:

1. Assess the degree of expansion of the possibility space

2. Assess the degree of irreversibility

3. Weigh the two: is the expansion of the possibility space sufficient to justify the irreversibility?

4. Look for alternatives: is there an alternative technology with higher reversibility?

5. Design mitigation measures: can the irreversibility be reduced through design?

Proposition 13.26 (the relation between PR and RFP)

The Reversibility Principle (PR) and the Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP) are usually complementary rather than conflicting. Reversibility keeps the possibility space open; irreversible actions may compress the possibility space.

II. Potential Conflict with the Negentropy Responsibility Principle

Conflict situation 13.2

Situation: a certain action can significantly maintain a negentropy source (consistent with PNR), but the action is irreversible (possibly violating PR).

Conflict analysis:

  • The Negentropy Responsibility Principle supports the action (maintaining a negentropy source)
  • The Reversibility Principle may oppose the action (irreversible)

Way of handling:

1. Assess the urgency of negentropy maintenance

2. Assess the degree of irreversibility

3. Weigh the two: is the urgency of negentropy maintenance sufficient to justify the irreversibility?

4. Look for alternatives: is there an alternative with higher reversibility?

5. Risk assessment: if the action fails, what are the consequences?

Proposition 13.27 (the relation between PR and PNR)

The Reversibility Principle (PR) and the Negentropy Responsibility Principle (PNR) are usually complementary. Reversibility preserves error-correction capacity and helps to maintain negentropy sources over the long term.

III. A General Framework for Handling Principle Conflicts

Definition 13.29 (framework for handling principle conflicts)

When the Reversibility Principle conflicts with other principles, the following framework is used:

Step 1: identify the conflict

  • Make explicit which principles are in conflict
  • Assess the nature and degree of the conflict

Step 2: assess the weight of each principle

  • In the specific situation, how important is each principle relatively?
  • Does any principle have priority status?

Step 3: look for a reconciling solution

  • Is there a solution that satisfies all the principles at once?
  • Can the conflict be reduced by modifying the action?

Step 4: if reconciliation is impossible, weigh

  • Determine the priority according to the situation
  • Choose the solution that is overall optimal

Step 5: record the reasons for the decision

  • Explicitly record why a particular solution was chosen
  • Provide a reference for similar situations in the future

Chapter 13 Appendix: The Axiom System of the Principle of Reversibility

Axiom System 13.A (Principle of Reversibility Axiom System, PR-AX)

[Basic definitions]

Definition D1 (reversibility function):

Rev: A → [0, 1]

Rev(a) = P(Restore(s₀) | Execute(a, s₀) → s₁, Reverse(a))

Reversibility is the probability of restoring the initial state after performing the inverse action

Mitchell, M. (2009). Complexity: A Guided Tour. Oxford University Press.

Definition D2 (deep uncertainty):

DeepUncertainty(Sit) ↔ ¬∃P: ProbabilityDistribution(P, Outcomes(Sit))

Deep uncertainty is a situation in which no probability distribution can be assigned to the outcomes

Definition D3 (catastrophic consequences):

IrreversibleCatastrophe(a) ↔ Rev(a) < 0.1 ∧ Severity(a) > θ_severity

An irreversible catastrophic consequence is one with extremely low reversibility and extremely high severity

Definition D4 (error-correction capacity):

ErrorCorrection(S, a) ↔ Reversible(a) ∧ Detect(S, Error(a)) → Correct(S, a)

Error-correction capacity is the capacity to correct an error once it is detected

[Axioms]

Axiom A1 (axiom of reversibility preference):

∀a₁, a₂ ∈ A: OtherEqual(a₁, a₂) ∧ Rev(a₁) > Rev(a₂) → a₁ ≻ a₂

Other things being equal, one should choose the action with higher reversibility

Axiom A2 (axiom of the justification threshold):

∀a ∈ A: Rev(a) < θ_rev → RequireStrongJustification(a)

An action whose reversibility is below the threshold requires strong justification

Axiom A3 (axiom of catastrophic consequences):

∀a ∈ A: IrreversibleCatastrophe(a) → ¬Permissible(a) ∨ ExtremeJustification(a)

Irreversible catastrophic consequences require extreme justification

Axiom A4 (axiom of the rollback obligation):

∀a ∈ A: Execute(a) ∧ NegativeConsequence(a) ∧ Reversible(a) → O(Rollback(a))

If an action produces negative consequences and is reversible, there is an obligation to roll back

Axiom A5 (axiom of epistemic humility):

∀S ∈ RationalAgents: O(Acknowledge(Fallibility(S)))

A rational agent should acknowledge its own fallibility

[Inference rules]

Rule R1 (rule of reversibility comparison):

If Rev(a₁) > Rev(a₂) and other conditions are equal, then choose a₁

Rule R2 (rule of the justification threshold):

JustificationThreshold(a) = f(1 - Rev(a))

The justification threshold is proportional to irreversibility

Rule R3 (rule of rollback triggering):

NegativeConsequence(a) ∧ Rev(a) > θ_rollback → Trigger(Rollback(a))

When there is a negative consequence and reversibility is above the threshold, rollback is triggered

Rule R4 (rule of exception handling):

Emergency(Sit) ∨ UnavoidableIrreversibility(a) ∨ InformedConsent(All) ∨ HigherPrinciple

→ AdjustedApplication(PR)

[Theorems]

Theorem T1 (theorem that reversibility preserves error-correction capacity):

∀a ∈ A: Reversible(a) → Maintain(ErrorCorrectionCapacity)

Proof: follows directly from Definition D4 and Axiom A4

Theorem T2 (theorem that deep uncertainty strengthens the preference for reversibility):

DeepUncertainty(Sit) → Strengthen(ReversibilityPreference)

Proof: follows from Axiom A5 and BP-2

Theorem T3 (theorem on reversibility and the space of possibilities):

∀a ∈ A: Reversible(a) → Maintain(PossibilitySpace)

Proof: reversible actions preserve the capacity to return to a previous state, and thereby preserve the space of possibilities

Procedure 13.4 (procedure for designing a rollback mechanism)

Step 1: Identify the key states

  • Determine the key state variables that need to be recorded
  • Determine the frequency and manner of state recording

Step 2: Design the monitoring mechanism

  • Determine the indicators that need to be monitored
  • Design alert rules and thresholds

Step 3: Define the trigger conditions

  • Clarify under what circumstances rollback is triggered
  • Distinguish automatic triggering from manual triggering

Step 4: Design the rollback procedure

  • Formulate detailed rollback steps
  • Consider partial rollback and full rollback

Step 5: Allocate responsibility

  • Clarify who has the authority to decide on rollback
  • Clarify who is responsible for executing rollback

Step 6: Guarantee resources

  • Ensure that the resources required for rollback are available
  • Establish an emergency resource reserve

Step 7: Testing and verification

  • Test the rollback mechanism regularly
  • Improve it based on test results

Step 8: Documentation

  • Record the design of the rollback mechanism
  • Record the process and results of each rollback
