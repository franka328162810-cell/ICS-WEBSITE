§D.1 The Challenge of Governing Interstellar Civilization

§D.1.1 Why Think about Interstellar Civilization Now?

I. Responding to the "Too Early" Objection

A natural objection is this: interstellar civilization seems to belong to a distant future, so why think about its governance now? This objection deserves to be taken seriously.

Definition D.1 (Interstellar Civilization)

Interstellar civilization is a form of intelligent civilization possessing the following features:

  • (1) a sphere of activity extending beyond a single planetary system;
  • (2) a time scale extending over many generations, even thousands of generations;
  • (3) possible inclusion of many types of intelligent agents (biological, artificial, hybrid);
  • (4) exposure to the possibility of contact with other intelligent civilizations.

Normative frameworks need to be established before technological capacities are acquired.

Contemporary human civilization faces a convergence of threefold pressure, and these pressures jointly point to the urgency of the governance problems of interstellar civilization:

Pressure One: Exponential growth of technological capability

Human technological capability is growing at an exponential rate, but the growth of ethical wisdom and governance capacity has lagged far behind.

III. On the Relation between the "Three New Views" and the "Three Old Views"

The "Three New Views" proposed in this work (the New Cosmology, the New View of Life, the New View of Cognition) are a paradigm shift from the traditional "three old views" (worldview, outlook on life, values), rather than a simple replacement.

Specific qualifications:

1. The historical contribution of the "three old views": the traditional worldview, outlook on life and values played an important role in the planetary stage of human civilization, providing individuals and societies with a framework of meaning and a guide to action.

2. The limits of the "three old views": under the new conditions of interstellar civilization, multiple intelligent agents and deep uncertainty, the old views face structural limits — they presuppose anthropocentrism, an Earth-bound environment and short time scales.

3. The stance of the "three new views": the Three New Views do not negate the three old views but provide a more inclusive and adaptive framework over a larger spatiotemporal scale and a more diverse range of agents.

4. Compatibility: at the Earthly scale and within human society, the three old views remain valid; the Three New Views are a "dimensional upgrade" of the old views at the interstellar scale.

§D.1.2 The Distinctiveness of the Governance Challenge

I. Fourfold Distinctiveness

The governance challenge of interstellar civilization has a fourfold distinctiveness that makes traditional governance frameworks hard to apply directly:

Distinctiveness One: Deep Time Scale

Definition D.3 (Deep Time)

Deep time is a time scale beyond the grasp of human intuition, usually referring to spans of thousands to millions of years. At the scale of deep time, the succession of human generations, the rise and fall of civilizations, and even the evolution of species become observable phenomena.

Distinctiveness Four: Deep Uncertainty

Definition D.6 (Deep Uncertainty)

Deep uncertainty is an epistemic state in which:

  • (1) the space of possible outcomes cannot be fully enumerated;
  • (2) even if it could be enumerated, no meaningful probabilities could be assigned to the outcomes;
  • (3) even if probabilities could be assigned, the utility function could not be determined;
  • (4) the decision-maker is also uncertain about his or her own preferences and values.

  • (2) because the number of future generations may greatly exceed the present generation, actions affecting the long-term future are of extremely high moral importance;

Introduction Notes

[1] The concept of interstellar civilization: This work's definition of "interstellar civilization" synthesizes several sources. For a cosmic-scale classification of civilizations, see: Kardashev, N. S. (1964). "Transmission of Information by Extraterrestrial Civilizations." Soviet Astronomy, 8, 217-221. For the time scale of civilizations, see: Bostrom, N. (2013). "Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority." Global Policy, 4(1), 15-31.

[2] Collingridge Dilemma: This dilemma was proposed by David Collingridge in 1980. See: Collingridge, D. (1980). The Social Control of Technology. Frances Pinter. The dilemma holds that the control of technology faces a double bind: in the early stage of technological development, impacts can be changed but are difficult to foresee; once the technology has matured, impacts can be foreseen but are difficult to change.

[3] Deep Uncertainty: The concept of deep uncertainty is widely discussed in decision theory and policy analysis. See: Walker, W. E., et al. (2013). "Deep Uncertainty." In Encyclopedia of Operations Research and Management Science. Springer. And: Lempert, R. J., Popper, S. W., & Bankes, S. C. (2003). Shaping the Next One Hundred Years: New Methods for Quantitative, Long-Term Policy Analysis. RAND Corporation.

[4] The Westphalian system: the principles of international order established by the Peace of Westphalia of 1648. On its history and contemporary significance, see: Krasner, S. D. (1999). Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy. Princeton University Press. On its limitations in space governance, see: Jakhu, R. S., & Dempsey, P. S. (Eds.). (2016). Routledge Handbook of Space Law. Routledge.

[5] Social contract theory: For classic statements of social contract theory, see: Hobbes, T. (1651). Leviathan; Locke, J. (1689). Two Treatises of Government; Rousseau, J.-J. (1762). The Social Contract; Rawls, J. (1971). A Theory of Justice. Harvard University Press. On the application and limitations of social contract theory in intergenerational justice, see: Gosseries, A., & Meyer, L. H. (Eds.). (2009). Intergenerational Justice. Oxford University Press.

[6] Utilitarianism: For classic statements of utilitarianism, see: Bentham, J. (1789). An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation; Mill, J. S. (1863). Utilitarianism. On the application of utilitarianism to long-term future problems, see: Parfit, D. (1984). Reasons and Persons. Oxford University Press, Part IV.

[7] Kantian ethics: For classic statements of Kantian ethics, see: Kant, I. (1785). Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals; Kant, I. (1788). Critique of Practical Reason. On contemporary developments in Kantian ethics, see: Korsgaard, C. M. (1996). The Sources of Normativity. Cambridge University Press.

[8] Longtermism: For a systematic exposition of longtermism, see: MacAskill, W. (2022). What We Owe the Future. Basic Books; Ord, T. (2020). The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity. Hachette Books. On the philosophical foundations of longtermism, see: Greaves, H., & MacAskill, W. (2021). "The Case for Strong Longtermism." Global Priorities Institute Working Paper.

[9] Existential risk: The concept of existential risk was systematically developed by Nick Bostrom. See: Bostrom, N. (2002). "Existential Risks: Analyzing Human Extinction Scenarios and Related Hazards." Journal of Evolution and Technology, 9(1). And: Bostrom, N., & Cirkovic, M. M. (Eds.). (2008). Global Catastrophic Risks. Oxford University Press.

[10] Expected utility theory: For classic statements of expected utility theory, see: von Neumann, J., & Morgenstern, O. (1944). Theory of Games and Economic Behavior. Princeton University Press. On its limitations under deep uncertainty, see: Gilboa, I., & Schmeidler, D. (1989). "Maxmin Expected Utility with Non-Unique Prior." Journal of Mathematical Economics, 18(2), 141-153.

[11] Robust Decision Making: The robust decision making method was developed by the RAND Corporation. See: Lempert, R. J., Popper, S. W., & Bankes, S. C. (2003). Shaping the Next One Hundred Years: New Methods for Quantitative, Long-Term Policy Analysis. RAND Corporation. And: Walker, W. E., Lempert, R. J., & Kwakkel, J. H. (2013). "Deep Uncertainty." In Encyclopedia of Operations Research and Management Science.

[12] Non-Identity Problem: The non-identity problem was proposed by Derek Parfit. See: Parfit, D. (1984). Reasons and Persons. Oxford University Press, Chapter 16. On subsequent discussions of the problem, see: Roberts, M. A., & Wasserman, D. T. (Eds.). (2009). Harming Future Persons: Ethics, Genetics and the Nonidentity Problem. Springer.

[13] Animal ethics: For classic works on animal ethics, see: Singer, P. (1975). Animal Liberation. New York Review/Random House; Regan, T. (1983). The Case for Animal Rights. University of California Press. On contemporary developments in animal ethics, see: DeGrazia, D. (1996). Taking Animals Seriously: Mental Life and Moral Status. Cambridge University Press.

[14] Environmental ethics: For classic works on environmental ethics, see: Leopold, A. (1949). A Sand County Almanac. Oxford University Press; Naess, A. (1973). "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement." Inquiry, 16(1-4), 95-100. For a systematic introduction to environmental ethics, see: Jamieson, D. (Ed.). (2001). A Companion to Environmental Philosophy. Blackwell.

[15] Complex systems science: For an introduction to complex systems science, see: Mitchell, M. (2009). Complexity: A Guided Tour. Oxford University Press; Holland, J. H. (2014). Complexity: A Very Short Introduction. Oxford University Press. On the research tradition of the Santa Fe Institute, see: Waldrop, M. M. (1992). Complexity: The Emerging Science at the Edge of Order and Chaos. Simon & Schuster.

[16] Process philosophy: For classic statements of process philosophy, see: Whitehead, A. N. (1929). Process and Reality. Macmillan. On contemporary developments in process philosophy, see: Rescher, N. (1996). Process Metaphysics: An Introduction to Process Philosophy. SUNY Press.

[17] Relational ontology: For contemporary discussions of relational ontology, see: Ladyman, J., & Ross, D. (2007). Every Thing Must Go: Metaphysics Naturalized. Oxford University Press. On structural realism, see: French, S. (2014). The Structure of the World: Metaphysics and Representation. Oxford University Press.

[18] Negentropy: The concept of negentropy was introduced into the life sciences by Schrödinger in What Is Life?. See: Schrödinger, E. (1944). What Is Life? Cambridge University Press. On the application of negentropy in contemporary life science, see: Kauffman, S. A. (1993). The Origins of Order: Self-Organization and Selection in Evolution. Oxford University Press.

The Oxford Handbook of 4E Cognition. Oxford University Press. On extended cognition, see: Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. (1998). "The Extended Mind." Analysis, 58(1), 7-19.

Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 370(1668). The UCS indicator of this work partly draws on the formal methods of IIT.

Cambridge University Press; Dehaene, S., & Changeux, J.-P. (2011). "Experimental and Theoretical Approaches to Conscious Processing." Neuron, 70(2), 200-227.

[23] International space law: For the basic documents of international space law, see: the Outer Space Treaty (1967), the Rescue Agreement (1968), the Liability Convention (1972), the Registration Convention (1975), and the Moon Agreement (1979). "Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment." Minds and Machines, 30(3), 411-437.
