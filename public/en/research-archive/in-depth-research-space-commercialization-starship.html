Space Commercialization: Starship's First Orbital Flight — When a Civilization's Physical Dimension Outruns Its Norms

On September 28, 2026, SpaceX's Starship flies its first true orbital mission, releasing 26 Starlink V3 satellites. This ICS analysis argues that the flight represents a step-change in the Energy Utilization (EU) and Information Processing (IP) dimensions of the Civilization Development Index (CDI) within a single mission cycle, while Normative Maturity (NMat) remains trapped in the institutional equilibrium of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. We re-read the event through the New Three Views (NC/NL/NK), run the RFP/NRP/REV principles live, check it against all five Forbidden Red Lines (FRL-1 to FRL-5), assign a CSIA rating, and conclude that reusable heavy lift is a mirror: it shows humanity has acquired the thrust for interplanetary survival without the matching normative capacity to steward the possibility space.

On September 28, 2026, at Starbase in Boca Chica, Texas, SpaceX's fourteenth integrated flight of Starship will attempt, for the first time, a true orbital mission: roughly 275 kilometers above the surface, six loops around the planet, a flight of nearly ten hours, ending in a Pacific Ocean splashdown west of Chile. This is no ordinary test flight. It is the moment this 124-meter, 33-engine vehicle abandons the "passively safe" suborbital envelope that the previous thirteen flights deliberately occupied, and finally releases 26 Starlink V3 satellites on orbit.

Why should a work of "Interstellar Civilization Studies" (ICS) care about a commercial rocket reaching orbit? Because from an ICS vantage, the engineering significance of this event is smaller than its civilizational significance: it is a live specimen of two physical dimensions of the Civilization Development Index (CDI) jumping within a single mission cycle, while the normative maturity dimension barely moves. The rocket has carried us into orbit; our governance framework is still parked in 1967.

1. The Threshold Deliberately Avoided for Three and a Half Years

Fix the facts first. Mission Flight 14 uses Ship 41—the third flight of the third-generation (V3) Starship upper stage. The launch window was originally September 22 at 7:15 a.m. Central; SpaceX gave no reason and slipped it to September 28 at 8:15 a.m. Eastern, a 75-minute window. The target orbit is about 275 km, roughly six revolutions, nearly ten hours total; reentry begins about T+9h28m, with the landing burn and splashdown by T+9h50m. All 33 Raptor 3 engines lighting normally is a hard requirement—that benchmark was not yet nailed on the V3's debut, Flight 12.

The payload is 26 Starlink V3 satellites, mission designator Starlink Group 31-1. These are the first V3 satellites to reach operational orbit. Each V3 adds about 1 Tbps of constellation capacity, so this single flight injects 26 Tbps—roughly ten times the capacity of a Falcon 9 launch of V2-mini satellites. The payload bay can hold 60 V3s at maximum load; only 26 are flying, because SpaceX is candid that this remains an engineering test, not routine commercial service, and wants margin to fly the orbital profile cleanly. Three of the 26 carry modified cameras to photograph Starship in space.

The Super Heavy booster is not returning to the chopstick tower this time. After separation it performs a boostback burn and splashes down at an offshore point in the Gulf of Mexico. This is a transitional profile: neither the tower catch of Flight 5 nor the early years' ocean breakups.

The detail worth pausing on is why the first thirteen flights were all suborbital. SpaceX's own mission notes are blunt: every prior flight "intentionally flew passively safe suborbital trajectories to maximize public safety while allowing for maximum learning." In ICS vocabulary, this was a thirty-six-month, textbook engineering instantiation of the Reversibility Principle (REV): under deep uncertainty, avoid irreversible paths; drop debris over uninhabited areas; leave no permanent objects in orbit. From April 2023, when Flight 1 ripped the concrete launch pad apart and hurled debris hundreds of meters, to July 2026, when Flight 13 splashed down an intact upper stage and re-lit a Raptor in space—those thirteen flights were not thirteen failures. They were thirteen incremental trials conducted under the axiom NK-4, that all cognitive standards are revisable.

Flight 14 is the first time SpaceX voluntarily crosses the watershed: it will release 26 permanent orbital objects and fly a ten-hour active profile requiring orbital maintenance. From this moment, "if something goes wrong it just falls in the Gulf" is no longer the default. This is a step-change in irreversibility.

2. A CDI Reading: Physical Dimensions Leap, Normative Dimensions Crawl

Interstellar Civilization Studies measures multidimensional civilizational development with the Civilization Development Index (CDI). Under the standard weighting of the revised six-volume edition, CDI is a weighted arithmetic mean of five dimensions: Energy Utilization (EU, weight 0.20), Information Processing (IP, 0.20), Negentropy Maintenance (NM, 0.25), Agent Diversity (AD, 0.15), and Normative Maturity (NMat, 0.20). Present-day Earth civilization scores about 0.35, reported as the interval [0.219, 0.413]. We are not making incremental progress within an already mature civilization; we are watching two dimensions of a 0.35-score civilization suddenly accelerate.

Starship's first orbital flight pulls both EU and IP upward at once. On EU: a fully reusable heavy-lift vehicle cuts the marginal cost per kilogram to orbit by an order of magnitude. This is not a few percent cheaper; it converts access to orbit from a state-level capability requiring national mobilization into a routine operation a single company can schedule. Cross-civilizational comparison uses logarithmic scales, and heavy-lift reusability is the key infrastructure by which a civilization pushes its dispatchable energy boundary outward one notch.

The IP jump is more concrete. Twenty-six Tbps on one flight is not "faster internet." It is the relocation of human civilization's information infrastructure from the planetary surface toward low Earth orbit. As this cadence continues, LEO ceases to be the exclusive preserve of a handful of research and military spacecraft and becomes a single company's continuously expanding communications artery. In CDI's arithmetic mean, that registers immediately as a score rise.

And NMat? Normative maturity measures how thoroughly RFP, NRP, and REV are institutionalized. As of September 2026, the core legal instrument governing outer space remains the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. The Artemis Accords are a voluntary, non-binding "soft law" club—this week San Marino became the 76th signatory. The UN COPUOS "recommended principles" on space resource activities are still a draft. The FAA is a U.S. domestic regulator; its environmental assessments and Findings of No Significant Impact evaluate the "U.S. human environment." It does not administer LEO as the common heritage of humankind.

This is the article's central claim: CDI is a weighted arithmetic mean. A leap in EU and IP does not automatically compensate for stagnation in NMat; the weighting instead exposes the asymmetry. Under a "normativity-priority" weighting, NMat's weight rises from 0.20 to 0.35—and then no number of successful Starship flights will move the civilization's total score. We are operating a 2026 launch vehicle under 1967 rules, and the vehicle's descendants are planned for Mars by the mid-2030s.

3. Re-reading Starship Through the New Three Views

NC-1: Relations precede entities

The first axiom of the New Cosmological View is relational graph isomorphism: the universe is a graph of relations, entities are nodes, and properties are determined by position in that network. Under this axiom, the phrase "Starship succeeded" is itself ill-formed—evaluating a node in isolation is precisely the entity-ontology that NC-1 rejects.

Starship's real identity is defined by the entire web it is embedded in: the dune ecology and nesting sea turtles of Boca Chica; the Gulf of Mexico and South Pacific splashdown zones; the thousands of objects already sharing the 275-km corridor; the Chilean Pacific tracking area; the FAA permitting chain from the 2022 programmatic EA to the February 2026 FONSI; Starlink users worldwide; other nations that will use the same launch corridors; and the space actors of the twenty-second century who will inherit the same LEO. If any node in that network fails, "Starship succeeded" must be discounted.

NC-2: Being is process, not state

The second group of cosmological axioms holds that existence is a process and a state is a snapshot. Flight 1's destroyed pad was not a failure; it was a necessary snapshot in that process. Without the shattered concrete there would be no water-cooled steel launch mount; without the Flight 7 aft fire there would be no V3 airframe redesign. Starship is not one particular rocket. It is a thirty-six-month, thirteen-flight process that revises itself on the data and wreckage of the previous flight. This "state-as-snapshot" view deflates the hero-myth single shot that pervades space narrative: no flight is the endpoint; every flight is the launch site of the next.

NL-1: Civilization need not be locked to one substrate

The most radical axiom of the New View of Life is substrate-independence: life is defined by function, not by a particular material base; functional equivalence implies life equivalence. Scaled to civilizational level, this means a civilization's persistence need not be bound to one planet, one biosphere, one source of negentropy.

Starship reaching orbit is not "humans moving to Mars"—it is nowhere near that. But it is the first engineering rehearsal of the NL-1 thesis: proving that a planetary civilization can stably move a portion of itself—even if only 26 satellites and an upper stage—above the atmosphere and maintain a negentropy flow for a time on another substrate (LEO). Read alongside mirror life, brain-computer interfaces, and AI-integrated society, it becomes clear that these are four faces of the same NL-1 proposition: life is not locked to its substrate, and neither should a civilization be locked to a planetary surface.

NK-4: Cognitive standards are always under revision

The fourth group of epistemological axioms holds that no universally fixed cognitive standard exists; valid standards are negotiated and always revisable. From pad-destruction to tower-catch, from V1 to V3, from suborbital to orbital, SpaceX's engineering "cognitive standards" have been revised every few months. That is not indecision; it is NK-4 in engineering form.

Outer space law, by contrast, is revised on a timescale measured in decades. NK-4 does not demand a perfect rulebook now; it demands that rules be revisable in step with reality. When engineering cognition iterates quarterly and legal cognition iterates per decade, the civilization's cognitive system develops a structural dislocation—and that is the textbook definition of deep uncertainty.

4. Running the Three Principles Live

RFP: Whose possibility space is being expanded?

The Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP) is the highest-priority principle: action should preserve or expand the possibility space rather than irreversibly compress it. Starship plainly expands human possibilities—multi-planet survival, in-space industry, zero-gravity manufacturing, a massive communications web. On the Recursive Freedom Degree (RFD) this is a positive contribution.

But RFP guards a distributed possibility space, not a single agent's. Consider: when one company holds 26 Tbps-per-flight deployment capability and de facto occupies the preferred LEO slots and Ka/Ku spectrum, has the possibility space of newcomers expanded or compressed? Starlink has brought connectivity to remote regions, but it has also turned "can I deploy a new constellation in LEO?" from an engineering question into a political negotiation with incumbent slot-holders. RFP is not "the bigger the capability the more just." It asks: did this round of possibility growth enlarge the pie, or lock the door?

NRP: Whose entropy bill is being externalized?

The Negentropy Responsibility Principle (NRP) is the resource principle: maintain negentropy sources; do not externalize entropy costs onto others or onto future generations. Starship got one big thing right on the launch side—reusability. Each flight no longer melts a launch pad or builds a new rocket from scratch. That is a genuine NRP asset.

But the entropy did not disappear; it changed address. At least four externalizations are running:

  • Orbital debris: 26 V3s join thousands of existing objects in the same corridor. Any on-orbit collision converts the corridor into a multi-decade, even multi-century, tragedy of the commons for all space actors.
  • Spaceport ecology: Boca Chica sits on a dune coast that is habitat for endangered sea turtles and dune vegetation. At the FAA's licensed cadence of up to 145 launches a year, the noise, light, and beach closures are costs borne by that ecosystem.
  • Splashdown marine environment: the Gulf and South Pacific zones absorb booster and upper-stage debris on every mission.
  • Radio and astronomical environment: mega-constellations are the most worrisome new foreground contaminants for radio astronomy—even as China's FAST telescope made news this very week for a narrowband signal toward K2-155; that signal-discipline work is continuously eroded by satellite passes.

NRP does not oppose launches. It opposes the arrangement where the present benefits and future generations pay. Starship scores on booster reuse; it still owes on the orbital and ecological side.

REV: Can this still be rolled back?

The Reversibility Principle (REV) is the strategic principle: under deep uncertainty, prefer reversible acts and preserve the ability to correct. The first thirteen suborbital flights were highly reversible—abort anytime, debris over uninhabited areas, no permanent orbital objects. Flight 14 breaks that safety cushion for the first time: once released, the 26 V3s are permanent orbital bodies for five to seven years, and any collision debris persists in LEO on century-scale half-lives.

This is where the CRV (Commitment Reversibility Verifiability) V_rev dimension bites: do V3 satellites have de-orbit propulsion? Can they actively burn up in the atmosphere at end of design life? That engineering parameter is what determines whether this flight scores well on reversibility. The booster's Gulf splashdown rather than tower catch is a reversibility regression—it turns a reusable booster into a one-shot expenditure—but it is still safer for the public than an early breakup near Boca Chica. That trade-off is exactly the kind of real dilemma REV is built to handle.

5. The FRL Red Lines: Has Starship Crossed One?

The Forbidden Red Lines (FRL) carry absolute priority over every principle, with no exceptions. Flight 14 and the space-commercialization route it represents are checked line by line:

Red lineAssessment
FRL-1 Intentional extinctionNot triggered. Starship itself is not an extinction instrument.
FRL-2 Irreversible large-scale environmental destructionBoundary zone. A single launch is not planetary ecological collapse, but the cumulative effect of up to 145 flights/year—LEO debris, dune ecology, ocean splashdowns—requires continuous oversight by a Cosmic-Scale IRB. It is a deliberation zone, not a core trigger.
FRL-3 Non-consensual irreversible cognitive modificationNot applicable.
FRL-4 Extreme intergenerational entropy externalizationThe closest call. The anchor document lists "indefinite nuclear waste storage" as the FRL-4 core zone and "carbon emissions" as the boundary zone. LEO orbital debris behaves more like nuclear waste: the present enjoys communications, future generations must operate inside a debris-polluted corridor, and cleaning it up requires launching still more objects. This is a near-textbook intergenerational entropy externalization requiring deliberation.
FRL-5 Closing rollback mechanismsGray zone. The rocket has an abort chain, but once thousands of satellites are networked, is there a "recall the constellation" rollback? If one day Starlink is judged unacceptable—astronomical interference, orbital congestion, weaponization—can humans bring those satellites back? Today the answer is no. This is not the "unkillable AGI" core trigger, but it is a trend worth flagging.

Bottom line: a single Flight 14 does not cross any FRL core line. But the normalized route it represents—145 flights a year plus mega-constellations—is slowly pushing FRL-4 and FRL-5 from "future risk" into "present pressure." The FRL has no exceptions, but the objects it protects need continuous monitoring—which is precisely why ICS posits a Cosmic-Scale IRB.

6. A CSIA Accounting of This Orbital Flight

The Cosmic-Scale Impact Assessment (CSIA) scores six dimensions: Scope D1 (weight 0.15), Depth D2 (0.15), Severity D3 (0.20), Reversibility D4 (0.20, inverted—high reversibility = low risk score), Uncertainty D5 (0.15), Cascading risk D6 (0.15). Under the first-edition four-volume bracket (0–25 L1 low, 26–50 L2 moderate, 51–75 L3 high, 76–100 L4 extreme), a rough read for Flight 14 and its route:

  • D1 Scope (~0.3/1): This event is confined to LEO, not interstellar. But it is the on-ramp to the Moon and Mars, with spillover expectations.
  • D2 Depth (~0.7/1): LEO debris effects run on century timescales, well past the "century+" long horizon.
  • D3 Severity (~0.5/1): Not an existential risk, but orbital congestion would materially weaken access to space for all nations—especially latecomers.
  • D4 Reversibility, inverted (~0.6/1): Satellites can deorbit, but collision debris cannot. High irreversibility converts to a high risk score.
  • D5 Uncertainty (~0.7/1): The Kessler threshold, inter-constellation collision probabilities, and long-horizon orbital evolution are deep-uncertainty problems.
  • D6 Cascading (~0.7/1): Orbital debris cascade is the textbook "self-sustaining once triggered" cascade structure.

Weighted, this lands around 0.5–0.6—on the first-edition bracket, a transition from L2 moderate to L3 high. Not L4; a single launch will not end civilization. But it is emphatically not L1. It is the kind of risk that looks harmless in any single instance and becomes serious only in aggregate—which is exactly what CSIA is built to catch: not to slam the brakes on the first launch, but to fold cumulative effects into continuous monitoring and preserve institutional rollback before the threshold is crossed.

6b. The Regulatory Long March: Seven Years at the FAA

To see why this is a governance event, look past the rocket at the permit. From SpaceX's 2022 environmental kickoff at Boca Chica to its orbital license for Flight 14 took more than four years. What happened in between?

In 2022 the FAA issued a programmatic EA and mitigated FONSI approving Boca Chica launches. In 2023 environmental groups sued, forcing a written re-evaluation. After Flight 1 pad debris, public safety faced another round of scrutiny. In February 2026 the FAA published a final tiered EA and FONSI/ROD widening Starship's airspace corridors to roughly 1,600 nautical miles east through the Straits of Florida—the legal precondition for the 275 km profile and the Chilean splashdown. On September 3, 2026, the FAA cleared an additional return trajectory to Starbase. The 145-flights-per-year authorization is bound up with NOAA biological-opinion consultations on sea turtles and marine mammals.

This timeline is itself a REV-institutional version: not a one-shot "launch away," but a tiered environmental review every time a trajectory expands or cadence rises, with new mitigation measures layered in. That is a prototype of versioned governance—but it is done by one national agency, not a global IRB. Boca Chica's review protects U.S. citizens and U.S. territory; it does not assess what 26 V3s at 275 km mean for astronomical observation, satellite navigation, or emergency communications in Argentina, Nigeria, or Indonesia. LEO is a commons with no sovereignty, and we are administering it with a sovereign-state domestic tool.

7. The Institutional Gap: Why We Need a "Cosmic-Scale IRB"

ICS proposes four institutional prototypes: a Multi-Intelligence Tribunal, a Cosmic-Scale IRB, Intergenerational Trust, and Versioned Governance. Starship illuminates all four gaps at once.

The Cosmic-Scale IRB is missing. Who approves a "145 flights a year plus a ten-thousand-satellite constellation"? The FAA is a U.S. domestic body; its FONSI evaluates the U.S. human environment. LEO is, under the Outer Space Treaty, "the common province of all mankind"—not any single state's domestic jurisdiction. Letting one country's civil aviation authority authorize activities that affect the global orbital commons is itself in tension with NRP's duty not to externalize costs onto other subjects.

Intergenerational Trust is missing. Who represents future generations' claims on LEO slots and spectrum? Every satellite launched today consumes an orbital slot that could have been left to the humans of 2126. The anchor document identifies intergenerational trust as the core institution for intergenerational justice—and on LEO, no institution is doing that work.

Versioned governance is missing. Starlink has gone from V1 to V2-mini to V3 in three years; the governance framework has not moved on the 1967 treaty. Versioned governance requires the framework to version, roll back, and revise alongside the technology—the institutional counterpart of NK-4.

The Artemis Accords point the right way but govern the wrong place. The 76th signatory joined this week; the voluntary coalition is expanding fast. But its agenda is lunar exploration coordination, not LEO slot allocation, debris mitigation, or mega-constellation governance. However carefully the lunar "safety zones" are negotiated, they will not stop thousands of satellites from converging in low Earth orbit.

7b. The AD Dimension: When Orbit Becomes a Club

One CDI dimension is routinely overlooked: Agent Diversity (AD, weight 0.15)—species, cultural, and substrate diversity. Scaled to space commercialization, it asks: is access to orbit a public capability shared across a civilization's many agents, or an exclusive privilege of a few?

Historically, when Sputnik flew in 1957, reaching orbit was a state act—and only two states could do it. The Space Shuttle was supposed to democratize access but turned out pricier and pickier than disposable rockets. The real inflection was reusable rockets: Falcon 9 cut launch cost by an order of magnitude, Starship is cutting another. On the surface this is democratization—smallsat companies, developing countries, university labs can all lift payloads.

But one layer down, a new concentration appears. Starlink is not an open orbital-access market; it is a vertically integrated private ecosystem: the rocket is SpaceX's, the satellites are SpaceX's, the ground stations are SpaceX's, the terminals are SpaceX's, the licenses are SpaceX's. Flight 14 injects 26 Tbps into one company's constellation on a single mission. When orbital slots and spectrum—the common property of humankind—are consumed by one company at this rate, the AD reading is: the barrier to buying access fell, but the barrier to independently deploying a new LEO system rose, because the best slots and spectrum are already taken.

This is not a denial of Starlink's engineering achievement. It is the point that RFP guards a possibility space distributed across agents. If only one company can put 26 Tbps on orbit and everyone else must be its customer, the civilization's Recursive Freedom Degree is distributed with severe inequality among agents. ICS does not object to commercial success; it objects to commercial success being mistaken for civilizational success.

8. The Kessler Shadow: A Deferred Bill

No discussion of orbital debris avoids the Kessler syndrome. In 1978 NASA scientist Donald Kessler proposed that once LEO object density crosses a threshold, a single collision produces debris that triggers further collisions, until the corridor becomes unusable for centuries. Where exactly that threshold lies, no one can compute—which is D5 deep uncertainty in its purest form.

Twenty-six V3s on Flight 14 are harmless in isolation. But stack three numbers: first, the FAA has authorized up to 145 Starship launches a year; second, the V3 bay holds 60 at max, and Flight 14 flies only 26 as engineering margin—60-per-flight is the steady state once mature; third, competitors—Amazon Kuiper, OneWeb, China's Guowang/G60—are all planning their own ten-thousand-satellite constellations. Those three curves together mean LEO object counts grow not linearly but exponentially.

The horror of Kessler is that it is "fine until it isn't": before the threshold, every launch looks safe; after it, humans may lose the use of LEO within decades—and LEO carries the communications, navigation, weather, and Earth-observation infrastructure of modern civilization. This is why CSIA D6 cascading risk scores 0.7: it is not today's catastrophe; it is a catastrophe that accrues today and that no institutional actor has an incentive to stop early.

Here NRP and FRL-4 fire together. NRP forbids externalizing entropy costs onto future generations; FRL-4 lists extreme intergenerational entropy externalization as an absolute red line. Every launch today tells the future: you will pay dearly to clean up our orbit. The cost of a missing intergenerational-trust institution will be settled in full the day the Kessler threshold is crossed.

9. Deep-Time Conclusion: Thrust Ahead, Norms Behind

Pull back to ICS deep time. At T2 (~50 years, to 2076): if Starship works, there may be routine Earth–Moon schedules and reusable cargo ports in LEO, with zero-gravity in-space manufacturing. But the same 2076 may find LEO debris so degraded that an "orbital cleanup industry" is required just to maintain it—and that industry itself launches more objects, generating more debris. That is a textbook NRP vicious cycle.

At T3 (100+ years): Starship is the first stage of an interplanetary civilization. On that there is no doubt. But ICS's honest stress-test finding is that in the first edition's four scenarios, First Contact (ECC in the six-volume edition) scored lowest at 0.44; the overall framework robustness self-rates 0.58, moderate, not excellent. In plain terms: our normative preparation for "becoming an interplanetary civilization" is far weaker than our engineering preparation for "putting things on top of a rocket." We are already knocking on the door technically; we have not finished writing the invitation.

Return to the core maxim: "Freedom is the Stewardship of Possibility." Starship has pushed the possibility space outward by a wide margin—that is its historical credit, and it should not be denied. But stewarding the possibility space requires more than thrust. Present-day Earth CDI is about 0.35, only 0.06 short of the "transitioning-successful" 0.41 and 0.09 short of "post-industrial" 0.44. That 0.06–0.09 gap is not closed by more EU, more IP, or more V3 satellites. It lies in NMat: whether we can build, in step with the rockets that carry us to orbit, a normative framework that represents future generations, monitors cumulative risk, and can roll back before thresholds are crossed.

The ignition on September 28 lifted 26 satellites and an upper stage. What was pushed to the cliff edge was humanity's own capacity to govern outer space. Starship did nothing wrong; reusable rockets did nothing wrong; space commercialization did nothing wrong. What is wrong is that we—in an era when engineering lives in 2026—are still pretending that 1967 rules are adequate.

Starship's first orbital flight is not the endpoint. It is a mirror. What it reflects is not "how great humanity is," but "how far humanity still is from deserving this greatness."

A final, possibly unwelcome note. There is a durable illusion in space narrative called "once we're up there we're free"—as if a big enough rocket and a Martian settlement can leave Earth's problems (climate, nuclear proliferation, governance failure) behind. The New Cosmological View does not permit that escape. NC-1 says we are bound to the relational web; NC-2 says being is a process, not a relocation; NL-2 says negentropy persistence is a cross-space continuous flow. Move to Mars and you carry Earth's normative defects with you; leave debris in LEO and your Martian colony inherits the question, "How did the first generation let the orbit look like this?" Multi-planet is not a back door out of planetary governance; it is an amplifier of it. A civilization that cannot manage its own orbital commons on Earth will, on Mars, merely scale up the same governance failure to an interplanetary scale.

So the thing worth remembering about that September 28 ignition is not that it put 26 satellites in orbit. It is that it reminded us, once again: rocket thrust and normative maturity must advance together. The first we have proven over thirteen flights. The second, we are still a long way from. Freedom is the stewardship of possibility—and stewardship is never completed merely by launching.
