AI “Wrote” 16 Novel Viruses: Life Is Being Generated, and Freedom Is Being Stewarded

📅 2026-09-02 | ⏱️ Reading Time: 10 min

On August 6, 2026, researchers at Stanford University and the Arc Institute reported in Science that they had used two genome language models, Evo 1 and Evo 2, to generate from scratch and synthesize 16 viable bacteriophages that do not exist in nature yet replicate in the laboratory. It is the first time AI has completed whole-genome generative design. This is both a hope for phage therapy against antibiotic resistance and a watershed for biosecurity governance. From the New Tripartite Outlook of Interstellar Civilization Studies, the real question is not whether AI can “create life,” but how we steward a possibility space that has just been pried open.

On August 6, 2026, Science published a paper that will be cited again and again. A team from Stanford University and the Arc Institute used two genome language models, Evo 1 and Evo 2, to “write” complete viral genomes from scratch, then synthesized, cultured, and validated them in the laboratory. The tally: of roughly 700,000 AI-generated candidate genomes, 302 were selected for synthesis, 285 were successfully built, and 16 came alive in the petri dish — replicating, infecting, and lysing E. coli. It is the first time humanity has let AI generate a “whole genome” and turned it into self-replicating living things.

Three things must be said plainly. First, these phages infect only bacteria, not humans; their target is E. coli, not people. Second, the success rate was only about 5 percent — AI is far from fluent at “writing” genomes. Third, and most importantly: three variants outperformed the parent strain ΦX174 in infection efficiency, and several bypassed the bacteria’s natural resistance to it. In other words, AI can not only write; sometimes it writes better. This is no longer a toy.

Supporters see hope: antibiotic resistance is casting the shadow of a “post-antibiotic era,” and AI-designed phages may be one of the few cards left in our hand. Critics see something colder: when “designing a self-replicating genome” becomes as cheap as writing a paragraph, the threshold of creation has, for the first time, dropped to a terrifyingly low level.

Both views are right. This is precisely why we should hold expectation and vigilance together. But Interstellar Civilization Studies wants to raise a question on yet another level.

Before Shouting “Creator”

The first axiom of the New Cosmological View says: the universe is isomorphic to a relational graph; entities are nodes on that web, and properties are determined by a node’s position in the network (relational reality); being is not a static thing but a process, and a state is only a snapshot of a process (process priority); knowers never observe from outside the world — they participate, and cognition itself changes the world (participatory cognition).

Hold this mirror up to “AI creating viruses,” and the conclusion turns calm. Evo 2 was trained on nine trillion base pairs; it reads DNA as a “language.” What is called “design from scratch” is, in fact, resampling, recombining, and rewiring within a vast web woven from existing relations of life. AI invented no new physical laws and conjured no information out of nothing — it reshuffled the “reserve of possibility” accumulated by billions of years of evolution.

So claims like “humanity has created God” or “AI has become the Creator” are emotionally stirring but theoretically untenable. Relation precedes entity: a genome is not an isolated “thing” but a node embedded in relations of host, environment, resistance, and co-evolution. Process precedes state: a “living” phage is not the product of a single generation event but a process that continuously maintains itself in interaction with its environment. Participation precedes observation: AI is not a Creator standing outside life; it is already a node inside the network of life, participating in the rewriting of life.

This judgment tells us what to watch. What deserves vigilance is not “the descent of God” but something else: the ability to rewrite life has, for the first time, become as cheap, as replicable, and as hard to recall as writing an essay. The object of vigilance shifts from “some deity” to “a tool on everyone’s desk.”

The Boundary of Life Is Being Remeasured

The New View of Life defines things without hesitation: life is defined by function — whatever performs life functions is life; functional equivalence implies equivalence of life (functional definition and equivalence); life is not bound to any particular substrate (cross-substrate life). Life is also a negentropy-maintaining process: to persist, it needs a continuous flow of negentropy; when the negentropy flow is interrupted, it decays (negentropy persistence condition).

Measure the 16 phages with this ruler, and the conclusion is clear: they replicate, they infect, they maintain a negentropy flow — they are life. The significance of this confirmation far exceeds a science headline: the anchor of the concept “life” has drifted from “where it came from” to “what it can do.” Cross-substrate life and synthetic life are no longer terms of science fiction; they are facts in a petri dish.

But the New View of Life also passes along a warning: life is a dissipative structure maintained by negentropy, and manufacturing life consumes negentropy resources as well. The more we build, the larger the entropy bill. Who pays for the “entropy bill” of these 16 new forms of life? If the answer is always “later civilizations, future generations,” the debt will eventually come due, with interest.

Red-Line Check: No Line Was Crossed, but the Whole Road Runs Along the Edge

The Forbidden Red Lines (FRL) are absolutely uncrossable boundaries: regardless of consequences, regardless of circumstances, there are no exceptions. Check this event line by line:

FRL-1, Prohibition of Intentional Extinction — does not apply to phages; but if the same technical path were used to design zoonotic pathogens, it would press directly against the line. Humanity, as a subject type, must not be placed under the risk of being “instrumentally designed.”

FRL-2, Prohibition of Irreversible Large-Scale Environmental Destruction — the synthesis, containment, and closed laboratory workflow did not trigger it; but once such organisms are released into an ecosystem, the ecological consequences are nearly irreversible.

FRL-3, Prohibition of Non-Consensual Irreversible Cognitive Modification — not involved in this study. Note: this red line prohibits “non-consensual, irreversible cognitive modification,” not “eliminating cognitive diversity.” Do not put the wrong hat on the wrong head.

FRL-4, Prohibition of Extreme Intergenerational Entropy Externalization — this is the real alarm. If we do not design containment, traceability, and accountability into the technology itself today, we are packaging biosecurity risk into an “intergenerational liability” to be repaid by future generations.

FRL-5, Prohibition of Closing Rollback Mechanisms — the most relevant line. Any synthetic biological system must be shutdownable and rollbackable. Removing eukaryotic-infecting viral sequences from Evo 2’s training data was a small step toward “rollbackability,” and deserves credit; but the real touchstone is whether capability, once diffused, can still be retrieved.

Conclusion: this study itself crossed no line. But the entire technical path is racing along the outer edge of the red lines at high speed. The FRL have no exceptions — the question has never been “whether to permit,” but how, inside the lines, to build guardrails faster than the technology runs.

Three Principles: Possibility, Negentropy, Reversibility

The Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP) says: an action should preserve or expand the space of possibility, not irreversibly compress it. AI genome design has suddenly expanded the “space of designable, verifiable genomes,” and the Recursive Freedom Degree (RFD) is rising. But the space of abuse is also being opened: handing the power to “create” to an unstewarded market is precisely the most dangerous irreversible compression of the possibility space. Here the core motto is not lyricism but an operational instruction — freedom is the stewardship of possibility.

The Negentropy Responsibility Principle (NRP) says: maintain negentropy sources and do not externalize entropy costs to others or to future generations. Synthetic biology throwing negentropy costs and the risks of failure onto ecosystems and descendants is a textbook case of entropy-cost externalization. Whoever produces should pay the negentropy bill — responsibility cannot be edited like a gene and then dumped on the future.

The Reversibility Principle (REV) says: under deep uncertainty, prefer reversible actions and retain the capacity to correct errors. A genome is information, and information, once diffused, is nearly irreversible; so reversibility must be built in before “design—synthesis—release”: sequence screening, synthesizer review, containment and traceability, and — most importantly — the off switch. When the three principles conflict, the FRL take absolute priority; in ordinary situations the three are balanced; only when reconciliation fails do we weigh by priority. Today’s situation happens to require all three at once.

Freedom is the Stewardship of Possibility. In this affair, the motto is not lyricism but an operational instruction.

Indicators: the MBCL of Information Risk, and the CSIA at Cosmic Scale

Let us ground the discussion in two indicators.

The Memetic Biosafety Containment Level (MBCL) measures the risk of information transmission. The capability of a genome language model is, in essence, information — and information travels along networks with almost no attenuation. Evo 2 chose openness, which advanced science while laying the capability of “whole-genome design” before everyone. Given the breadth of transmission and the depth of impact, the risk level of this information pathway is assessed at MBCL-3 to MBCL-4 — from “strict restriction” to “maximum protection.” Openness and safety are, for the first time, negotiating at the same table.

The Cosmic-Scale Impact Assessment (CSIA) takes a comprehensive view of this technological leap: the scope of impact already reaches the globe, its depth spans generations, its severity contains latent existential risk, reversibility is low, uncertainty is extreme, and cascading risk is high. Weighing the six dimensions, the overall rating falls at L3 to L4 — high to extremely high risk. This is not alarmism: not because it built 16 phages today, but because it has, to an unprecedented degree, amplified the capacity to make nearly irreversible decisions under deep uncertainty.

Fifty Years and a Hundred Years Hence

Pull the lens back to deep time.

T2, fifty years: phage therapy matures, and the shadow of the “antibiotic apocalypse” may be rewritten; meanwhile, the capacity to synthesize pathogens keeps diffusing, and biosecurity turns from “a topic of national laboratories” into “a topic at every dinner table.” Expecting any single institution or nation to guard the gate alone is fantasy.

T3, a hundred years and beyond: the concept of life is thoroughly reconstructed. Cross-substrate life, synthetic life, the dissolution of the biological–mechanical boundary — looking back at 2026 from then, people may find that humanity did not “create” life, but for the first time learned to “steward” the possibility of life’s generation. On the scale of interstellar civilization, the genetic language may even be one of the earliest common languages shared between civilizations. Our attitude toward 16 phages right now will be written into that civilization’s draft.

Conclusion: Versioned Governance, Not One-Size-Fits-All Finality

Against a question like this, the most dangerous advice is “first enact one eternally correct law.” Under deep uncertainty there are no eternally correct rules, only frameworks that can update themselves and roll back. The four institutions offered by Interstellar Civilization Studies deserve a place on the table: a Cosmic-Scale IRB, to conduct precautionary review of actions with cosmic-scale impact; an Intergenerational Trust, to hold a seat for future generations at today’s negotiating table; a Multi-Intelligence Tribunal, to let humans and AI adjudicate disputes together; and Versioned Governance, to let rules iterate and roll back like software.

This framework is itself honest — it positions itself as a “First Generation Draft,” an open research program rather than a closed doctrine, with an overall framework robustness self-assessed at 0.58, moderate, without exaggeration. A framework that dares to admit its own imperfection is the only kind worthy of an age of deep uncertainty.

Return, finally, to the core motto: freedom is the stewardship of possibility. Today, AI has pried open an opening in possibility. Whether we can steward it well decides whether we deserve to possess it.
