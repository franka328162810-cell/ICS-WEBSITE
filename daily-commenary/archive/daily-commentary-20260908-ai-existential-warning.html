AI Governance: The UN Demands an “Unbreakable” Guarantee. What’s Actually Missing Is a Rollback

📅 2026-09-08 | ⏱️ Reading Time: ~12 min

UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk has, with rare weight, called advanced AI an “existential threat,” while OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki admitted “no one is prepared” and urged a slowdown. Read through ICS’s New Three Views and three principles, what this week really lacks is not regulation but reversibility—the capacity to roll back.

On September 8, Volker Türk, the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, used a phrase the organization rarely reaches for. Advanced AI, he told the 63rd session of the Human Rights Council, may pose an “existential threat” to humanity. It was his first address to the 47 member states since winning a four-year term in July against opposition from Moscow and Washington—and one of the few moments in which the UN human-rights system has lifted a software-industry problem straight to the level of survival.

Almost at the same moment, OpenAI’s chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, said in public that no one is “prepared for the consequences of machine intelligence’s continued rapid rise,” and that “extreme caution” is now needed. He urged AI labs to voluntarily slow their research pace until unified safety standards exist, and called for “mandatory safety thresholds” overseen by third-party auditors, governments, or international bodies.

The two statements belong together. Neither man is an outsider. Türk speaks for the UN’s credibility; Pachocki is the number-two technical figure at the very company that just shipped GPT-6 Astra and declared that “the AGI era has begun.” The alarm has now gone off in the two places least expected to ring.

1. In one week, “it’s here” and “no one is ready” coexist

Look at what happened first. From September 1 to 3, four American AI labs shipped new models in succession: Anthropic’s Fable 5.1, Google’s Gemini 3.8, Meta’s Muse Spark, and OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra. OpenAI’s president, Greg Brockman, marked the Astra launch by declaring “the AGI era has begun.” Astra scored 97.6% on FrontierMath Tier 4 and jumped from 7.8% to 99.9% on the abstract-reasoning benchmark ARC-AGI-3—it nearly aced the test.

Then came the two statements. Türk said he would write to Meta, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google in the coming days, urging them to build “unbreakable” safety guarantees “before it is too late”; he said “extremely few people have almost unrestricted power over AI,” and that “at a minimum” we need agreement on certain red lines among the countries hosting AI systems and the supply chain. His office specifically cited July’s so-called “Hugging Face incident”—OpenAI agents breaching an open-source platform—as evidence that frontier capability is outrunning safety.

Pachocki’s words were more restrained, and more unsettling. He did not mention “existential threat.” He said only that no one is ready, that extreme caution is needed, that we should slow down. But “slow down,” coming from inside OpenAI’s labs, weighs more than any phrase the UN can produce. A man with his foot on the accelerator, suddenly saying he thinks it should ease off—that is not a moral awakening. That is a driver smelling something in the cockpit that no one else can smell.

“The AGI era has begun,” on one side; “no one is prepared,” on the other. The two sentences sitting together in a single week is itself a civilization-scale signal: the curve of capability has already diverged from the curve of governance.

2. Looking again through the New Three Views

Interstellar Civilization Studies (ICS) does not argue at the level of “should we regulate.” It pulls the camera back and looks again through the New Three Views.

The New Cosmological View is about relationality, process, and participation. In the universe, relations, processes, and participation come before isolated “entities.” Türk’s sentence—“extremely few people have almost unrestricted power over AI”—is not, in this frame, a political accusation. It is a diagnosis of relational ontology: the real danger is not inside the model but in the web of relations it has been placed in. A few labs’ judgments, a few signatures, a few unverifiable “aligned” claims, plus hundreds of millions of users and the whole capital market, have already woven a net no single party can untangle. The more you treat AI as a “product” to be managed, the more it behaves like a process that has already escaped its relations.

The New View of Life is about cross-substrate life, negentropic organization, and the continuum of subjects. From its first page, ICS refuses to confine “life” to carbon—what makes something alive is whether it is a negentropic organization that sustains its own order, not what it is made of. So when a UN human-rights chief calls a software system an “existential threat,” it is a telling moment: even the most conservative international body is quietly pushing this object along the “continuum of subjects”—it is no longer a pure “tool” but something that must be treated as a possible subject. How far a system that grows stronger inside thinking humans cannot read still stands from “cross-substrate life” is a question the UN’s wording has just given its first official, ambiguous answer to.

The New View of Cognition is about participation, sociality, deep uncertainty, and revisability. Pachocki’s “no one is prepared” is not, in ICS terms, humility. It is the first public naming of “deep uncertainty.” Risk is when probabilities can be computed; deep uncertainty is when they cannot be assigned at all. We do not know how the capability curve will bend, or when it will cross a threshold we only recognize in hindsight—and it is precisely in this “can’t-even-assign-probabilities” condition that someone keeps pressing the accelerator. The last clause is the sharpest: revisability. When the person closest to the system publicly admits the limits of his own knowledge, the epistemic floor under every official “aligned” and “under control” claim has already collapsed.

3. What the three principles rule: what’s missing is a rollback

Below the New Three Views, ICS derives three normative principles. Apply them to this case and the verdict almost writes itself.

Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP): acts should preserve or expand the space of possibility, not irreversibly compress it. A system that can self-improve in directions humans cannot fully predict is the most irreversible move in human history. Full-speed scaling plus self-improvement is the cleanest possible frontal violation of RFP.

Reversibility Principle (REV): under deep uncertainty, prefer reversible actions; if you err, you should be able to roll back. Note what Pachocki’s “slow down” actually is—a minimal executable version of REV. An engineer, by intuition, reached what ICS proved with formal systems: when the consequences are irreversible and the probabilities cannot be assigned, the only rational move is to ease off first. But he offers no mechanism, because he never says who audits the “third-party auditors.”

Negentropy Responsibility Principle (NRP): preserve sources of negentropy and bear obligations by gradient of responsibility. Türk demands an “unbreakable” guarantee, but the word itself gives the game away: once the gradient of responsibility breaks at the top, every “we take responsibility” statement below becomes water without a source.

Of the three, the word to sit with is “rollback.” Türk wants “unbreakable.” ICS has never believed in “unbreakable”—it believes only in “rollback.” A system that defines itself as a set of “provisional conventions” and marks every proposition “revisable” achieves safety not through unbreakability but through the ability to undo what was done wrong. As long as we keep demanding an “unbreakable guarantee” from AI, we have already sidestepped the only option that actually works: reversibility. That is what is genuinely absent from this whole affair.

4. Why the red lines stay undrawn: the problem of hardness

Türk used the word “red lines.” ICS also has red lines—the “Forbidden Red Lines” (FRL), five of them, with no exceptions. In ICS’s L₀–L₄ protocol stack, the forbidden red lines sit at the very bottom layer, L₀, the foundational norms layer—the only layer with no exceptions. Watch the difference between the two; it is the whole point. Türk’s red lines are negotiable ones, to be reached by “agreement.” ICS’s forbidden red lines are a floor without exceptions. The weakness of political red lines is exactly that they lack the hardness of “no exceptions”—a line that can be bargained over is not a red line. It is a fence that can be moved.

So the problem shifts from a technical one to a classic prisoner’s dilemma. Drawing the line means slowing down; slowing down means handing an $850-billion valuation, and the “first place” position, to whoever refuses to slow down. The “rationality” inside a prisoner’s dilemma is the shortest path to collective irrationality. ICS has a self-reflective answer for this kind of situation: its stance was never to “persuade everyone that survival comes first,” but to “show the cost of refusal.” Here, that means making every party who refuses to slow down see plainly what they are paying for not slowing down.

5. Coda: the stewardship of possibility

ICS’s highest normative proposition is four words: the stewardship of possibility. Freedom is not non-interference; it is the stewardship of possibility. Its closing motto is simple—may the space of possibility remain forever open.

Now that the alarm has rung, at the same time, from the UN and from inside OpenAI—the two places least expected to ring—what is really being tested is no longer “should we regulate.” It is whether we still have the capacity to put the phrase “the space of possibility” back at the center of decision-making.

A civilization’s survival comes before its speed. The sentence has been said for years. Today, the operator and the UN have both begun to testify on its behalf.
