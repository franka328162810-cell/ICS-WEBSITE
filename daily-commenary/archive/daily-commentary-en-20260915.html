Climate Change: When Regulation Becomes a Revocable Promise — EPA Repeal and the Retreat of Civilizational Negentropy

📅 2026-09-15 | ⏱️ Reading Time: 约10 minutes

On September 14, EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin, flanked by Interior Secretary Doug Burgum and Energy Secretary Chris Wright, announced at the G20 Energy Ministers meeting in Houston the final repeal of the Biden-era 2024 Carbon Pollution Standards, removing the 90% carbon capture mandate for new gas plants and existing coal plants while proposing to rescind remaining Obama-Biden GHG rules. EPA claims over $300 billion in industry savings. Through ICS's New Three Views, Three Principles and FRL red lines, this commentary examines the fatal mismatch between policy reversibility and physical irreversibility, and how a reverse ratchet compresses future generations' possibility space.

The Houston Numbers

Houston, September 14. In a gap of the G20 Energy Ministers meeting, EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin stood between Interior Secretary Doug Burgum and Energy Secretary Chris Wright and made the announcement: the Biden-era 2024 Carbon Pollution Standards are finally repealed. New gas plants and existing coal plants are no longer required to capture 90% of stack emissions or shut down.

EPA's arithmetic is straightforward—over $300 billion in cost savings for the energy sector, $310 billion saved for households. Democratic lawmakers fired back: this is offloading hundreds of billions in climate costs onto taxpayers.

Skip the tired choice between "deregulation" and "environmental rollback." Through the lens of Interstellar Civilization Studies (ICS), this is more complicated than a label. EPA has simultaneously proposed to rescind the remaining Obama-Biden greenhouse gas standards for power plants, and is paving the way to revoke the greenhouse gas endangerment finding in February 2026. A new round of Supreme Court litigation under the Clean Air Act is already in motion.

This Is Not Savings. It Is a Deferred Bill.

Power generation is the second-largest source of U.S. CO₂ emissions. The old rule forced 90% capture—or shutdown—putting the compliance cost on the generation side. Repealing it wipes that cost off the industry's balance sheet.

But money does not vanish. It simply changes accounting ledgers.

The Negentropy Responsibility Principle (NRP) states it plainly: maintain negentropy sources, and do not externalize entropy costs onto others or future generations. The "$300 billion in cost savings" is, in essence, a transfer of negentropy-maintenance costs from the current energy industry to future generations and climate-affected populations.

This is not energy policy. It is intergenerational cost transfer. $300 billion is not savings. It is a deferred bill—deferred onto people not yet born.

The New Three Views: Nodes, Process, Cognitive Frames

Relationality (NC-1): A power plant is never an isolated entity. It is embedded in a web of grid, ecology, and public health. The repeal does not change "the plant itself"—it changes the node's relational position within the network. Loosen the emission constraint, and the node's externalities on the whole web are re-legitimized.

Process priority (NC-2): Climate change is a process, not a state. Once CO₂ enters the atmosphere, it stays for centuries. Reversing a policy is not "returning to origin"—it is changing the trajectory parameter of an ongoing process. Press rewind, and the thermometer does not follow.

Participatory cognition (NC-3 / NK-1): EPA's decision is itself a cognitive act. It reshapes the collective cognitive framework through which society reads climate risk. The planned February 2026 revocation of the endangerment finding is not a technical update. It is a political rewriting of the frame—from "GHGs are an endangerment" to "GHGs are not."

Negentropy persistence (NL-2): Life persistence depends on negentropy flows; power plant emissions are a direct source of entropy increase. Repealing the 90% capture mandate systematically loosens the physical constraint on that increase.

Cognitive humility (NK-4): Climate science is a domain of deep uncertainty. But deep uncertainty is not a license for inaction. The Reversibility Principle requires preferring reversible action under deep uncertainty—and the repeal does the opposite, locking in an irreversible emissions pathway.

The Three Principles: Policy Reversible, Physics Not

RFP (Recursive Freedom Principle): action should preserve or expand the possibility space. Short term, the repeal "releases industry possibilities"—no costly capture equipment, plants keep running. Long term, it compresses the climate-adaptation possibility space of future generations. RFD measures the direction of the entire possibility space. The direction is downward.

NRP (Negentropy Responsibility Principle): who the $300 billion moves from and to—the ledger is clear. The principle forbids externalizing entropy costs onto others or future generations; this step stands directly on that line.

REV (Reversibility Principle)—the sharpest cut here. The fatal mismatch: the policy itself is rollbackable. A future administration can rewrite the rules. The Supreme Court can rule again. The Clean Air Act framework survives. Democrats are already preparing a new round of Supreme Court litigation.

But the physical consequences of emissions are not rollbackable. CO₂ stays in the atmosphere for centuries. Every additional ton emitted today converts, in the second half of this century, into warming, sea-level rise, and extreme weather. Policy is reversible. Physics is not. CRV's V_rev dimension measures this mismatch directly: commitment reversibility and verifiability—the policy promise can be rolled back; the physical consequences cannot.

The structural fissure between "policy reversibility" and "physical irreversibility" is the real problem. It lets one generation book an irreversible cost onto someone else's ledger.

FRL Red Line Check: Approaching, Not Crossing

FRL-4 (Prohibition of Extreme Intergenerational Entropy Externalization)—the most direct hit. Transferring $300 billion in costs from the current industry to future generations is, in essence, intergenerational entropy cost externalization. To be precise: whether a single policy decision meets the "extreme" threshold of FRL-4 requires a magnitude judgment. But the direction is unambiguous—it systematically pushes entropy costs onto people not yet born.

FRL-2 (Prohibition of Irreversible Large-Scale Environmental Destruction): a single repeal does not directly equal an FRL-2 violation, but it systematically increases both the probability and the speed of touching FRL-2. This is "approaching the red line," not "crossing it." Once climate tipping points are crossed, environmental damage is irreversible. The repeal accelerates the approach.

The Reverse Ratchet and the Failure of Anti-Drift

The climate governance ratchet normally runs forward—standards tighten, commitments accumulate. EPA's action demonstrates the reverse ratchet.

Worse, the reverse ratchet is reinforced by multiple mechanisms: litigation delays, the Supreme Court appointment structure, the cascade effect of revoking the endangerment finding. One administration can systematically dismantle the governance frameworks of previous ones, and make reversal itself extraordinarily difficult. ICS's "anti-drift" mechanism fails here.

What institutional design prevents single-administration policy drift? The answer is not to make rules immutable—that is rigidity. The answer is to make the act of relaxing itself bear a cost: any decision loosening emission constraints must simultaneously demonstrate it is not externalizing entropy costs to future generations, and must preserve verifiable correction channels. ICS's versioned governance and intergenerational trust prototypes offer exactly this—holding a seat at the decision table on behalf of the unborn.

Deep Time: This Week, Fifty Years On

Fifty years from now, in 2076, looking back at this week of September 2026. On the same day, Iranian legislators initiated legislation to withdraw from the NPT. One system retreats on climate governance; the other retreats on nuclear non-proliferation.

This is not coincidence. It is two facets of the systematic retreat of global governance in the 2020s. Two systems, two contracts, both being pried loose by single-term political will. The physical consequences of climate emissions will concentrate in the second half of this century. The generation that made the repeal decision will not bear the primary consequences.

This is exactly what NRP and FRL-4 warn against—the temporal transfer of costs.

Stance

EPA prices its "$310 billion saved for households" in electricity bills. The unpriced line item is what people on the coasts will pay in the summer of 2076.

The repeal appears to "release freedom"—reducing present regulatory constraint. But ICS's core maxim is clear: Freedom is the Stewardship of Possibility. Genuine freedom is not unconstrained in the moment. It is the stewardship of possibility. A decision that compresses the possibility space of future generations is not freedom, however attractive its political ledger looks today. It is freedom drawn in advance.
