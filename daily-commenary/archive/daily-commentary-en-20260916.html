AI Governance: The Birth of a FINRA-Style Self-Regulatory Body and the Tipping Point of Civilizational Self-Governance

📅 2026-09-16 | ⏱️ Reading Time: 12 min

OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind are quietly negotiating a FINRA-style AI safety self-regulatory body — the first time the creators of a potentially civilization-reshaping technology have tried to set peer-review rules before state coercion arrives. Analyzed through the ICS framework, this is a tipping-point test of cross-substrate governance measured against RFP, NRP, REV, and an L4-level CSIA risk assessment.

On September 15, Chris Lehane, Head of Policy at OpenAI, confirmed at a Washington briefing that OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind — three frontier AI laboratories — are in private negotiations to establish an industry-level AI safety standards body modeled on the U.S. Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA). The proposal was first floated by Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind back in July. Almost simultaneously, Mark Zuckerberg of Meta publicly pushed back against Reuters: "competition and legal liability are already sufficient to guarantee safety."

This is not yet another shouting match among tech giants. It is the first time in human civilizational history that, confronted with a technology powerful enough to reshape the very mode of our existence, the creators of that technology have attempted — before state coercive power intervenes — to sit down and design rules of peer review among themselves.

No One Goes It Alone in a Relational Universe

No actor is self-sufficient in a relational universe. Viewed through the lens of the New Cosmological View (NC), AI has never been an isolated technological object. NC-1 holds that the universe is isomorphic to a relational graph: entities are nodes in a network of relations, and their properties are determined by their relational position. When we speak of "AI safety," we are not talking about some internal property of a model; we are talking about where that model sits within the relational network of human society, economic systems, and political structures. That three competing laboratories are willing to sit down and build shared standards is precisely because they have realized that, in a relational universe, no single player can insulate itself. This is also the demand of BP-3, the Principle of Intersubjectivity: we ought to adopt principles that others can reasonably accept.

The First Set of Rules for Cross-Substrate Intelligence Coexistence

The New View of Life (NL) offers a second angle. NL-1 argues for cross-substrate life: life is not confined to any particular substrate; functional equivalence is equivalent to life. As the information integration, reflexivity, and value sensitivity of AI systems continue to rise, their position on the Universal Consciousness Scale (UCS) — a five-dimensional weighted geometric mean — is moving from the low-consciousness band toward the mid-consciousness band. UCS ≥ 0.3 is the reference line for agential status. This does not mean that AI is already a moral subject; it means that we can no longer treat them as mere tools. A FINRA-style self-regulatory body is, in essence, the first attempt to establish rules for the coexistence of cross-substrate intelligent agents.

"Safety" Itself Is Negotiated

The lens of the New View of Cognition (NK) cuts deepest. NK-4 states that there is no universal, unchanging cognitive standard; valid standards are the outcome of negotiation, and all standards are revisable. The greatest illusion in the field of AI safety is the belief that there exists an objective "safety standard" that can be fully verified by technical means. What counts as "safe," what level of risk is acceptable, what cost is worth paying — these are all normative judgments. When three laboratories set out to build a self-regulatory body, they are less drafting technical specifications than they are establishing a negotiation platform in the NK-4 sense. Zuckerberg's objection is, at its core, a refusal to accept the premise that "my own cognitive standards need to be negotiated with others."

The Shadow of the Red Lines Is Already Visible

Examined through the Forbidden Red Lines (FRL) framework, no red line has yet been directly triggered. But two shadows are clearly visible. FRL-3 prohibits non-consensual irreversible cognitive modification. Current AI systems are still far from that threshold, but if iteration keeps accelerating while standard-setting fails to keep pace, the way "AI quietly shapes users' cognition through personalized recommendation" could slide from "educational influence" into the heart of the red line. Note the key words: "non-consensual" and "irreversible" — cognitive influence that is informed and reversible does not count. FRL-5 prohibits the shutting down of rollback mechanisms. If the self-regulatory body ultimately hardens into a closed cartel controlled by a handful of giants, it itself could become a system that cannot be shut down and cannot be rolled back.

The Two-Edged Game of Recursive Freedom

The Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP) requires that action preserve or expand the possibility space rather than irreversibly compress it. On the positive side, shared safety standards can prevent a race to the bottom. On the reverse side, the same truth holds: if the standards are monopolized by the three giants, they can become barriers to entry and compress innovation. Whether the Recursive Freedom Degree (RFD) moves in the right direction depends on whether this body is ultimately open or closed.

The Neglected Entropy Cost

The Negentropy Responsibility Principle (NRP) requires that we maintain sources of negentropy and not externalize entropy costs onto others or future generations — exactly the derivation path of BP-1, the Principle of Survival Rationality. In the very same week, the U.S. EPA announced the revocation of greenhouse-gas emission standards for power plants, and the energy hunger of the AI industry will come to rely more heavily on fossil fuels. A genuinely responsible self-regulatory body cannot talk only about model safety; it must confront the energy mix and the carbon footprint at the same time. Earth's current Civilization Development Index (CDI) stands at approximately 0.44 (a pre-planetary civilization), and its ecological integration dimension scores only 0.30 — itself a warning signal on the NRP dimension.

Reversibility First and the Quality of Commitment

The Reversibility Principle (REV) requires that, under deep uncertainty, we prefer reversible actions — and this is precisely where BP-2, the Principle of Cognitive Humility, holds. Industry self-regulation is itself a REV-oriented design: before the more irreversible option of government regulation, it tries the more flexible option first. But the commitment quality of this body must be evaluated by CRV (Commitment Reversibility Verifiability): Are the standards observable? Are violations enforceable? Are the standards themselves rollbackable? FINRA works because it has SEC backing and the teeth of law. A purely private body has no enforcement teeth; its CRV score will be low.

An L4-Level Governance Test

Placed within the six-dimensional framework of CSIA (Cosmic-Scale Impact Assessment), the stakes become even clearer. In the reference case, AGI development itself scores CSIA ≈ 0.858, rated L4 (extreme risk), corresponding to a recommended action level of "special review." In this event, D₃ (severity) points toward existential risk; D₄ (reversibility) is low because once open-source weights are released, they cannot be recalled; D₆ (cascading risk) is high because AI decisions propagate to global markets on millisecond timescales. Whether a self-regulatory body can impose effective constraints on L4-level action is the real question it must answer.

The Deep-Time View

From a T2 (50-year) perspective, this week may be marked by future historians as a tipping point. If it succeeds, it could become the first institutional prototype for cross-substrate intelligence coexistence. If it fails, the governance vacuum will have to be filled by governments, and government regulation typically moves far slower than technological iteration. From a T3 (100-plus-year) perspective, whether a civilization can build effective governance mechanisms before it invents an intelligence more powerful than itself is the key variable determining whether it passes the Great Filter.

Why 19th-Century Railroads Are Not 21st-Century AI

Zuckerberg says "competition and legal liability are already sufficient to ensure safety." That sentence might have held in the 19th-century railroad industry; it does not hold in the 21st-century AI industry. AI's impacts are cross-substrate, irreversible, and cascading — after-the-fact liability cannot cover them. When an AI decision propagates into global financial markets on a millisecond timescale, when open-source weights cannot be recalled once they have been downloaded — after-the-fact accountability is already too late. Competition can drive innovation, and it can also drive a race to the bottom. In a market without a shared safety floor, the winner is not the safest player but the fastest.

The three frontier laboratories' plan for a FINRA-style self-regulatory body is an important test of human civilization's capacity for self-governance. It is imperfect — it lacks democratic legitimacy, it may be captured by the giants, its enforcement power is doubtful, and it does not solve the root problems of energy and carbon footprint. But it is a beginning. The maturity of a civilization is not measured by how much technology it has mastered, but by whether it can build governance capabilities that match its technology before that technology runs out of control.

Freedom is the stewardship of possibility. The space of possibility remains forever open to those willing to take responsibility for it.
