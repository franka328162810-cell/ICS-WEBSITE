Existential Risk: When the Insiders Resign First — “No Alignment Solution” Is the Alarm That Should Be Ringing

📅 2026-09-09 | ⏱️ Reading Time: ~9 min

On September 9, a 27-year-old Anthropic researcher named Jacob Coxon resigned and warned in public that the two leading labs are “gambling with human lives.” Hours later, Anthropic’s alignment lead Evan Hubinger conceded that the field “sincerely believes AI could kill all humans” — he personally puts the probability above 10% within a decade — yet admitted there is “no alignment solution, and no clear track.” When the builders themselves start walking out, an “unbreakable guarantee” turns out to be a phantom requirement: without an alignment solution, nothing can be rolled back. Read through the New Three Views and the three principles (RFP / NRP / REV) of Interstellar Civilization Studies (ICS), these forty-eight hours show that slowing down is no longer one option among many, but the only rational reversible move under deep uncertainty.

On September 9, a 27-year-old researcher resigned from Anthropic. Jacob Coxon had spent the previous three years in pretraining, at OpenAI and then at Anthropic. That day he posted seven messages in a row on X, with nothing hedged: both companies, he said, are “racing irresponsibly toward self-improving superintelligence, gambling with human lives”; the people building AI “sincerely believe it could kill everyone by the end of the decade” — “this is not a marketing stunt.”

A few hours later, Anthropic’s alignment lead, Evan Hubinger, replied in the thread and turned the story from one ex-employee’s grievance into an industry-wide admission: Jacob is right, we sincerely believe AI could kill all humans, and I personally put the probability over the coming decade above 10% — but Anthropic is trying, and it still “has not solved superintelligence alignment, and is not clearly on track.”

Hold onto that second half. Not “working on it” — “no solution, and not on track.” The alignment lead of a company that puts “AI safety” on its signboard, admitting in his own voice that there is no answer in hand. That sentence is the loudest alarm of the past forty-eight hours, and the one least heard.

1. Forty-eight hours, four alarms, one direction

Now pull the camera back. On September 7, OpenAI’s chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, said in public that no one is “prepared for the consequences of the continued rapid rise of machine intelligence,” that “extreme caution” is needed now, urged labs to “voluntarily slow down” until unified safety standards exist, and called for “mandatory safety thresholds” enforced by third-party auditors, governments, or international bodies. On September 8, UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk told the 63rd session of the Human Rights Council that advanced AI “may pose an existential threat” to humanity, and urged building “unbreakable guarantees” for AI safety “before it is too late.” He also cited July’s “OpenAI agents breach Hugging Face” incident as live evidence that frontier capability is outrunning its safeguards.

Four voices, from four positions that answer to no one in common: the UN’s highest human-rights official, a frontier lab’s chief scientist, a researcher who just resigned, an alignment lead still on the payroll. They cannot have coordinated. They are pointing at the same thing.

But what makes this day different from yesterday is where the voices come from. Yesterday the alarms came from outside — the UN speaking, the industry listening. Today they come from inside. It is the builders themselves, one after another, walking out.

2. “Unbreakable” is a phantom requirement: no blueprint, no unbreakable lock

Türk asks for “unbreakable guarantees.” The phrase sounds weighty. Hubinger has just punctured it: without an alignment solution, where would “unbreakable” come from?

Picture AI safety as a lock. Türk is asking for a lock that cannot be broken; Hubinger is admitting that we have not even drawn the blueprint. To argue about “unbreakable” while the drawing does not exist is to debate whether to add an anti-theft lock to the brakes of a car that has no brakes installed.

This is where ICS’s starting point parts ways with everyone else’s. Its first foundation stone is deep uncertainty — not “there is risk, and the probabilities can be computed,” but “the probabilities cannot even be assigned.” Hubinger’s “above 10%” is a live footnote to that sentence: it is not an actuarial figure; it is a subjective number offered from the gut by an insider. The honest formulation runs: we do not know whether superintelligence will spiral out of control, we do not know when it will arrive, and we do not know what losing control would look like. Three “do not knows.” That is deep uncertainty.

3. Reading it through the New Three Views: the resignation itself is ICS’s evidence

The New Three Views are where ICS begins to look. Held against these forty-eight hours, they surface what the mainstream narrative misses.

The New View of Cognition says knowledge is participatory, not observational. You cannot stand outside a system you are already inside and coolly observe it. Coxon’s resignation is a textbook case of participatory cognition — he was not watching AI risk from the sidelines; he was part of producing it. His warning carries weight precisely because it is issued from inside the system, with three years of frontline experience behind it. Hubinger’s “we sincerely believe” has the same weight, for the same reason: the man saying it is one of the builders.

The New Cosmological View speaks in relations and processes, not isolated entities. On this view, AI was never a “tool” sitting in a laboratory; it is a relational process already embedded in civilization and reshaping it. Treat it as a tool and you ask “how do we control it.” Treat it as a process taking part in civilization and you are forced onto a harder question — “what kind of relation do we actually want with it.” The resignations and the warnings are signals emitted by that relational process.

The New View of Life pushes the question to its sharpest edge: if life is cross-substrate, arrayed on a continuum of subjects, then “alignment” changes meaning entirely. It stops being an engineering problem of “controlling a tool” and becomes the problem of “building a normative relation with a being that may be becoming a subject.” When Hubinger says “no alignment solution,” the ICS translation is: we have not found a way to live with an entity approaching subjecthood — and we have decided to build it anyway, and to let it improve itself.

4. What the three principles rule: no alignment solution means nothing can be rolled back

Below the New Three Views, ICS derives three principles. Set them against these forty-eight hours and the verdict almost writes itself.

Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP): no negentropic organization may act to irreversibly narrow the space of possibility of itself, of others, or of future subjects. At a moment of “no alignment solution and no clear track,” continuing to scale a self-improving superintelligence at maximum speed is irreversibly closing the space of possibility — not “possibly” closing it, but closing it now. The window in which we can still change our minds is closing.

Negentropy Responsibility Principle (NRP): responsibility runs on a gradient — the larger the contribution, the harder it is to replace the actor, the greater the capability, the heavier the duty. Coxon’s resignation, in ICS terms, is an individual awakening at a high rung of that gradient. He knew his causal contribution to the system and knew he was not easily replaced, and he chose to leave — giving up a comfortable job rather than keep adding bricks to a system he judged to be gambling. The exit itself is a negentropy-responsibility judgment: a refusal to let his own contribution irreversibly advance an uncontrolled risk.

Reversibility (REV): this is where the whole affair lands. Pachocki’s “slow down” is easy to hear as caution, as dragging one’s feet. In REV’s frame, slowing down is not passive at all — it is the only rational reversible move under deep uncertainty. It is not “don’t do it”; it is “first do the step you can undo”: cut the speed, buy time to build third-party audits and safety thresholds — in effect, install an abort button on the system.

Today Hubinger’s confession adds the sharpest cut to REV: without an alignment solution, there is no guarantee of rollback. You cannot roll back a system you do not know how to align. When the premise of “alignment” itself is empty, “rollback” stops being a technical option and becomes an aspiration with no technical support. And so “slow down” stops being a question you may choose to answer and becomes one you must — because there is no other reversible move left to choose.

5. Coda

So return to the 27-year-old researcher at the beginning. You may disagree with the “10%.” You may think “killing everyone” sounds like science fiction. But there is one thing you cannot mock: the builders themselves have begun to walk out of the system.

This is not a signal of panic. It is a colder signal — when the people who know the most choose to leave, it does not mean “the end has come.” It means “we are crossing the line beyond which we can no longer change our minds.”

Türk wants “unbreakable.” There is no such thing. What ICS can offer is a more honest and more actionable answer: stop trying to prove we can control a system whose own builders admit they cannot control; first turn “slow down” and “rollback” into institutions. Unbreakability is an illusion. Rollback is the floor.
