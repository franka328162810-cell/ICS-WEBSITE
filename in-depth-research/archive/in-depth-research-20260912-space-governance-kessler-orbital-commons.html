Space Governance: Kessler Syndrome and the Collapse of the Orbital Commons — Why the Paris Summit Cannot Save Orbit

The Paris International Space Summit of September 2026 ended with 44 nations signing a Declaration on the Sustainable Use of Outer Space — while SpaceX, which controls roughly 67% of active satellites, declined to attend. This essay deploys the analytical tools of Interstellar Civilization Studies (ICS) to argue, through its new views of the cosmos, life, and cognition, that the Kessler syndrome is unstoppable because orbital governance's institutional layer (L₃) rests on a physical world (L₁) that no longer exists. Anchored in the Recursive Freedom Principle, the Reversibility Principle, and the Negentropy Responsibility Principle, it proposes four institutional remedies — layering, versioning, responsibility grading, and reversibility-by-design — and closes with a falsifiable prediction.

I. An Empty Chair in the Room

On September 9 and 10, 2026, Paris hosted a meeting without precedent: the International Space Summit. Emmanuel Macron presided; the UN Secretary-General attended; representatives of 44 countries signed a Declaration for the Sustainable Use of Outer Space. For the first time, humanity elevated the question of "how to govern space" to the level of heads of state.

But what deserves to be remembered is not who signed, but who did not come. SpaceX — which controls roughly 67% of all active satellites — declined to attend. In six years the company has placed more than ten thousand objects into low Earth orbit, and has launched more cumulative spacecraft than every other entity in the history of spaceflight combined. It is the single largest stakeholder in orbital governance, and the single largest absentee.

This is not a small diplomatic oversight. It is a structural fissure: the fastest runner is not in the room where the rules are written. When one actor can unilaterally alter the orbital environment, and every rule constraining him rests on the assumption that he will cooperate, the rules have already failed. That is what this essay argues — Kessler Syndrome is not being stopped because humanity lacks rules, but because rules grow slower than orbit fills up; and the deeper cause is that the foundation of those rules was laid on a world that no longer exists.

II. The Orbit Is Filling Up: Numbers That Must Be Remembered

Pin down the facts before any judgment. When the 1967 Outer Space Treaty was signed, humanity had placed roughly 130 objects in orbit, and the players were essentially two superpowers. Today, more than 30,000 trackable debris objects circle the Earth at about 7.8 kilometers per second — at that speed, a fragment the size of a bolt carries the energy of a hand grenade. SpaceX's Starlink alone has added more than ten thousand objects to low Earth orbit in six years.

And this is only the beginning. Olivier Hainaut, an astronomer at the European Southern Observatory, offered a more sobering figure: projects now underway plan to place about two million satellites in orbit, while only about fifteen thousand are currently active. From fifteen thousand to two million is a hundredfold leap. Every satellite streaks through a telescope's field of view; one streak is a minor nuisance, a million streaks is a catastrophe for astronomy.

That is only the "visible" cost. The real danger is invisible: debris. NASA scientist Donald Kessler described the scenario as early as 1978 — once the density of orbital objects crosses a threshold, one collision produces debris that causes more collisions, a cascade that could render certain orbital bands unusable for generations. In 2009 Kessler himself wrote that models already showed the debris environment in a self-reinforcing cycle. Not "possibly" — "already."

III. The Gravity of the Space Economy: Why Orbit Fills So Fast

What fills orbit is not malice but temptation. The space economy is in a rare boom: forecasts see it growing from roughly $630 billion to about $1.8 trillion by 2035 — a near threefold expansion whose physical carrier is the increasingly crowded few hundred kilometers of low Earth orbit.

SpaceX is the heaviest star in this gravitational field. Its CFO's remarks at a Goldman Sachs conference on September 10 read almost as a manifesto of orbital commercialization: Starship's fourteenth flight will carry production V3 Starlink satellites and become its first revenue-generating mission; orbital computing could reach cost parity with ground data centers as early as 2027; the company just signed a hosting agreement worth roughly $1.1 billion per month starting December 1, putting it on track toward a $100 billion annualized run-rate by year-end. Farther out on the drawing board: a 2027 launch of "Starmind" — space-based AI data-center satellites. Compute, the scarcest resource of our age, is being lifted into orbit.

In the language of the New View of Life (NL-2, Vol. I Introduction), all of this has a "negentropic" logic: civilization, as a negentropic organization, instinctively seeks new energy sources and new ordered structures to sustain its expansion. Ground data centers are choked by power, cooling, and land; orbit offers nearly limitless solar energy and heat dissipation. Lifting compute into space is the natural extension of a negentropic organization's expansion drive. The problem is that the logic of expansion collides with the limit of the physical space that carries it — orbit is not infinite. It is a finite, fragile corridor, and once polluted it is extraordinarily hard to restore.

IV. Kessler Syndrome: When Removal Cannot Catch Up With Creation

To understand Kessler Syndrome, grasp one counterintuitive fact: orbital debris does not go away by itself. On the ground, garbage decomposes and gets recycled; in low Earth orbit a few hundred kilometers up, debris must slow under atmospheric drag and fall, a process that can take years, decades, even centuries. The debris we create today is a problem we are manufacturing for our grandchildren a century from now.

Worse still is the nonlinearity. A dead satellite does not become one piece of debris — as it breaks up or collides it produces thousands; each fragment can cause new collisions. It is a self-accelerating positive feedback: more debris, higher collision probability; more collisions, more debris. The International Space Station performs over a dozen avoidance maneuvers a year to dodge fragments smaller than a centimeter — fragments already so numerous they must be "predicted" statistically.

The crux is not whether humanity can remove debris, but that removal can never catch up with creation. The number of satellites launched each year has already surpassed any order of magnitude that debris-removal technology could match. We are pouring water into a leaking bucket while debating whether to develop a faster spoon. More brutally: no nation or company is willing to pay for active debris removal, because it produces no revenue, only bills. In an industry driven solely by commercial return, the thing with no revenue is, by definition, last in line.

V. The New View of the Cosmos: Orbit Is Relational Space, Not Empty Space

Here the "New View of the Cosmos" of Interstellar Civilization Studies (ICS) offers an explanatory frame no one else can. Its first proposition (NC-1, Vol. I Ch. 2) is that the fundamental fact of the universe is not isolated entities but the relations between them. Relation precedes entity. The same volume adds process (NC-2) — the universe is not a collection of static objects but an ongoing process of becoming.

Through this lens, orbit yields a nearly subversive conclusion: orbit has never been "empty space." It is a space constituted by relations — a satellite's entire value lies not in itself but in its relations to ground stations, to other satellites, to future satellites. Navigation, timing, communication, Earth observation: every one is a relational function. The UN Secretary-General offered a figure at the summit: nearly 40% of the Sustainable Development Goals depend on data from space.

Then the deeper meaning of Kessler Syndrome becomes clear: what it destroys is not a few satellites but the very possibility of relation. When an orbital band fills with debris, humanity loses not some specific device but the possibility of "establishing new relations in that space." This is exactly what the New View of the Cosmos warns us to watch — in a relational universe, the most expensive loss is not entities but connections. And debris is precisely "a connection already dead": every failed satellite was once a node in the network, now become a wall blocking new links.

VI. The New View of Life: Orbit Is Civilization's Negentropic Infrastructure

The New View of Life (NL-2, Vol. I Introduction) defines life as a "negentropic organization" — an entity that consumes energy to maintain ordered structure against entropy. Human civilization is a negentropic organization at colossal scale. And that organization now stands on an invisible foundation: orbit.

Timing runs on orbital atomic clocks; navigation runs on orbital constellations; weather forecasting runs on orbital observation; millisecond synchronization of financial trades runs on satellite timing; even remote surgery, grid dispatch, and cross-border logistics quietly depend on the hundreds of thousands of artificial objects overhead. Orbit is civilization's negentropic infrastructure — not decoration, but the physical precondition of its ordered functioning. The moment it begins to disorder, civilization's "order" starts peeling away at the edges.

From this angle the threat level of Kessler Syndrome must be re-evaluated. It is not "a niche concern for space enthusiasts" but "irreversible degradation of the infrastructure a negentropic organization depends on." The New View of Life liberates life from the carbon-based cage, and along the way it enlarges the list of "what is worth protecting" — protecting orbit is not protecting some satellite, it is protecting the capacity of civilization, as a negentropic organization, to sustain itself. This is the key step by which ICS moves from "protecting individuals" to "protecting systems," and what distinguishes it from traditional environmental ethics.

VII. The New View of Cognition: We Don't Even Know How Full Is Too Full

The New View of Cognition (NK-3, Vol. I Ch. 3) names a special epistemic condition "deep uncertainty": uncertainty to which not even a probability can be assigned. It is not risk ("70% chance of something bad") but ignorance ("we do not know the probability of the bad thing at all"). This is harder than risk, because risk can be computed with expected value, and under deep uncertainty the tool of expected value simply fails.

The Kessler threshold is a perfect specimen of this condition. Scientists know a critical density exists above which the cascade ignites; no one can say precisely what that value is, or what percentage of the way we have already traveled. Debris orbital distributions, collision cross-sections, material strengths, breakup models — every variable carries a wide error bar, and their product is a number whose order of magnitude cannot be stated. We are driving in the dark, unsure how far the cliff is from the front wheel, certain only that our foot is still on the accelerator.

The New View of Cognition reminds us of a second thing (NK-1, participatory cognition): cognition is not spectatorship but participation. SpaceX's refusal to attend is, from a cognitive standpoint, not "missing information" but "missing participation" — the actor who best knows the orbital environment chose not to enter the shared cognitive scene. A shared cognition without its largest player is, in essence, a crippled cognition. And rules built on crippled cognition are doomed to be castles in the air. More subtly: participatory cognition means "knowing" is itself an act — when the one who should know most chooses not to know, that "not knowing" is already a consequential choice.

VIII. RFP's Textbook Counterexample: One Collision, Locked for a Millennium

Now the first normative principle of ICS — the Recursive Freedom Principle (RFP, Vol. I Ch. 7): no negentropic organization may irreversibly reduce the possibility space of itself, of others, or of future subjects. Its three criteria for irreversibility: time scale (recovery exceeding about 1,000 years), energy cost (reversal cost beyond what can be borne), and information loss (once lost, unreconstructable).

A Kessler cascade hits all three. Debris lingers in low orbit for centuries; the energy cost of removing it, with today's technology, is astronomical — every kilogram costs thousands of dollars to launch, and retrieving it costs an order of magnitude more; as for information loss, once an orbital band is sealed off by a cascade, every space activity that might have happened there — new science, new industry, new civilizational possibility — is permanently zeroed. This is not metaphor; it is RFP's three criteria for irreversibility struck one by one.

Thus, from ICS's standpoint, Kessler Syndrome is not "a risk to be managed" but "a scenario that, once triggered, violates the red line." It converts "present convenience" into "permanent closure of future generations' possibility space." And the cruelest irony is that this conversion is brought about not by a malevolent actor's scheme, but by thousands of everyday decisions, each reasonable and harmless on its own — every launch, considered in isolation, is rational. What RFP aims to block is precisely this "evil accumulated out of good intentions."

IX. Six Indicators: A Ruler for Orbital Health

ICS does not stop at principles; it insists principles must be operationalizable. The six indicators — UCS, RFD, CRV, CDI, MBCL, CSIA — are its tools for building scales for abstract concepts (Vol. III). Transfer them to orbital governance and they immediately expose the blind spots of existing governance.

The sharpest ruler is Recursive Freedom Degree (RFD), which measures whether the possibility space is being irreversibly compressed. Applied to orbit, RFD asks not "how much debris is there now" but "what can still be done in this orbit in the future" — the watershed between stock thinking and flow thinking. A second key is the Commitment-Responsibility Vector (CRV), which at the operational layer corresponds to "reversible verifiability": when an operator promises "we will deorbit at end of life," is that promise reversible, verifiable, and enforceable? Today most deorbit commitments are voluntary, unverifiable, and unenforceable — CRV scores near zero. Third, the lesson of the Civilization Development Index (CDI) is equally sharp: it warns precisely against "surface prosperity masking structural loss." Starlink's expansion drives connectivity indices ever upward, yet if that rise is purchased with irreversible degradation of the orbital environment, the rising CDI is a dangerous illusion.

The value of indicators is not "producing precise scores" but "forcing the right questions." When your governance framework has no dimension for "is the possibility space being locked," the debris problem is guaranteed to be underestimated — because every existing indicator measures "what we have now," and none measures "what we will still be able to have."

The remaining three rulers each find their own purchase. The Universal Civilization Scale (UCS) reminds us that the intensity of orbital use has, for the first time, pushed human civilization toward the boundary of "planetary carrying capacity" on this single dimension — no previous human activity could make an irreversible change in one physical layer count its consequences in centuries. Meme Risk Classification (MBCL) illuminates the narrative layer: high-propagation, high-emotion, low-reversibility narratives like "space race" and "staking claim to orbit" are pushing public cognition toward a "first-come, first-own" jungle logic, and that logic is precisely the psychological amplifier of the debris crisis. The Civilization Stability Index (CSIA) is the coldest of all: it asks not "can orbit still be used" but "how fragile is civilization when orbit suddenly cannot be used" — global navigation, timing, emergency communication, and Earth observation are all staked on this corridor, and CSIA's answer will not be reassuring. Taken together, the six rulers reach one conclusion: what orbital governance lacks is not data, but a few questions no one dares to look at squarely.

X. The Gradient of Responsibility and Absence: Who Is Responsible for Orbit?

The Negentropy Responsibility Principle (NRP, Vol. I Ch. 8) answers "who bears how much responsibility" through the responsibility function Φ(c, p, k) = c · (1 − p) · k: responsibility strength is jointly determined by causal contribution (c), substitutability (p — the less substitutable, the heavier the responsibility), and capacity (k).

Apply this function to orbit and the answer leaves no suspense. SpaceX's causal contribution c is the highest in the field — it alone holds roughly 67% of active satellites; its substitutability p is the lowest — no entity can replicate its launch capacity and constellation scale in the short term; its capacity k is also maximal. All three factors point in the same direction: the primary bearer of orbital-governance responsibility is the largest constellation operator.

And so the nature of "absence" changes. It is no longer a commercial company's attitude toward a diplomatic conference, but an NRP-identified responsibility bearer choosing not to enter the shared scene of responsibility allocation. ICS repeats one line throughout: transferring responsibility is not discharging it. Shifting responsibility onto the vague collective of "the international community" is the classic path to responsibility dilution — and responsibility dilution is the most lethal chronic disease of negentropic organizations in governance. NRP's value is precisely that it turns "who is responsible" from a moral verdict into an arithmetic problem, using c, p, and k.

XI. The Failure of the 1967 Treaty: An L₃ Institution Built on an Old L₁ World

ICS's protocol-stack layering (Vol. III Ch. 10) divides governance into five layers: L₀ physical-informational base, L₁ subject identification, L₂ normative principles, L₃ institutional design, L₄ operating procedures. The most consequential of its three disciplines is "downward dependence": upper layers depend on lower layers, but lower layers do not depend on upper ones — when the bottom collapses, it drags the top down with it.

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty is a work of the L₃ institutional layer. But the L₁ physical world it was built on is not the same object as today: at signing there were 130 objects and two players; today there are hundreds of thousands of objects, a hundred players, and commercial giants capable of unilaterally altering the orbital environment. However elegant the treaty, the physical reality it constrains has changed. When L₁ undergoes a qualitative shift while L₃ stays motionless, the result is not "a somewhat outdated institution" but "an institution hollowed out by physical fact."

The debris rules are especially absurd. The only "existing" debris rules — the IADC guidelines and the UN's 2008 debris-mitigation guidelines — are entirely voluntary. "Voluntary" means no satellite operator has a legal obligation to be responsible for not creating debris. An L₃ built on "everyone will do the right thing" is doomed to be a dead letter in a competitive environment where "whoever does right first loses." This is not wickedness; it is structure. The wisdom of the protocol stack is precisely to point out that an L₃ institution cannot be built on "moral self-restraint" — the most unstable of foundations — but must be re-aligned with the physical facts of L₁.

XII. Reversibility Systematically Sacrificed: Why No One Wants an Exit

The Reversibility Decision principle (REV, Vol. I Ch. 9) is ICS's trump card under deep uncertainty: when probabilities cannot be computed, the only rational move is to keep options open, prefer reversible actions, and build rollback mechanisms. Its decision rule: between two actions, prefer the one with higher reversibility and lower rollback cost.

But the reality of the orbital business runs directly counter to REV. Once a satellite is launched into orbit, "withdrawing" it is nearly impossible — a dead satellite does not come home; it becomes debris. REV would say: then design passive deorbit capability, choose shorter-lived orbits, reserve tow-deorbit interfaces. Commercial incentives point the other way: deploy faster, launch more, occupy denser. The SpaceX CFO's September 10 remarks — "orbital compute at parity by 2027," "$100 billion ARR by year-end," "Starmind space data centers" — are the apotheosis of "seize the orbit," with not one sentence reserving room for reversibility.

Reversibility is not free. Designing deorbit hardware costs money, mass, cost, performance. In a race where everyone sprints, the first to voluntarily "leave an exit for themselves" loses on the short-term ledger. This is the collapse of collective rationality under deep uncertainty: each individual makes the choice optimal for himself, and the collective result is everyone sprinting together toward a cliff no one can turn back from. REV's insight here is that when individual rationality conflicts with collective reversibility, there must be a mechanism above individual short-term interest to buy the "exit" on behalf of the collective.

XIII. The Paradox of the Red Line: Individually Reversible, Collectively Irreversible

The lexicographic priority (Vol. I Ch. 9 §9.8.3) contains a red line no situation may cross — Rule 9.2: actions that cause irreversible collapse of the negentropic base, or irreversibly deprive all future subjects of possibility space, are absolutely prohibited, excluded from trade-offs.

A Kessler cascade plainly falls under this red line. The problem is that no single launch will ever be stopped by it — because every launch, taken alone, is reversible, harmless, even beneficial. The red line can stop "the one who maliciously triggers the cascade" but cannot stop "thousands of individually harmless everyday decisions." This is what ICS's combined-scenario analysis warns about: risks each "reversible and acceptable" on their own, when superimposed, can give rise to irreversible consequences that none of them individually possessed.

This is the deepest dilemma of space governance, and what makes it harder than nuclear proliferation. Proliferation is "a visible villain building a visible bomb" — clear red line, clear responsibility bearer. Orbital debris is "a million good people doing a million good things, and the good things piling into disaster." For the former we have the NPT and the IAEA; for the latter we have only a sheet of voluntary guidelines. The type of disaster has changed, but the toolbox of governance has not upgraded with it. For a red line to catch "emergent irreversibility," it cannot watch only individual actions — it must monitor the possibility space at the system level, which is exactly why indicators like RFD exist.

XIV. Fragmented Governance in a Multipolar Order

The Paris summit carried another signal worth noting: governance itself is fragmenting — not physically, but politically. Europe poured $22 billion into "space sovereignty," and German startup Isar Aerospace launched Europe's first commercially developed orbital rocket from Norwegian soil on September 5; the EU's Space Act seeks to unify spectrum and safety standards. Meanwhile the US-led Artemis Accords and the China-Russia International Lunar Research Station are dragging space governance toward two rival camps.

The irony here: an orbit being filled with physical debris needs "unified, binding governance," while reality offers "fragmented, voluntary governance." Europe's "sovereignty" narrative is, at bottom, "we too must have a seat at the table" — a legitimate strategic anxiety, but it solves "who occupies orbit," not "whether orbit remains usable." When governance attention is absorbed by camp competition, the truly public problem — debris, cascade, generational equity — finds no one willing to act on it in earnest.

China is the heaviest piece of this multipolar order and the one most easily omitted by Western narratives. It fields an independent space station, the BeiDou navigation system, a fast-growing commercial constellation, and the Chang'e lunar program — on the list of "who can alter the orbital environment," China stands alongside SpaceX in the first rank. But ICS's position must be honest: the authoritative data cited in this essay comes almost entirely from Western institutions and media, which itself constitutes a cognitive asymmetry. A complete judgment about the "orbital commons" cannot hear only one side; a genuine commons problem demands that we place all major players' conduct into the same framework of responsibility. This is also the methodological reminder of participatory cognition (NK-1) — cognitive incompleteness often comes not from lying, but from absence.

XV. Deep Time and Generations: Keeping Orbit for Those Not Yet Born

The lifetime of debris drags this question into "deep time." ICS treats deep time (the T2 and T3 scales) as its default coordinate system — when consequences span longer than a single human lifetime, ordinary time-sense fails, and you must decide on behalf of "subjects who do not yet exist."

A fragment drifting in low orbit for centuries means the person making today's launch decision will never see the full consequences of his own decision; and the person who bears those consequences does not exist yet. This is exactly why RFP includes "possible future negentropic organizations" within its scope of protection, and why ICS designs institutions like the Intergenerational Trust (IGT) and the Deep-Time Trust (DTT) — because no existing, living stakeholder will spontaneously pay for the orbital future a few centuries out.

The Paris summit exposed its ceiling precisely here. A declaration can call for "sustainable use" and support "information sharing," but no mechanism exists to translate the concern "will orbit still be usable a century from now" into an institution with teeth today. Intergenerational justice in the orbital question is not rhetoric; it is a technical parameter measured in "debris dwell time" — and our governance remains stuck at the stage of "issuing shared visions." ICS's deep-time trust idea is to give an institution independent of any contemporary government standing to speak, in orbital decisions, for "those not yet born." It sounds ahead of its time; but against the physical fact that debris persists for centuries, it is the only proposal that matches the time scale.

XVI. A Falsifiable Prediction: ICS's Self-Discipline

If this essay ended as merely another "space is dangerous, everyone should care" exhortation, it would be no different from the summit's declaration. ICS has a self-discipline that separates it from all empty talk: everything it claims must be able to say "what evidence would overturn it." This is the "provisional convention" and "refutability" written plainly into the anchor document — a discipline that does not voluntarily declare its own falsification points degenerates into a stance.

So what tests can this essay's argument accept? First, if active debris removal becomes dramatically cheaper over the next decade, such that "removal catches up with creation" shifts from impossible to possible, then the core premise "Kessler Syndrome is irreversible" is weakened — and I would lower the urgency of my conclusion accordingly. Second, if evidence emerges that actual debris density in low orbit is orders of magnitude below the cascade threshold and the growth curve is flattening, then the judgment "the threshold is near" is wrong. Third, if SpaceX and other largest operators voluntarily sign binding deorbit and debris-mitigation obligations, and write those obligations into their launch contracts, then the diagnosis "the responsibility bearer is absent" is partly falsified by reality.

Conversely, what would confirm this essay? If within the next few years a major collision caused by debris cascade occurs, or orbital insurance costs jump by orders of magnitude due to debris risk, then reality has endorsed the judgment. More importantly, this "prediction-falsifiability" framework is itself the method ICS wants to demonstrate: turning serious judgments about the future from "emotion" into "propositions testable by data."

This is the meaning of epistemic humility — not "I am uncertain, so I stay silent," but "precisely because of deep uncertainty, I must state clearly the conditions under which I will change my position." Facing a situation like the Kessler threshold, where probabilities cannot even be computed, humility is not an excuse to avoid judgment but the precondition for making judgment correctable. The first thing orbital governance needs is perhaps not more satellites or more declarations, but more people willing to say "under what circumstances I will admit I was wrong."

Conclusion: From Tragedy of the Commons to Negentropic Governance

Orbit is a commons — that is common sense. But ICS injects into this common sense a normative core no one else offers: orbit must be governed not because of the romantic phrase "it belongs to all humanity," but because it is civilization's negentropic infrastructure, and its irreversible destruction crosses the red line drawn by the Recursive Freedom Principle. This is a complete chain — from physical fact, through the bridge principle (BP-1, Vol. I Ch. 5), to normative obligation.

What, then, is ICS's prescription? Not the empty phrase "strengthen international cooperation," but four concrete institutional moves. First, layer — re-layer orbital governance along the protocol stack, pressing the clause "no irreversible debris accumulation" to the bottom as a non-negotiable red line, and leaving operational details like spectrum coordination and collision avoidance at the top for flexible iteration. Second, version — treat the Outer Space Treaty as v1.0, and use the five elements of versioned governance (version identity, change history, revision procedure, traceable basis, controlled rollback) to advance it to v2.0, making revision a controlled process rather than "finalized once, unchanged for sixty years." Third, grade responsibility — use NRP's responsibility function to press the heaviest regulatory weight precisely onto the actors with the greatest causal contribution and the least substitutability, rather than diluting "everyone is responsible" into "no one is responsible." Fourth, design for reversibility — turn "passive deorbit capability" from a voluntary option into a licensing condition, so every launch carries its own exit, while using indicators like RFD to continuously monitor whether the possibility space is being irreversibly compressed.

Every one of these moves is hard. But the hard part has never been technology; it is admitting an uncomfortable fact: in a relational universe, no one can run into the stars alone without leaving consequences behind. The real lesson of the Paris summit's failure to bring SpaceX into the room is not "invite him next time," but "when the largest player is allowed to stay away, the rules themselves must be rewritten." Orbit will not wait for us to think it through. At 7.8 kilometers per second, it is making the decision for us. All we can do, before that threshold arrives, is answer the three questions one by one — for whom are we responsible, for whom do we leave an exit, and whose possibilities are we protecting.
